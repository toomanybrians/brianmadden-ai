# ⚙️ The music industry's AI reckoning is already here

## Insights

- AI use in music spans a wide spectrum: assistive tasks (stem separation, cleanup, mastering), ideation/demo-building, and fully generative song creation from prompts — practitioners and experts draw ethical lines at different points along this spectrum.
- Multiple sources argue AI output quality depends on human "editorial control" and a pre-existing creative vision; without that, musicians report the tools can slow them down by generating overwhelming numbers of options rather than speeding up work.
- AI threatens "middle-class" music jobs specifically — session players, sync licensing staff, jingle writers, entry-level roles — because AI-generated tracks are cheaper/faster substitutes, raising calls for new pathways for emerging artists to enter the industry.
- Major labels are simultaneously suing AI companies (Suno, Udio) over alleged copyright infringement in training data while signing licensing partnerships with those same or similar companies (Warner/BMG/Believe with Suno, Universal with ElevenLabs), reflecting conflicted "embrace and fight" positioning.
- Streaming platforms are grappling with AI-generated spam/fraud diluting royalty pools (Spotify deleted 75M spam tracks; Deezer reports ~44% of daily uploads are AI-generated), prompting labeling and royalty-blocking policies (Tidal, YouTube, Deezer, Apple Music) that vary widely in enforcement.
- Experts suggest a plausible future direction is small, niche AI models trained to capture individual artists' styles, paralleling a similar "small model" trend discussed in enterprise AI contexts.
