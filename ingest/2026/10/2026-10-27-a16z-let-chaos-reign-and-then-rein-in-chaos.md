# Let Chaos Reign, and Then Rein In Chaos

## Insights

- Andreessen frames "chaos" as a leading indicator of disruptive technology adoption: when a tool enters workplaces messily and bottom-up (unsanctioned, ungoverned), that's a signal it's genuinely valuable, not a problem to suppress outright.
- Historical pattern traced through PCs (employees smuggling them in via falsified procurement forms), spreadsheets (ungoverned, undocumented "rogue" models prone to error, akin to "hallucinations"), and email (which broke the mailroom's control over information flow and triggered proposals like requiring managerial approval to send messages).
- The resolution in each case wasn't suppression or free rein, but building a "habitat" — governance structures, access rules, conventions (cell-locking, version history, IT practices) — that preserved bottom-up initiative while containing risk.
- Applies this lineage to AI agents: Replit's "Agent Habitat" concept and the idea of the "self-driving company" (via Replit CEO Amjad Masad) are the current iteration — agents today live in ungoverned chat windows/personal accounts with unclear credentials and no visibility into their actions.
- Argues the people who benefit most from disruptive tools are those who already "know what to do" — citing an example of one employee at Sumitomo Mitsui Finance and Leasing completing a six-person, six-month dev project solo in one week using agents.
- Claims code is the "ultimate aligning artifact" since it embodies business logic, and that letting more people generate it (chaotically, at first) is what builds deep organizational alignment; proposes that a company's alignment will eventually be measurable by the richness of its "agent habitat."
