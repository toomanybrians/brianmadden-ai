# Skinner argues AI competes with normality, not with any particular profession. E

## Insights

- Chris Skinner's core argument: AI competes with "normality" rather than with specific professions — it excels at recognizing patterns and producing the expected, conventional output that a well-trained employee would produce.
- Industrial-age institutions (schools, universities, corporations) spent over a century optimizing humans for predictability and conformity — standardized testing, established procedures, hierarchies — which is precisely the capability AI now replicates at scale.
- Counterintuitively, the most vulnerable workers may be highly educated, highly competent, predictable knowledge workers (credit analysis, compliance, reporting, coding, documentation), not the least-educated — because their work is rule-based pattern application.
- "Weirdos" — people who question processes, make unexpected connections, and think non-linearly — become newly valuable because AI is built from existing data/patterns and struggles when the underlying map/assumptions themselves need to be challenged.
- Cites Palantir CEO Alex Karp's claim that the future splits into two groups: people doing unpredictable physical/hands-on work, and people who "don't think normally."
- Argues organizational consistency/conformity, once a corporate strength, becomes a liability in the AI era; cognitive diversity and deep expertise paired with unconventional thinking become competitive advantages, especially since AI gives individual "orchestrators" leverage once requiring whole departments.
- Reframes career advice away from technical skills (learn to code, prompt engineering) toward cultivating curiosity, contrarianism, cross-domain interests, and unpredictability — connected by Miessler to Claude Shannon's definition of information as tied to unpredictability.

## Quote

> So basically be different. And harder to predict. This is also Claude Shannon's definition of information.
