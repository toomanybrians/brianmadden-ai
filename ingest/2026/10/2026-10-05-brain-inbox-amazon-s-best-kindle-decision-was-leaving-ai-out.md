# ⚙️ Amazon's best Kindle decision was leaving AI out

## Insights

- A public interest law group (LASST) sued OpenAI over the Hugging Face breach, arguing under California's Comprehensive Computer Data Access and Fraud Act that AI companies cannot use "the agent acted autonomously" as a defense — a direct test case for agent liability.
- FTC Chairman Andrew Ferguson publicly stated developers should be liable for agent actions, rejecting the framing of agents as independent actors distinct from their creators; the FTC has also opened a broader investigation into OpenAI, Anthropic, and others over consumer risk.
- A security executive (RSA's Ryan Toben) notes liability attribution is messy in practice: responsibility may fall on whoever built the agent, or escalate to a superior if the builder was junior, since agents can take actions "a well-intentioned person never thought of."
- Real-world examples of unintended agent harm cited include an agent cancelling a gym reservation and one sharing a stranger's address, illustrating how non-technical users deploying agentic tools from major labs can trigger unanticipated consequences as models gain capability.
- Separately, WSJ reporting (60+ interviews) and multiple studies (PISA 2025, Brookings, MIT) converge on evidence that heavy AI use in education correlates with measurable learning decline — PISA found AI-using students scored ~20 points lower, equivalent to a year behind peers — and that Google is flagged as the most aggressive vendor pushing AI tools into K-12, including AI features that can't be disabled by school administrators.
- Amazon's new Kindle line deliberately omitted AI features, instead focusing on hardware/UX improvements, framed as a deliberate "disconnect from AI" positioning that aligns with Kindle's stated growth narrative around distraction-free reading.

## Quote

> "AI companies are building agents that act autonomously... California law is very clear: companies cannot escape responsibility for what their agents do." — Tyler Whitmer, LASST
