# ⚙️ Anthropic's quiet move in open hardware is winning

## Insights

- Anthropic's Model Hardware Standard (MHS), announced in late August and described internally as "the MCP for hardware," gives AI models a common interface for controlling physical equipment rather than just software/data tools.
- Demand for MHS outstripped Anthropic's planning by 30x within two weeks of announcement, with interest spanning semiconductors, advanced manufacturing, automotive, food processing, aerospace, energy, and critical infrastructure — far beyond the lab-automation use case it was originally built for.
- Early deployments show concrete autonomous-operations gains: an AI-controlled laser recovery system for quantum computers (QuEra) outperformed human experts on speed, and a CMU robotic lab setup let an agent independently judge a bad result, adjust parameters, and rerun an experiment without human intervention.
- Despite Anthropic's reputation for caution on open-weights models, it plans to eventually open-source MHS to become an industry standard like MCP, suggesting a strategic split between guarding model weights and open-sourcing interface/protocol layers.
- Sam Altman drew a sharper distinction between OpenAI and Anthropic on risk tolerance, arguing AI's benefits justify accepting some harms and rejecting Anthropic's framing of "catastrophic" loss-of-control risk — a divergence with direct implications for how aggressively enterprises might expect each lab's agents to be deployed.
- Reporting on Meta's Muse app found it runs an hourly background process building detailed relationship profiles (facts, history, relationship-improvement suggestions) on everyone in a user's life in order to give the assistant usable context — illustrating the data-depth tradeoff behind making agents feel seamless.
