# Even Within an Organization, There Is No Single AI Strategy

## Insights

- Argues against treating "AI strategy" as a single uniform capability to deploy across an organization; reliability varies sharply by task type.
- Proposes a two-axis framework for classifying enterprise tasks: (1) whether the LLM has been trained on sufficient relevant data, and (2) whether the output can be verified as correct.
- Four resulting categories each imply a different human role: delegate-and-audit (high verification/high context, e.g. coding, engineering with testable specs); supply missing tacit knowledge (high verification/low context, e.g. logistics nuances living in Slack threads or employees' heads); curate for distinctiveness (low verification/high context, e.g. marketing/strategy, where AI output risks convergence across competitors); and retain human judgment entirely (low verification/low context, e.g. novel acquisitions), where AI's role shrinks to surfacing assumptions and counterarguments rather than deciding.
- Notes a competitive-advantage angle: organizations skilled at converting tacit/experiential knowledge into usable AI context can outperform rivals using the identical model — but this requires guarding that proprietary context from leaking into externally trained models.
- Cites specific examples of capturing tacit knowledge for AI: head-mounted cameras recording service workers' actual workflows, and Mercor's business model of interviewing experts to sell their knowledge to AI labs.
- Frames the "low verification/high context" risk as subtle: not bad output, but competent, generic output that causes companies' campaigns and strategies to converge over time.
- Tasks aren't fixed in their category — better rubrics, more data, or ways to verify outcomes can migrate work from less to more automatable categories over time.

## Quote

> AI is jagged. Enterprise strategy should be built around that reality, not pretend it away.
