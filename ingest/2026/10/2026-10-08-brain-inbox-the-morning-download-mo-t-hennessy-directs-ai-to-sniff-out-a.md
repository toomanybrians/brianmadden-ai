# The Morning Download: Moët Hennessy Directs AI to Sniff Out a Stubborn Problem in Winemaking

## Insights

- Moët Hennessy, Analog Devices, and UC Davis built an AI system to detect "Fresh Mushroom Aroma," a Champagne grape defect invisible to humans until after fermentation, when it spoils the wine and costs millions in wastage.
- The breakthrough approach reframes the sensing problem: rather than identifying individual chemicals in a smell, the AI is trained to recognize whether an aroma "fingerprint" matches patterns of good vs. bad samples — a pattern-matching approach over molecular analysis.
- Early testing showed 99% accuracy, but the system still takes ~2 hours per sample, too slow for production use (small houses need ~300 samples, larger ones thousands); limited production use is targeted for next year.
- Researchers see broader potential for the same olfactory-sensing-plus-AI approach: detecting smoke taint in wine (a $4B loss event in California in 2020) and emerging plant viruses.
- Separately, Appian CEO Matt Calkins argues for a mandatory U.S. AI "alignment test" designed by top researchers (citing Hinton, Hassabis) that would deliberately slow AI development, arguing AI labs are testing alignment "gently" and releasing misaligned models anyway.
- Calkins frames unaligned advanced AI as a near-term bet with high stakes ("massively empowering us or substantially oppressing us" within a decade), and says he's maintained this stance despite investor pressure to stop discussing it.

## Quote

> "Rather than asking 'What chemicals are present?,' we ask 'Does this smell like a good sample or a bad sample?'" — Max Shulaker, Analog Devices
