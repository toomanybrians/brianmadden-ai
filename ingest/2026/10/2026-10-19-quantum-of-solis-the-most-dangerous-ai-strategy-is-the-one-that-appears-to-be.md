# The Most Dangerous AI Strategy Is the One That Appears to Be Working

## Insights

- Argues AI success metrics (tasks automated, costs cut, efficiency gains) can mask a deeper failure: organizations may be optimizing inherited processes rather than questioning whether those processes should exist at all — creating an "AI-powered status quo."
- Proposes a distinction between "iterative AI" (improving existing workflows/efficiency) and "innovative AI" (creating new capabilities, products, business models); argues companies need both simultaneously, run as parallel agendas rather than letting cost-cutting consume reinvention.
- Introduces "WWAID" (What Would AI Do?) as a pre-prompt mindset: before automating a task, ask if AI could eliminate the need for it; before improving a workflow, ask if the whole experience could be redesigned around the outcome.
- Describes a maturity progression from automation (efficiency) to augmentation (AI as "cognitive exoskeleton" expanding human judgment/creativity) to agentic AI (agents interpreting context, coordinating tasks, acting autonomously within parameters, operating in "event time rather than calendar time").
- Claims agentic AI breaks traditional organizational boundaries (departments, working hours, headcount-based scale), potentially giving smaller firms access to capabilities once requiring large headcount/capital — forcing leaders to rethink org charts and the link between company size and market power.
- Frames the core leadership failure mode as treating AI as a cost/optimization program rather than a platform for reinvention, warning that traditional ROI models (automate → cut costs) may undermine long-term competitiveness even while showing short-term efficiency gains.

## Quote

> The risk for leaders is not thinking too big, but rather too small.
