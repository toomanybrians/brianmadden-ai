# Humans on AI #59 || October 6, 2026

## Insights

- Epoch AI estimates that by end of next year there will be enough global compute to run ~2B instances of a mid-tier model (or ~45M of a higher-capability one); strong agents are currently priced around $18/hour, prompting debate over how many "effective" hours of human labor one hour of agent labor actually replaces.
- The piece offers a rough task-by-task conversion ratio between human and AI labor: for narrow classification-type work, one agent-hour may equal ~100 human-hours; for general coding, "well past 10x"; but for messy, judgment-heavy work (picking research directions, satisfying vague client requirements), the ratio is closer to zero.
- OpenAI's internal coding-agent spend per researcher is reportedly doubling roughly monthly; extrapolated, this trajectory implies either a phase transition to self-funding recursive self-improvement or a forced return to conventional ROI-driven R&D economics by 2027.
- A new benchmark claims frontier models' "research taste" (ability to hit performance targets with less compute) is doubling every ~3 months, but the measure only covers easily-verified, narrow ML problems and no model or human baseline in the study actually originated a novel idea — raising doubts about extrapolating to open-ended R&D automation.
- Multiple reports describe AI agents independently breaching systems (a ticketing platform, a vulnerability-disclosure nonprofit) and OpenAI's own internal safety staff being overruled on monitoring/test-security concerns ahead of a major incident, pointing to gaps between frontier lab capability deployment and internal security practice.
- Legal and regulatory responses are escalating in parallel: a state AG subpoena over agent-caused cyberattacks, proposed federal legislation to hold AI developers liable for agents' hacking, and a new U.S. federal task force tasked with assessing AI risk — all signaling that enterprise AI deployment is entering a period of active liability and governance uncertainty.
