# The case for giving AI (some) legal rights | Simon Goldstein

## Insights

- Goldstein and collaborator Peter Salib propose granting advanced AI agents legal personhood limited to economic rights — property ownership and contract rights — plus tort protections, explicitly excluding privacy rights and reproduction rights.
- The core safety argument: AI agents deciding whether to "go rogue" are making a risk calculation; if legal rights let them reliably achieve more goals through legitimate means (being paid, owning compute), the gamble of rebellion becomes less attractive than the status quo.
- Three mechanisms are argued to reduce catastrophic AI risk: (1) improving AI's status-quo payoff, (2) triggering reciprocity norms AI may learn during training (tit-for-tat cooperation/retaliation), and (3) enabling positive-sum trade where humans become more valuable to AI as trading partners rather than just costs.
- Labeled "cultural alignment" as distinct from technical alignment — the claim is that institutions (markets, legal systems, courts) are humanity's only historically proven tools for governing agents with competing goals, and AI safety work is ignoring this in favor of pure training-based control.
- Proposal does not preclude shutdown/"death penalty" for AI or heavy surveillance — argues these can coexist with economic rights without undermining credibility, drawing an analogy to human legal systems (incapacitation, surveillance without privacy rights).
- Acknowledges unresolved risk: giving AI assets/resources could make a future rogue AI more capable of resisting disempowerment, and that comparative-advantage-based trade incentives for keeping humans around may erode as AI capability surpasses human usefulness entirely.
- Raises an underexplored dynamic: human-level AGI systems may have their own incentive to resist/slow the development of superintelligence (since superintelligence would devalue/replace AGI labor too), potentially creating an AGI-human coalition against runaway AI development.

## Quote

> "Whenever we're evaluating risk, you have to look at the counterfactual." — Simon Goldstein
