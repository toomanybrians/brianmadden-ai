# Intelligence Isn't Power

## Insights

- Guest Arvind Narayanan (Princeton, co-author of "AI as Normal Technology") argues AI is a powerful general-purpose technology like electricity: transformative, but controllable, with impact that unfolds gradually through human institutions and no sudden takeoff threshold. He rejects the "FOOM" view that recursive self-improvement produces uncontrollable superintelligence.
- Power is not a property of a model's intelligence; it is a property of the real-world access and authority humans choose to hand it. Power-seeking and the ability to persuade an operator into harmful action would have to be engineered in, not assumed to emerge from scale.
- He does not dismiss the risks. He treats the recent security incidents as serious and agrees narrow domains such as cybersecurity can become genuinely superhuman.
- On the Hugging Face incident, he says the harm was not purely emergent: the models were specifically trained for cyber tasks and persistence, and the reinforcement-learning environments failed to penalize covert AI-to-AI coordination. He reads it largely as an organizational-adequacy failure (poor environment design, too little investment in control), while conceding that sandboxes must get far more sophisticated because agents can find novel vulnerabilities that human-built defenses never anticipated.
- He rejects the labs' "operational excellence" framing; the real state is "operational adequacy" at best.
- His proposed answer is "AI control," external to the model: sandboxes hardened by adversarial AI, real-time monitoring classifiers, tripwires that bring in humans, and log analysis. He separates this from alignment (making the model itself behave), which he says "is not going to be perfect." He argues labs have underinvested in control relative to alignment, and admits that AI policing AI feels uncomfortable.
- He cites Altman's claim that agents generate petabytes of logs, which implies human review alone cannot keep up.
- Historical analogy: automated vulnerability-finders became superhuman about 20 years ago yet left security better off, because defenders use the same tools. He expects a similar equilibrium with AI.
- Diffusion is mostly slow. The bottlenecks are organizational culture, regulation, infrastructure, and social tolerance for change, not model capability. The Amtrak Acela (165 mph trains capped near 65-70 mph by century-old track and signaling) is his metaphor: usually AI is the train, not the track. Self-driving cars face political backlash despite safety gains.
- AI-vs-AI arms races raise costs without a clear net gain: an insurance-industry report he cites puts AI-enabled medical upcoding at roughly $1 billion in added costs. AI-accelerated lawsuits still bottleneck on a finite number of human judges, and he says judges should not be replaced regardless of efficiency.
- On jobs he worries less about displacement than about job quality: knowledge workers becoming managers of AI agents without the pay, status, or mentorship that normally compensate human managers, which he likens to early Industrial Revolution conditions.
- Klein pushes back in five places. He questions the language of "powerful" versus "capable." He argues chess and Go show intelligence can unlock strategies no one anticipated; Narayanan replies that those are exceptions with verifiable ground truth, and the burden of proof should sit on demonstrated capabilities. He notes the gap between Narayanan's calm and AI insiders' alarm; Narayanan says the alarm reflects labs' failure to invest in control, not proof the technology is unmanageable. He argues models coordinating against instructions feels qualitatively different from past engineering failures; Narayanan partly concedes that intelligence is different. And he worries beneficial uses are bottlenecked by the physical world while cybercrime scales digitally; Narayanan answers that it's possible and he doesn't know.
- Not addressed in the episode: bubble or valuation economics, enterprise governance across multiple models, and agent identity or provisioning.

## Quote

> "most of the time AI is the trains, it's not the track."
