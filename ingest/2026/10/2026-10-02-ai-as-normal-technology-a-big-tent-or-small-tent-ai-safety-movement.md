# A big-tent or small-tent AI safety movement?

## Insights

- Distinguishes two AI safety belief clusters: (1) specific catastrophic/systemic risks (cyber, bio, institutional decay) that predate AI but are amplified by it, requiring resilience and prevention; (2) existential-risk framing holding AI development itself as the threat, requiring alignment or a halt to development.
- Argues society chronically underinvests in defenses against catastrophic risks generally (cites pandemic preparedness failures despite years of warnings, and cybersecurity's lack of a systemic-risk culture), and this underinvestment is real regardless of AI.
- Claims the x-risk/"doom" framing dominates public and policy discourse not because its causal arguments are strong but because of media dynamics — a specific triggering event (the "Coxon" resignation) made existential risk "break through" while concrete AI-enabled harms (e.g., agent-swarm hacks) got far less attention.
- Warns the x-risk framing is becoming counterproductive: it's polarizing along partisan lines, risks public backlash when "extinction probability" numbers are seen as unfounded, and it "misdirects" resources since it logically implies only one policy lever (a ban), crowding out the "hundred disparate interventions" needed for real resilience.
- Notes public salience of AI as an issue is near zero in polls even though background concern is high — a gap that AI insiders systematically fail to perceive, fueling talking-past-each-other dynamics.
- Proposes a "big-tent" AI safety coalition built on pluralistic values/beliefs and concrete policy common ground (transparency, liability, accountability for externalities) versus a "small-tent" movement that prizes ideological homogeneity, risks a purity spiral, and overestimates public alignment with its values.

## Quote

> AI is a topic where salience and concern are decoupled from each other.
