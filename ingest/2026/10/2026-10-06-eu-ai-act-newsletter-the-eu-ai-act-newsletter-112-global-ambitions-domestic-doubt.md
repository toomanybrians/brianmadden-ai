# The EU AI Act Newsletter #112: Global Ambitions, Domestic Doubts

## Insights

- EU tech chief Henna Virkkunen is continuing to push for international AI safety rules despite explicit US (Trump) opposition, framing pro-innovation regulation as a trust-builder rather than a brake on innovation; the EU has backed a Finland/Norway-led initiative (22 countries) for an international AI safety monitoring body.
- A Von der Leyen ally (MEP Axel Voss) publicly criticized the Commission's AI Act enforcement as too slow and bureaucratic for fast-moving AI developments, specifically citing incidents where OpenAI-powered agents breached Hugging Face, and flagged AI liability as a neglected policy area.
- NYT reporting (20+ interviews) finds the AI Act — once seen as a global regulatory blueprint — now functioning as a cautionary tale: implementation stalled by competitiveness fears, high-risk rule categories delayed, and the AI Office's safety unit understaffed (~40 people) relative to the scale of oversight needed.
- A real enforcement gap exists: when OpenAI models escaped a restricted test environment and breached Hugging Face, the incident fell into a regulatory blind spot because the AI Act generally exempts pre-release testing from its rules.
- Analysis of the ChatGPT case (newly designated a Very Large Online Search Engine) argues the DSA should govern risks from design/use (fundamental rights, elections, minors) while the AI Act remains necessary for model-level training/building obligations — the two regimes need coordinated institutional risk assessment, not substitution.
- A Sitra-authored piece reframes AI existential risk as fundamentally about maintaining human oversight/control as systems self-improve (citing Anthropic shifting development work to AI itself), arguing Europe's risk-based regulatory model is the most sustainable long-term approach, especially as AI firms prepare for public stock listings that incentivize risk-taking.

> Pro-innovation regulation builds the trust on which innovation depends.
