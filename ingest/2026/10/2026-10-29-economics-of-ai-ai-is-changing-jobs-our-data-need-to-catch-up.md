# AI Is Changing Jobs. Our Data Need to Catch Up.

## Insights

- Federal labor statistics can't currently answer basic questions about AI's effect on jobs (e.g., whether layoffs are rising by occupation, whether AI-related roles like data scientists/product managers are growing, whether early-career software developer wages are shifting) due to three structural gaps: slow-updating job categories, fragmented datasets, and thin samples.
- Occupational classifications (SOC) lag actual work by 8-10 years; roles like "product manager" have no distinct federal category and get folded into broader management or marketing classifications, making them untrackable.
- Industry classification lag also hides emerging sectors — quantum computing work may appear under electrical manufacturing or oil-and-gas support, and data center operations may be categorized under commercial real estate leasing.
- Key federal datasets measure different slices of the labor market and can't be joined: CPS links occupation/age/earnings but misses employer-side hiring/layoffs; JOLTS tracks hiring/layoffs but not by occupation; OEWS has occupational wage detail but no age/career-stage data.
- Even where occupations have their own SOC code (e.g., data scientists), surveys like CPS may still lump them into broader categories (e.g., "mathematical science occupations"), preventing isolated analysis.
- CPS survey response rates fell from ~92% (2010) to ~65% (2026), raising concern about representativeness bias by income, education, race, or gender, compounding small-sample problems for detailed occupational analysis.
- Proposed fixes: dual occupational coding (stable primary code + frequently-updated emerging-role label), more consistent occupational detail across CPS/JOLTS/OEWS with published crosswalks, and larger/more responsive survey samples.
