# What Laws (If Any) Might Help Us Safeguard Against Deepfakes this Election Cycle? (Inside my AI Law & Policy Class #12)

## Insights

- Research (OpenAI/Georgia Tech, Sept 2025) shows AI hallucination rates have a mathematically irreducible lower bound tied to classification error rates (generative error rate ≳ 2× misclassification rate) — meaning model unreliability isn't an engineering bug to be fully solved but a structural property of generative systems.
- Hallucinations and deepfakes are categorically distinct: hallucinations are unintentional statistical byproducts of next-token prediction, while deepfakes are intentionally fabricated — this intent distinction is why laws target deepfakes specifically rather than falsehoods generally, and why the First Amendment constrains regulation differently for each.
- US v. Alvarez (2012) establishes that even deliberate lies have First Amendment protection except for defamation, fraud, or incitement — meaning broad "anti-deception" deepfake laws face a high constitutional bar (strict scrutiny) since they are content-based restrictions.
- A six-stage intervention framework for deepfake harms (from a DHS report) — Intent, Research/org preparedness, Model creation, Dissemination, Detection/disclosure, Victim response — is used to show that every single chokepoint has failed or been struck down in practice (e.g., Hawaii's election deepfake law enjoined; California's AB 2655 struck down on Section 230 grounds; TAKE IT DOWN Act only covers intimate imagery, not political deepfakes).
- Disclosure/labeling regimes (Utah, Indiana, Michigan, Colorado, EU AI Act Art. 50, China's labeling rules) face their own First Amendment compelled-speech problem per NIFLA v. Becerra (2018): disclosures effective enough to matter may be constitutionally "too burdensome," while weak disclosures are ineffective.
- Real-world economic deepfake harms are already material and distinct from election harms: Arup lost $25M to a deepfake CFO scam; Ferrari fended off a voice-clone fraud attempt via verification questions — illustrating enterprise-level fraud risk from synthetic media independent of political contexts.
- The piece's normative conclusion (via a hypothetical candidate losing an election to a viral deepfake) is that no governance chokepoint reliably stops fast-moving synthetic media before damage occurs, and the fallback position echoes Brandeis's "more speech, not enforced silence" — favoring counter-speech over regulatory/platform-based suppression.

## Quote

> If there be time to expose through discussion the falsehood and fallacies... the remedy to be applied is more speech, not enforced silence. —Justice Louis Brandeis (1927), cited by Farahany
