# Less for More

## Insights

- Employer-sponsored health insurance covers ~165 million US workers; premiums are projected to rise 11% YoY this year, the steepest increase in two decades, with employees absorbing 8% more in costs via premiums, deductibles, and copays.
- AI in healthcare is cited as a cost driver rather than an efficiency gain: providers use it to generate more detailed documentation, which produces more billing codes and higher reimbursements from private insurers.
- Healthcare is described as a major and growing share of the economy — 18% of GDP now, projected to hit 20% by 2034 — and is forecast to account for 37% of all new US jobs through 2035.
- Health insurance tied to employment is framed as a labor-market distortion: 1 in 4 workers report feeling "locked" into their job specifically because of healthcare coverage, restricting job mobility.
- Industry consolidation (hospital mergers, declining independent practices) and regulatory/licensing restrictions (certificate-of-need laws, state scope-of-practice limits) are identified as structural constraints on healthcare labor supply and competition.
- Proposed reforms include expanding Medicare eligibility downward, enacting site-neutral payments, easing credentialing barriers for foreign-trained and underutilized healthcare workers, and ending private insurance subsidies in favor of "Medicare for More."

## Quote

> AI, which many had hoped would lower administrative costs, is actually increasing reimbursements paid by private insurers.
