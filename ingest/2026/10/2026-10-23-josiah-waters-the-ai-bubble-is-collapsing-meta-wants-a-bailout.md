# The AI Bubble Is Collapsing, Meta Wants a Bailout, Oil Prices Are Surging, and Trump Is Taking America Back to War

## Insights

- Waters writes a partisan, advocacy-style weekly column. The figures are his account of Meta's earnings release, an SEC filing, and press and analyst commentary, and were not checked against primary documents here. A separate wire report (AP) matches the headline numbers: Q3 revenue up 26% to about $64.6 billion, 2026 capex guidance narrowed to $135-145 billion, and nonvoting Class C shares taking effect in January.
- Meta's free cash flow: he says capital spending of about $34.9 billion exceeded operating cash flow of about $32.8 billion, putting free cash flow near negative $2.1 billion. He calls this the first time in over a decade that Meta has spent more than it earned. He adds that Meta has bought back no stock for four quarters, long-term debt rose from about $58.7 billion to $80.9 billion, and roughly $825 billion of longer-term cloud, energy and hardware commitments are on the books. Analysts quoted expect capex near $250 billion in 2027.
- The new Class C shares: he reads the filing as preparing equity offerings of "hundreds of billions" over several years, possibly starting this fall, and argues the nonvoting structure lets Zuckerberg raise money without giving up control. The stock fell about 7% the next day. A Dow Jones report that Berkshire Hathaway might take $40 billion or more is described as unconfirmed, and he argues a Berkshire-style rescue would come on expensive terms.
- Muse, Meta's AI agent, which Zuckerberg now calls the company's future: Waters quotes him saying it is compute-intensive to run and has not launched globally, and that "the math" has to work out. AP separately reports about 10 million accounts in the US and Canada and a Meta target of more than 10x better serving-cost efficiency within a year to make it profitable at scale. The exact wording of the compute remark is Waters' account; I could not confirm it against a transcript. A Wells Fargo analyst quoted by Waters says Muse only becomes profitable with a substantial cut in serving costs.
- Market reaction: he says chip stocks, Nvidia, AMD, Lam Research and Alphabet fell sharply on Thursday (semiconductor index down about 6.7%), and that his own tally of losses across the AI names exceeds $1 trillion over two days. Money moved into Treasuries and defensive stocks. He cites a Bank of America fund-manager survey in which 39% name AI capex as the likeliest source of a credit event, even as the same managers hold their largest tech overweight since 2020.
- GPU economics: he says an H100 that rented for $8-10 an hour in late 2024 now fetches about $2-3, and that debt secured on those chips is losing value faster than the industry admits. He describes CoreWeave bonds yielding about 15% and a Bernstein estimate that it needs about $85 billion to fund signed work. The same stress shows up in other recent notes in this repo.
- His political argument: he says the buildout became national economic policy through the Stargate announcement and later executive actions, that officials who shape AI policy hold financial stakes in the industry, and that reassurances such as the Treasury secretary's early-October "no bubble" claim sit badly beside what Meta's own board was reportedly discussing in August.
- The rest of the piece is war and energy: Brent at a six-month high near $117, a depleted US strategic reserve, and military repositioning toward a possible resumption of the Iran war. These are outside the enterprise-AI scope and are included here only as the macro backdrop he ties to the AI financing story.
- His closing frame: the costs of the buildout are being passed to the public through debt service, electricity bills (he cites higher PJM-grid bills) and retirement-fund losses. His "watch" list is who buys Meta's new stock and at what discount, Brent, and the 10-year Treasury yield around the Fed meeting.
