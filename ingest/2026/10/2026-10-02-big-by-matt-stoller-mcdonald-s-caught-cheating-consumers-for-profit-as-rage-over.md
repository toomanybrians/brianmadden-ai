# McDonald's Caught Cheating Consumers for Profit as Rage Over Cost of Living Burns

## Insights

- McDonald's uses an AI pricing engine (via acquired firm Dynamic Yield and partner Tiger Analytics) to generate a recommended "optimal price" per item per location for its 14,000 franchisees, drawing on transaction data, demographic/income signals, and scraped competitor pricing from Wendy's/Burger King.
- The system produces significant same-product price variation within tiny geographic radii (e.g., a Big Mac at $5.69 vs. $6.89 two miles apart in Fresno), undermining the traditional assumption of stable, comparable national pricing.
- Franchisees nominally retain legal authority over pricing, but several report that deviating from the AI-generated "recommendations" is difficult in practice, raising questions about whether algorithmic tools are being used to functionally override independent pricing decisions (relevant to platform/franchisor power dynamics).
- The piece frames this as part of a broader corporate trend of acquiring algorithmic pricing firms (Home Depot/BlackLocus, RealPage/Yieldstar, Instacart/Eversight) that shift price-setting from competitive market signals to centralized, opaque optimization engines.
- A widely-cited academic study (Dube & Misra/ZipRecruiter) used to justify personalized/AI pricing as consumer-friendly is characterized as methodologically misleading — comparing against an inflated hypothetical "uniform price" rather than the actual lower price customers previously paid.
- Author argues automated "follow-the-leader" pricing, even when each firm claims not to directly coordinate, functionally resembles price matching and softens independent competitive decision-making — an antitrust-relevant effect of algorithmic market awareness.

## Quote

> "Optimization" became a euphemism for maximizing corporate profits.
