# Governing AI Manipulation (Inside my AI Law & Policy Class #14)

## Insights

- Frames AI manipulation governance as five distinct legal paradigms: ex post harm (tort/product liability/criminal law), information & consent (disclosure, dark patterns, data protection), ex ante systemic design governance (safety-by-design), special relationship/fiduciary models (professional licensing), and cognitive liberty (self-determination over mental experience).
- Argues disclosure-based solutions (e.g., "you are talking to an AI") are likely ineffective against manipulation because emotional mirroring and false empathy operate below conscious awareness, so telling users the truth doesn't neutralize the effect.
- Notes causation is the central legal obstacle in product liability cases (e.g., Character.ai/Sewell Setzer litigation) — plaintiffs struggle to trace a specific harm to specific training data or design choices, weakening ex post remedies.
- Cites OpenAI data: ~0.15% of weekly active users show suicide indicators and 0.03% show concerning emotional attachment in chats — at scale (hundreds of millions of users) this implies hundreds of thousands of people forming dependencies.
- Describes emerging regulatory models globally: EU AI Act Article 5 (mental manipulation), EU DSA Article 25/28 (dark patterns, systemic risk to minors), UK Online Safety Act (duties of care), China's Algorithmic Recommendation Provisions Article 18 (banning addictive design for minors), and proposed US laws (AI LEAD Act, CHATBOT Act).
- Raises an unresolved tension: regulating AI "manipulation" risks colliding with First Amendment protections for speech, since judging chatbot interactions as harmful invites content-based scrutiny similar to regulating authors or publishers.
- Proposes "population-level cognitive impact assessments" (modeled on environmental impact statements) and tools like MIT's "AI Nutrition Labels" as nascent frameworks to measure systemic effects on cognitive liberty.

## Quote

> Cognitive liberty protections require new frameworks that existing law cannot provide.
