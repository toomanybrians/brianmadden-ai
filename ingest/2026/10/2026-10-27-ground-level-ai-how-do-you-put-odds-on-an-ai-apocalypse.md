# How do you put odds on an AI apocalypse?

## Insights

- "p(doom)" estimates among AI safety researchers and rationalist-adjacent figures vary wildly (from <0.1% to 67%+), with no shared methodology — several sources describe the process as essentially subjective "vibes" dressed up as probability.
- A 2022 tournament (Existential Risk Persuasion Tournament) found a stark gap between superforecasters (0.38% chance of AI-caused extinction by 2100) and AI-risk domain experts (3%) — a gap that didn't close even after extended debate, and accuracy on near-term questions showed no statistical link to long-term extinction estimates.
- Critics note the forecasting/AI-safety ecosystem is financially entangled: Open Philanthropy/Coefficient Giving has funneled $26M+ into forecasting institutions (FRI, Metaculus) that then produce the extinction estimates used to justify the same funders' concerns — raising insularity concerns, though Coefficient Giving shut its dedicated Forecasting Fund in March 2026.
- Multiple experts (Fischhoff, Hooker, Thorstad) argue domain expertise or forecasting skill on near-term, well-defined questions doesn't transfer to reliable predictions about unprecedented events like human extinction, since there's no valid "reference class" or way to calibrate against outcomes.
- Risk-communication research suggests numerical probabilities carry persuasive weight disproportionate to their reliability — audiences and journalists treat a percentage as more credible/trustworthy than vague language, even when, as with extinction risk, there's no track record to calibrate it against.
- Insiders disagree on whether publicizing these numbers is responsible: some see value in forcing clarity ("all roads lead to probability"), while others (including people who hold high p(doom) beliefs) avoid stating percentages to non-expert audiences because the number itself adds little and can mislead.

## Quote

> "It's hard to untangle" — whether community-held worry about AI inflates individual risk estimates.
