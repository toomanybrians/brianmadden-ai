# The Cyber Risk Discourse is Broken

## Insights

- Argues the open-weight AI cyber risk debate has three camps: frontier labs/national security voices who view open weights as untenable risk, Western moderates (including the author) who see banning them as making the world less safe, and Chinese labs acting on their own government's risk calculus — with the first camp dominating Western discourse disproportionately.
- Claims public evidence to date shows closed models, not open-weight models, have been the documented cause of most existing cyber attacks, challenging the "open dangerous, closed safe" framing; suggests both may be closer to "open unsafe, closed unsafe."
- Contends that if current open-weight models are judged too risky to permit, logical consistency would also require banning public APIs for frontier closed models, since closed-model safeguards are improving more slowly than their cyber capabilities.
- Describes China's AI safety approach as shaped by distinct domestic incentives: mandatory model registration with the government (originally for information control), decentralized bureaucracy, researchers barred from leaving the country, and heavier personal/political risk for lab staff than in the U.S., producing earlier-stage but functioning oversight rather than indifference to safety.
- Points to the Hugging Face–OpenAI incident and Hacktron's hacking of OpenAI as evidence that Western lab institutions prioritize business value over the safety rigor their public messaging implies.
- Notes that predictions of catastrophic harm from Claude Mythos (if leaked open-weight) look overstated in hindsight, since GLM-5.3 has since crossed a comparable capability threshold without evidence of the predicted societal destabilization.

## Quote

> If you think the latest open-weight models need to be banned... you probably also need to make public-facing APIs for the frontier closed-models illegal.
