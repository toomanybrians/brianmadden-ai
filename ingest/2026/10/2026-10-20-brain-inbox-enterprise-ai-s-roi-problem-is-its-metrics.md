# ⚙️ Enterprise AI’s ROI problem is its metrics

## Insights

- Wharton's fourth annual AI adoption report (with GBK Collective, 1,416 US enterprise respondents) defines three markers of AI success — proving value, transforming workflows, and aligning the organization — but only 7% of organizations hit all three.
- Metric choice correlates strongly with results: 35% of departments tracking at least one growth measure (new revenue, new products, better CX) report strongly positive ROI, versus just 13% of those tracking efficiency/hours-saved alone — yet only 22% of managers actually use AI to grow the business, while most focus on productivity/cost-cutting.
- ~40% of respondents judge ROI by hours saved; net-new revenue is the least-used success metric at just 27%, despite its link to better outcomes.
- Having a designated Chief AI Officer correlates with better results: 58% of managers with a newly created CAIO role report strongly positive ROI.
- The report frames 2023–2024 as early "exploration" waves, 2025 as a deeper-integration wave, and 2026 as a fourth wave where "adoption is no longer the question" — the harder work now is reorganizing processes, skills, and departments around AI rather than just deploying it.
- Microsoft's new MAI-Base-2 model (2.8T parameters, 80B active) emphasizes compute efficiency over frontier-scale brute force, claiming 4–12x better FLOPs-efficiency than DeepSeek's top open model, reflecting a broader Microsoft strategy of prioritizing cost, security, and efficiency for enterprise customers.
- Rokt COO Bill Barton describes collapsing the company's job-ladder structure from "more than a dozen" to four, using AI to blur role boundaries and expand the value of generalists over specialists.
