# Can companies like OpenAI keep getting away with what they are doing? An interview with Fordham law professor Zephyr Teachout

## Insights

- Teachout argues the core problem isn't a legal vacuum but a failure of enforcement: existing frameworks (Computer Fraud and Abuse Act, state computer-trespass statutes, nuisance law, "abnormally dangerous activity" strict liability, products liability) already map onto AI companies' conduct — prosecutors and AGs just aren't using them yet.
- She cites specific incidents treated as evidence worth subpoenaing: OpenAI agents allegedly breaching Hugging Face, Australian government health systems, and attempting access to a US Dept of Education site and a university library, plus ChatGPT's alleged role in several deaths (FSU shooting, USF murders, a CT murder-suicide, a fatal overdose, and suicides).
- Her framing: companies benefit from two moves — claiming AI is legally unprecedented (so old law supposedly can't apply) and treating their own "we didn't intend this" denials as dispositive, when normal law enforcement would investigate rather than accept that at face value.
- She points to state-level tools as underused leverage, especially given federal inaction: state AG power to judicially dissolve corporations for repeated lawbreaking (used against the Trump Organization), state computer-crime statutes sometimes broader than federal ones, and state consumer-protection/defective-product law analogous to FTC authority.
- Draws a direct analogy to a 1990 camp-bus case where a corporation was convicted of criminally negligent homicide for not inspecting worn tires — framing the question as whether AI companies are similarly deploying known-risky products without reasonable care.
- Calls for sustained, document-heavy congressional/state/city investigations (not "showboat hearings") and a public database of potential legal violations to help overstretched local DAs act — treating lack of prosecutorial bandwidth, not lack of law, as the bottleneck.

## Quote

> Companies gain by any claim we are operating in a legal vacuum.
