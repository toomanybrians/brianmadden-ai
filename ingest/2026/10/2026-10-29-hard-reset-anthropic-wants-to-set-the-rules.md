# Anthropic Wants To Set The Rules

## Insights

- Anthropic's S-1 filing (filed ahead of its public offering) required explicit disclosure of catastrophic/existential AI risk as a legal risk factor, lending external confirmation to safety claims the company has long made voluntarily.
- The filing frames regulation itself as a business risk: Anthropic acknowledges its own policy advocacy could increase compliance burdens, limit product flexibility, slow growth, and invite heightened scrutiny or political backlash.
- Anthropic has selectively supported weaker state-level regulation (endorsing California's SB 53) while opposing stronger measures (SB 1047), while separately lobbying Congress on a narrower federal framework — reportedly proposing government authority to block model releases only in cases of "imminent risk" of killing more than 50 people.
- The piece highlights Anthropic's political spending infrastructure: donations are routed through PACs like Public First Action and other vehicles, funding "bipartisan" candidates who publicly favor AI safeguards, which gives candidates plausible deniability about corporate influence (e.g., a congressional candidate denying "Anthropic money" despite PAC-routed funds).
- The author argues Anthropic's posture — publicly self-critical on safety while shaping the regulatory agenda it would be subject to — risks producing regulation that reflects the company's own preferences rather than independent oversight.
- The filing states perceived (not just actual) safety incidents could materially harm the business, suggesting safety branding functions partly as reputational risk management for a company in a low-trust industry.

## Quote

> Regulation is hardly regulation if a company wins its own preferences for external oversight.
