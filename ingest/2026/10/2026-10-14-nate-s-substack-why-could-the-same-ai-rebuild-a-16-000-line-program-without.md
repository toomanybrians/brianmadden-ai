# Why could the same AI rebuild a 16,000-line program without seeing its code, yet fail to customize a library for METR? The answer shapes what you hand it.

## Insights

- Challenges the common assumption that AI capability is a single underlying "intelligence" — that excelling at one hard intellectual task (e.g., advanced math) should predict competence at others (e.g., engineering judgment, business reasoning).
- Proposes "narrow superintelligence" as a framework: AI could become extraordinarily capable in specific domains while its abilities grow increasingly uneven ("spikier") rather than converging toward general competence.
- OpenAI's release of 700+ machine-checked mathematical research manuscripts (from an unreleased internal model) is cited as a vivid example of narrow, domain-specific superintelligence — though it can't itself prove capabilities are becoming more uneven since there's no public comparison point.
- Argues training feedback quality drives uneven growth: in domains with cheap, plentiful, verifiable feedback, each attempt yields more learning signal, so those specific abilities compound faster than others (like judgment or contextual appropriateness).
- Gives a concrete coding example: the same model reimplemented a ~16,000-line toolkit purely from observing its behavior, yet failed to produce a usable result when asked to customize a library for METR — illustrating a gap between reconstructive/pattern-matching skill and applied judgment.
- Acknowledges the strongest counterargument: broad improvement across many tasks and convergence toward general ability are distinct claims, and current evidence only supports the former, not the latter.
- Specifies a falsification condition: evidence against the narrow-superintelligence view would be a system that proactively notices when a requirement conflicts with a project's underlying purpose, without being prompted to check.

## Quote

> What if becoming more superintelligent doesn't mean becoming proportionately more general?
