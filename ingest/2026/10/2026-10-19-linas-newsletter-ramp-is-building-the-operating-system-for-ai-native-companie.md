# Ramp is building the operating system for AI-native companies 📲🤖; Revolut is developing its own Meta Muse & Grok Bot, betting that owning the card beats owning the model 🤖💳

## Insights

- Ramp built an AI stack in 90 days — token spend controls, a model router (sends requests to the cheapest model that clears a quality bar), and now Glass, a shared workspace with company memory and 350+ versioned "skills" connecting tools like Slack, Notion, Gong, Snowflake.
- Ramp's internal usage: 323 employees made 6,500+ edits to Glass's knowledge base, one skill scaled feature-request logging from ~300/month to 20,000, and routing cut Ramp's own AI bill 30% overnight — though all figures are self-reported with no external customer data yet.
- The broader workspace layer (memory, shared skills, multiplayer sessions) is being commoditized/open-sourced (e.g., Block's Buzz, YC's QM), so Ramp's differentiation is shifting to controlling and metering AI spend for CFOs rather than owning the workspace itself — still ~70% of Ramp's revenue is card interchange.
- Revolut is testing AIR, an autonomous shopping/purchasing AI agent (UK pilot in December), competing with Meta's Muse and xAI's Grok Bot — its structural advantage is owning the card, payment approval, and banking license outright rather than renting rails through Stripe Link.
- Revolut's argument: owning the full transaction ledger (30-40M transactions/day) feeds a proprietary model (PRAGMA) trained on banking event sequences, giving it insight into what customers can actually afford/buy — not just preference data like Meta — enabling risk-based autonomy limits per customer.
- This connects to a wider standards fight (Meta/Sierra's Personal Agent Protocol) over which party "holds the credential" when an AI agent spends money on a user's behalf; Revolut's MCP rails position it as a potential regulated endpoint other AI assistants could call in Europe.
- Key unresolved question for Revolut: whether the December AIR test still requires human approval per purchase (matching existing competitors) or allows pre-set spending budgets (turning virtual cards into standing mandates) — the latter would open access to commerce commissions far above EU debit interchange caps.
