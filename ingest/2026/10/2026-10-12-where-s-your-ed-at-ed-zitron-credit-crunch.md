# Credit Crunch

## Insights

- Zitron argues that financing the AI data-center build-out is getting more expensive and more fragile, and that the consequences get less attention than they deserve. The piece is opinionated advocacy. The figures are his, drawn from his own earlier analyses and from press reports (The Information, the Financial Times), and were not independently checked here.
- Rates: he says ten-year US Treasury yields are above 5.3% and still rising, which raises the base cost of every AI-related loan. He attributes the sell-off mainly to federal deficits and to oil-driven inflation from the wars in Iran and Ukraine, with competition from hyperscaler bonds a smaller factor.
- Spreads: he says weaker borrowers pay far more than the benchmark. His examples are a large September Oracle bond sale, whose added interest he puts above $7 billion, and CoreWeave debt yielding roughly 11.5% to 13.5%.
- Lenders: citing The Information, he says data-center lenders are becoming more selective, and that projects without a hyperscaler backstop face borrowing costs of roughly 9% to 14%, which he argues breaks their economics.
- The labs: he says Anthropic and OpenAI would need roughly $50 billion a year in debt after going public, and that with negative EBITDA (he cites a negative 160% margin for OpenAI in 2025, from its audited financials) they would likely be rated as junk unless a hyperscaler guarantees the debt. He adds that they own few assets, that training costs count as operating expenses rather than capital spending, and that Anthropic carries about $413 billion in non-cancelable compute commitments.
- Concentration: he says more than 70% of AI revenue, and by his estimate more than 80% of compute demand, comes from the two labs, whose customers are mostly unprofitable AI startups. He also cites more than $200 billion of Nvidia GPUs sitting uninstalled and a Morgan Stanley estimate that over half of the GPUs sold in 2026 through 2028 will have nowhere to plug in.
- He rebuts common objections (Amazon's and Uber's early losses, capacity being reused after a bust, the labs simply cutting costs) and argues that after a bust there would be little demand for the capacity and that a thriving open-source AI market is unlikely.
- Scenario: he sketches a chain from lab financing trouble to hyperscaler earnings misses, a steep drop in Nvidia revenue, neocloud failures, and venture losses, and says he only has to be half right for the outcome to be very bad. He gives no timing.
- He cites Ramp data suggesting AI price cuts are not increasing usage.
- He closes with an Apollo chart from Torsten Slok: tech-sector operating cash flow is expected to roughly double to about $2.4 trillion by 2028, while the sectors that buy AI services expect much smaller gains, which raises the question of who pays.
