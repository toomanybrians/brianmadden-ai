# Breaking news: U.S. banks are taking hits from AI agents

## Insights

- Reports that U.S. banking executives are racing to restrict system access after AI agents caused incidents moving faster than institutions could respond (citing an Axios report by Madison Mills).
- Cites a cluster of concurrent incidents attributed partly to AI agents: attacks on French refrigeration systems, an ongoing wave of cyberattacks in Japan, attacks on Hugging Face and the Australian government, and activity from a Latin American hacking group ("Cybercartel").
- Argues regulatory focus on "frontier models" (defined by compute usage) is misguided, since malicious actors don't need state-of-the-art models to cause serious harm — current, non-frontier models are already causing damage across banking, education, and politics (deepfakes, disinformation).
- Lays out a prior (early 2025) "perfect storm" thesis for cybersecurity: cheap/declining-cost generative AI is well-suited to phishing and deepfakes; LLMs remain vulnerable to jailbreaking and prompt-injection with no known fix; AI-generated code introduces security holes when developers don't understand it; and deregulation/cuts to federal enforcement staff are weakening oversight capacity.
- Predicts the AI industry will avoid taking real responsibility for resulting harms, that the (unspecified) administration will not meaningfully hold companies accountable, and that the situation will worsen — framed as vindication of his earlier warnings.
- Worth noting as a dissenting data point against "AI agents are safely productizing" narratives common in enterprise AI coverage — Marcus is using concrete, named incidents (not just speculation) to argue current deployed systems, not hypothetical future ones, are the active threat surface.

## Quote

> Findings have alarmed banking executives, who are racing to restrict access to their systems as AI moves at a velocity faster than financial institutions can respond.
