# Enough with the Human Claw Machine

## Insights

- Kinder uses mathematicians' emotional reactions to AI solving famous math problems (citing Steven Strogatz and PhD student Kirwin Hampshire) as evidence that work loss triggers grief tied to identity, purpose, mastery, and even spiritual crisis — not just income loss.
- She critiques a common policy script she calls the "human claw machine": the reflexive advice to move displaced workers into trades, care work, or entrepreneurship ("build, care, or start"), arguing this ignores individual temperament and treats people as interchangeable units.
- Draws on interviews with workers across professions (auditor, bank teller, engineer, electrician's apprentice) to show job fit is deeply personal — a swap that suits one person (e.g., customer-facing work) would be miserable for another.
- Cites sociologist Jennie Brand's research that displaced workers suffer lasting well-being harm even when reemployed at equal pay, partly from lost relationships/community and partly because "one job isn't just as good as another."
- Argues the "coal miners learn to code" retraining paradigm failed before and will fail again, and that current AI-era discourse repeats the same mistake by prioritizing wage replacement over fit and meaning.
- Calls for reframing the future-of-work narrative around a third "expand" category (proactively growing diverse good jobs) rather than only defending a shrinking "remain" circle, and for bottom-up policymaking that asks people what they actually want rather than deciding for them.

## Quote

> People need agency, and a belief that the future will be better for them and their kids.
