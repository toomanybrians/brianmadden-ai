# How AI Is Changing Strategy: Rethinking What Your Business Is Designed to Do

## Insights

- AI's productivity gains are often absorbed rather than captured strategically: without changing KPIs or processes, employees just produce more output (more code, more decks) rather than better outcomes — Mollick calls pure-automation framing a trap.
- Agents can now reliably complete tasks that previously took humans 2-18 weeks, with high success rates — a jump from AI handling ~30-45 minutes of work a year ago to 16+ hours per instruction now, fundamentally changing how work should be assigned (not prompted back-and-forth, but delegated like a team via a brief/RFP — Mollick's "conjurer" model, succeeding earlier "centaur" and "cyborg" framings).
- Job boundaries are blurring in practice (coders doing design, designers coding, PMs doing everything), forcing organizations to confront basic questions about what the organization actually produces and why.
- Recommended organizational model for AI adoption: "leadership, lab, and crowd" — leadership must make real decisions (not just mandate AI use), a dedicated cross-functional lab benchmarks and builds new systems, and the broader workforce ("crowd") needs access to frontier-grade (not inferior) models to avoid miscalibrated expectations.
- Since everyone now effectively manages AI, "AI skills look like management skills" — cited research found the most successful first-pass coders were managers, not engineers, because specifying intent well matters more than raw technical skill.
- Durable sources of human differentiation/competitive advantage when everyone has access to the same frontier models: deep domain expertise, wide cross-domain knowledge, "taste" (judgment in selecting among AI-generated options, including fit with company identity), and agency (willingness to act under uncertainty).
- Experience is becoming more valuable, not less, in the AI era — junior/less-experienced users perform worse with AI (cited in studies of consultants and from Anthropic), undercutting the assumption that "digital natives" or younger workers have an inherent AI advantage.
- Labor market impact is genuinely uncertain: squeezed junior hiring is plausible, but so is more insourcing/hiring as non-specialists (e.g., non-coders) gain capability — Mollick states AI labs are explicitly aiming to replace human labor entirely, a goal he says society should resist.
- Near-term risk concerns raised: cybersecurity environment is expected to become severely strained as agents proliferate (companies reportedly reallocating 20-50%+ of staff to hardening systems), plus risks of job automation over augmentation, degraded information environment, and disempowerment from passive "verifier" roles.

## Quote

> The AI labs... are aiming for the job apocalypse. They want AI to replace all human labor.
