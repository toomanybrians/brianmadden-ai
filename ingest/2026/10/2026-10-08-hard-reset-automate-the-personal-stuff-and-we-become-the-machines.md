# Automate the Personal Stuff and We Become the Machines

## Insights

- Agentic personal assistants (e.g., Meta's Muse, X's Grok Bot, Instinct) are already being used for tasks like finance, flowers, fantasy football, and outfit planning — and already producing real-world errors, not just hallucinated text: a CFO's Grok Bot posted his bank balance to company Slack, and an AI assistant signed a watermark on flowers sent to a user's fiancé.
- The "AI utopia" pitch (automation freeing humans for meaningful leisure, funded by UBI) is framed as suspect because it's being sold by the industry's wealthiest figures with no visible move away from income-based work.
- Even when these assistants work as intended, the cost isn't failure but minimized human involvement — delegating discernment over one's own plans, relationships, and experiences in exchange for convenience.
- Instinct's CEO (Noah Shinn) deliberately designed the product to mimic a human personal assistant via text (no interface), explicitly questioning how society reorders once everyone has an "Instinct"-style agent.
- The piece argues assistants that centralize interactions with the internet into one company's infrastructure strip away the everyday exposure that forms personal taste and judgment — leaving only work as the remaining non-automated activity.
- Cited external counterpoint: Anthropic whistleblower Jacob Coxon publicly argued for a global consensus favoring a post-work, UBI-funded AI utopia; Jon Stewart pushed back on air, questioning whether that's a majority view.

## Quote

> "It's easiest to literally pretend you have a superhuman person with a phone... and that's it." — Noah Shinn, Instinct CEO
