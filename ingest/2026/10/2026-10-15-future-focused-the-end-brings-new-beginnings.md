# The End Brings New Beginnings

## Insights

- The doomsday predictions that software engineers would be fully replaced by AI by end of 2026 proved wrong on extinction but correct on disruption: layoffs, squeezed early-career hiring, and heavy role change are real.
- AI excels at pattern recognition (predicting statistically likely output) but lacks system architecture judgment, problem-solving intuition, and domain context — it cannot reason through why legacy infrastructure was built the way it was, only guess and "flatten" answers.
- Jobs bundle routine/mechanical tasks together with true domain expertise under one title; when AI automates the routine parts, it feels like identity loss even though it can free people to focus on higher-value work.
- Resistance to AI-driven change is framed as a grief response (denial, clinging to "the way things have always been done," hoarding institutional knowledge) rather than a rational calculation — and this pattern shows up especially among senior professionals nearing retirement.
- Clinging to legacy knowledge instead of transferring it doesn't preserve a professional's legacy — it dies with them, leaving successors to deal with breakage in systems no one understands.
- Passivity in the face of AI disruption is framed as an active choice to cede agency to market forces, not a neutral stance; suggested individual responses include unbundling monotonous tasks from real expertise, building a "portfolio of skills," and adopting a "short memory" to recover from failed attempts.
- The software engineering disruption is presented as an early signal ("canary in the coal mine") for the same dynamics — role unbundling, identity grief, resistance — expected across all fields and industries.

## Quote

> AI can synthesize pages of text and spot statistical patterns in massive databases in seconds, but it will never understand what the text means.
