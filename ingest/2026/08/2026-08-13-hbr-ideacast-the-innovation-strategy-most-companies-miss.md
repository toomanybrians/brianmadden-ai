# The Innovation Strategy Most Companies Miss

## Insights

- Winter and Govindarajan argue companies should design for emerging/low-resource markets first, not adapt premium-market products downward — solving for extreme constraints (cost, no electricity, no repair infrastructure) forces innovations that can later disrupt wealthy markets with better performance at lower cost ("reverse innovation").
- The biggest barrier to this approach isn't technology but organizational "dominant logic" — entrenched systems, incentives, and processes built around rich-market assumptions, which the authors compare to turning a Titanic.
- Three converging forces are pushing multinationals toward emerging-market-first innovation: slowing growth in rich countries, emerging-market competitors capable of disrupting incumbents at home, and a widening affordability crisis even within wealthy countries (healthcare, education, groceries).
- Their framework for spotting opportunities uses three "merits": problem merit (does it affect many people with a viable business behind it), innovation merit (is genuine innovation actually required, vs. philanthropy or minor adaptation), and value merit (will both producers and adopters actually gain enough value to sustain it).
- On AI's role in this design work: it's described as strong for rapidly aggregating background/context information, but weak at the core tasks of this kind of innovation — integrating unrecorded human/emotional information, understanding physical-world nuance (friction, weather, tactile feedback), and building the face-to-face rapport with stakeholders that shapes design decisions.
- Process emphasis: rigorous, in-person ethnographic requirement-gathering (e.g., a team living in rural India for two months) is treated as the critical, hard-to-compress foundation of successful design — in one cited case, three months of requirements work preceded a mostly successful 18-month product development cycle.

## Quote

> The biggest barrier to reverse innovation is not technology... It is the dominant logic. — Vijay Govindarajan
