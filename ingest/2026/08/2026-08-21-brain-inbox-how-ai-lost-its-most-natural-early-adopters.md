# ⚙️ How AI lost its most natural early adopters

## Insights

- Goldman Sachs research finds AI is already slowing job growth in specific white-collar categories globally — call centers, software publishing, management consulting, advertising — with call center employment down 39% from historical trend in the US, 33% in Canada, 27% in Germany since 2022.
- Entry-level workers in AI-exposed occupations are being hit hardest by hiring slowdowns, even as overall AI adoption rates rise 15-20% in developed markets and 10-15% in emerging ones.
- Broader labor market signals remain contradictory: other research shows AI-investing companies growing headcount (entry-level hiring up 12%), half of AI-related layoffs resulting in rehiring, and AI increasing scope/hours of work rather than narrowing it.
- Companies may be using AI as a scapegoat for headcount cuts unrelated to actual automation gains, which risks backfiring via unproven ROI and growing employee/public resentment toward AI deployments.
- Pew Research (Aug 2026) finds a majority of under-30 Americans (55%) are now more concerned than excited about AI, matching older age groups — a reversal from the historical pattern of youth being the earliest, most enthusiastic tech adopters.
- Gen Z sentiment shift extends to career choice: large majorities in the US, UK, Germany, and France say technical trades feel more meaningful than office jobs, a trend echoed even by Geoffrey Hinton advising people to "train to be a plumber."
- Separately, enterprise vendors (Snowflake's Cortex AI Gateway, NVIDIA's NeMo Switchyard) are pushing "model routing" — matching task complexity to model cost/size — as inference costs are projected to rise more than fivefold per agentic workflow through 2028.
