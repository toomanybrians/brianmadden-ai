# How People Without Formal Power Gain Influence

## Insights

- Argues authority in organizations is now distributed rather than hierarchical, making indirect influence (rather than direct command) a critical skill for cross-functional, vendor, and upward-management work.
- Grounds the mechanism in self-determination theory: people have needs for competence, relatedness, and especially autonomy; direct pressure triggers "reactants" (pushback) because it threatens autonomy, so effective influence works by preserving the other person's sense of agency.
- Describes "influence spotting" — identifying informal power holders (not just titles) by watching who sways hard decisions, tracking micro-alliances, and doing "chain analysis" back through who influences the influencer.
- Distinguishes healthy backchanneling (temporary, trust-earned, used to unlock a specific frozen decision) from being a "power grabber" who runs permanent backchannels or over-networks superficially — warns that indiscriminate connecting (intros, favors, forwarded articles) erodes credibility.
- Introduces "pulse reading" — sensing a room's emotional/motivational state and naming it aloud (e.g., "there's a trust issue here") to build resonance and influence, with timing (room "expansive" vs. "contracting") determining whether to speak up or hold back.
- Recommends a "triple B" prep method (background, behavior, beliefs) — researching people's fuller identity before a meeting — as foundational groundwork for building the rapport indirect influence depends on.
- Notes in passing that organizational anxiety about AI displacing jobs ("ambient dread") is itself an example of a room-pulse a skilled reader would need to name and address.
