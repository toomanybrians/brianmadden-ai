# The Pangloss of Pangram

## Insights

- AI-detection tools like Pangram classify text by pattern-matching stylistic features (word choice, syntax, sentence shape) rather than assessing whether the writing is worthwhile — the tool measures composition, not quality.
- Even Substack's own framing admits the ceiling: detection can show whether AI was used, not whether "great human care" went into the work, yet readers collapse the nuanced spectrum into a binary shame verdict.
- Historical parallel: authorship suspicion is an old ritual, not a new one — Lincoln's Gettysburg Address was mocked as unpresidential, and MLK's "I Have a Dream" was later shown to draw on at least nine contributors' prior work, without diminishing its impact.
- The piece argues authorship's real ethical line isn't "how much was typed" but whether the human genuinely weighed, read, and stood behind the output before publishing — dictation, ghostwriting, and AI assistance are treated as equivalent in kind.
- Predicts that AI detectors will become less reliable as models improve, meaning the false authenticity signal won't disappear but will become quieter and harder to notice — an unresolved problem obscured rather than solved.
- Frames AI-detection scores as a "Rorschach test": reactions to a score reveal the reader's pre-existing bias about AI more than anything about the text itself.

## Quote

> Regardless of the tools used, a human being must mean what is said.
