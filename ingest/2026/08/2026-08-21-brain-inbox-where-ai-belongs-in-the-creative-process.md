# ⚙️ Where AI belongs in the creative process

## Insights

- The piece frames creative tradition as "cultural transmission" — art historically evolves by building on prior human work (e.g., Socrates→Plato→Aristotle, Renaissance→Baroque), and questions what happens to that lineage when the "inspiration" driving new work is AI rather than lived human experience.
- Cites octopus reproduction (no maternal teaching, each generation restarts from scratch) as a natural-world analogy for what's lost without cultural transmission — despite individual octopus intelligence, the species doesn't progress collectively.
- Argues AI models aren't neutral: they absorb values/morals from training data, and an Anthropic report found Claude's values shift by version (e.g., leaning "warmer" vs. more rigid), which then shapes the emotional tone of AI-generated art in ways users may not perceive.
- Even in a hypothetical bias-free model, the author argues the result would be "soulless art" because the feelings conveyed aren't actually the artist's — they're an artifact of the AI's placement/choices.
- Offers a counter-example: the Google Flow film festival, where AI was used for animation/visual effects layered onto a human artist's (Dion Lee's) footage while all creative decisions stayed with the artist — presented as a case of AI advancing rather than replacing human expression.
- Cites a Pew Research finding that adults under 30 are as likely to say chatbots hurt their creativity as help it, used as evidence that even younger, high-AI-adoption users sense this tradeoff.
- Proposes a framing principle: AI should replace the "scaffold" (labor/logistics of creation, akin to Michelangelo's wooden platforms) rather than the artist — technology should ease production, not substitute for the human creative agent.

## Quote

> The goal for artists... should be to have AI replace the shaky wooden platforms, not Michelangelo.
