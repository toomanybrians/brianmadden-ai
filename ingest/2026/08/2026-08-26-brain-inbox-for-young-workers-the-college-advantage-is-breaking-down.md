# For young workers, the college advantage is breaking down

## Insights

- CPS data through July 2026 shows a historic inversion: 22-26 year-olds with a bachelor's degree now have higher unemployment (6.1%) than those with some college/associate degree (5.4%), breaking a 30-year pattern where more education meant less unemployment.
- Young non-graduates are near the bottom third of their own historical unemployment distribution (a good labor market for them), while young BA+ workers sit at the 75th percentile of their own history and are still climbing — this is a targeted, not broad, weakness.
- The deterioration scales precisely with career stage among graduates: ages 22-26 at the 75th percentile of historical unemployment, 27-34 at 66th, 35+ at a near-normal 54th — pointing to an entry-level ladder problem, not a "college graduates in general" problem.
- The weakness is concentrated in hiring, not layoffs: Handshake data shows entry-level postings down 15% year-over-year with applications per posting up over 25%; tech, consulting, and finance froze hiring while in-person service sectors kept adding workers.
- Two proposed drivers: (1) generative AI is well-suited to entry-level knowledge work (drafting, coding, research, basic analysis) but not to the hands-on/in-person work non-graduates typically do; (2) a decades-long rise in the share of each cohort holding a bachelor's degree created an oversupply of graduates chasing professional entry jobs, a slow-building condition that made this market vulnerable to shocks.
- A Stanford "canaries in the coal mine" study (ADP data through June 2026) finds employment of 22-25 year-olds in AI-exposed occupations is 19% below where it would be had it tracked less-exposed peers — driven by reduced hiring, not layoffs, and appearing specifically where AI substitutes for human tasks.
- Immigration enforcement is cited as tightening the labor market for non-graduates specifically, since undocumented workers compete mostly in lower-education job markets, leaving graduate labor markets unaffected by that dynamic.
- The author notes this is occurring amid record corporate profits and rare layoffs overall (4.1% national unemployment), warning that a recession could turn this into much sharper cuts given how little slack currently exists in graduate hiring.

## Quote

> The problem is not college graduates in general. It's the bottom rungs of the white-collar career ladder.
