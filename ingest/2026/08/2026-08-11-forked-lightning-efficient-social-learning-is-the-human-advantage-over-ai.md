# Efficient social learning is the human advantage over AI

## Insights

- Deming frames the human-AI division of labor through comparative advantage: humans don't need to be better than AI at anything to remain economically valuable, only relatively better at some tasks — comparative advantage lets both "trade" tasks profitably.
- He argues humans' core edge is sample-efficient learning — citing Dwarkesh Patel's estimate that humans learn from thousands to a million times less data than AI models — while AI's edge is near-zero-cost replication and distribution of learned knowledge at scale.
- This asymmetry implies humans retain comparative advantage in "high-context" tasks requiring continual updating and inference (reading intent, trust-building, team dynamics), while AI dominates "low-context" tasks with verifiable, stable answers like code.
- Social skill is redefined not as charisma/networking but as inference over hidden mental states — what people want, know, and believe others think — and this inference is largely person-specific, so it doesn't transfer/scale the way AI knowledge does.
- Cites his own research (with Ben Weidmann) using randomized team assignment to isolate individuals who reliably boost team performance ("team players"), finding this trait correlates with a theory-of-mind test (RMET) rather than IQ.
- Notes frontier LLMs already perform well on theory-of-mind/social-cognition benchmarks, complicating assumptions that social inference will remain a durable human moat — an open tension the piece doesn't fully resolve.
- Positions this argument alongside a broader debate (Imas, Ozimek, Sun, Abaluck, Noah Smith) over whether "human touch" will remain economically demanded or is just a costly signal/marketing pitch.

## Quote

> Because humans learn more efficiently than AI, we (currently) have a comparative advantage in sparse, high-context tasks.
