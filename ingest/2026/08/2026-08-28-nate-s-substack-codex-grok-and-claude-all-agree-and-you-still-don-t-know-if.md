# Codex, Grok and Claude all agree, and you still don't know if they're right. The guide I use to decide.

## Insights

- The author distinguishes a subtler form of "AI brain rot" from laziness: extraordinary, fast, polished productivity where the model forms opinions, resolves ambiguity, and interprets feedback while the human just approves/rejects outputs — eventually the person stops being able to make those decisions unaided.
- Because output quality keeps improving, this erosion of independent judgment is hard to detect — "brain rot can coexist with excellent output."
- Proposes "friction-maxxing" (adapting a term coined by Kathryn Jezer-Morton in The Cut, Jan 2026) as a deliberate practice: adding resistance back into AI workflows at the exact points where a smooth answer would cost a needed decision, rather than removing friction everywhere.
- Gives concrete risk examples across domains: students submitting better essays but losing the argumentative struggle that makes an idea their own; employees producing convincing strategies without knowing which assumptions are fragile; product agents hiding their limits and training users to trust false "completion signals."
- Piece previews a framework (not detailed in this excerpt): five conditions for deciding when to slow down vs. let AI run at full speed, judging new agents by how they disclose limitations, resisting flattened taste by requesting more variations, and a one-question method for structuring arguments with AI.

## Quote

> Brain rot can coexist with excellent output. That is what makes it dangerous.
