# These Harvard Students Don't Want to Waste Their Careers at McKinsey

## Insights

- The piece frames elite career recruiting as a "war for talent" in which consulting, banking, law, and VC firms have historically outspent alternative causes for top graduates — a structural talent-allocation problem relevant to how knowledge workers choose where to apply their skills.
- Bregman's Moral Ambition Fellowship at Harvard drew applications from 8% of the entire junior class in its first year, suggesting real appetite among high-achieving students for alternatives to traditional prestige-track "BS jobs."
- A concrete example, UpSide.org, is building shared AI infrastructure for under-resourced advocacy groups (e.g., public health, tax fairness) to match the analytical capacity of corporate lobbying teams — a case of AI being used to redistribute knowledge-work leverage rather than concentrate it.
- In an early pilot, UpSide's AI tooling analyzed regulatory data across 37 countries (2,000+ data points), work one advocate said would otherwise take weeks — illustrating AI's potential to compress research/analysis labor in policy and advocacy work.
- The fellowship model is explicitly expanding its remit to include "making sure AI doesn't go catastrophically wrong" as a future focus area for redirected elite talent, alongside democratic resilience.
- The broader movement reflects a mid-career and early-career talent redirection thesis: pulling skilled people out of extractive/status-driven knowledge work into public-interest-oriented work, partly enabled by AI tools that lower the resource barrier to competing with well-funded institutions.
