# How America Turned Against the AI Industry

## Insights

- Cites a survey finding 75% of Americans now oppose local data center development, with the opposition consistent across gender, age, socioeconomic status, and political party.
- Argues the water-usage narrative against data centers is factually weak (data centers use far less water than golf courses, lawns, or alfalfa fields per cited sources) but persists because it's a memetically "sticky" story — simple, concrete, emotional, and moralized — regardless of accuracy.
- Frames the water story as a stand-in for deeper, harder-to-articulate grievances: local process failures (NDAs, tax subsidies), company towns dependent on a single employer, distrust of distant wealthy tech interests, and broader anti-corporate/anti-AI sentiment.
- Notes a rapid political reversal: Republican and Democratic officials who recently supported AI/data center growth are now backing moratoriums or halting projects (examples: Texas governor Abbott halting up to 1,800 projects; Michigan Senate candidate backing a one-year moratorium; a Pennsylvania governor's race ad attacking data centers) — attributed to politicians fearing voter backlash rather than genuine reconsideration.
- Contends the industry oversold AI's benefits (curing cancer, democratizing creativity, productivity gains) while downplaying known risks (job loss, data theft, brain rot) and introducing new fears (doom, recursive self-improvement), which fed public distrust once the promised outcomes didn't visibly materialize.
- Argues that public opposition rests on people's own lived, anecdotal experience (kids doing worse in school, new grads worried about jobs, AI slop, dependency/loneliness effects) that they consider valid truth, regardless of industry statistics or long-term projections claiming net benefit.
