# Previously Unmanufacturable

## Insights

- Traditional software requires learning a specific vocabulary/interface (Figma's layers, Salesforce's accounts, CAD's splines), and power has always scaled with required expertise — usability labs exist to bridge that gap.
- AI changes this by making natural language a universal interface layer: a person states an intended outcome and an agent translates it into the software's native "grammar," first seen in AI translating English into code (C++, Rust, TypeScript), now extending to application-layer concepts.
- Example cited: a founder (Yana Welinder) with no CAD skills used AI to operate CAD software on her behalf to design a garment inspired by Ruth Asawa's sculptures — something she couldn't have manufactured/designed herself.
- This inverts product design logic: rather than software needing to be simple for untrained humans, software built for AI agents can be complex and highly documented, since agents can hold and reason over more of a system's schema than a human clicking through menus.
- The product manager's role shifts from designing every screen/interaction to building a "harness" enabling an agent to act safely and completely within a system.
- Documentation becomes more central to product design (previously mostly an infrastructure/API concern), and usability testing evolves into "evaluations" — observing how an agent operates the software rather than how a human does.
- The expert practitioner doesn't disappear; the skill shifts from configuring individual components/syntax to higher-level systems architecture — understanding how pieces fit together.

## Quote

> AI closes it a different way, by making English the interface.
