# $12B of US ratepayers' money wasted on a modeling mistake and PJM wants to do it again

## Insights

- SemiAnalysis reverse-engineered PJM's opaque "Reserve Requirement Study" (its capacity-planning model) and found it underestimates existing power plant reliability by ~4GW, because it ignores winter cold-air turbine efficiency gains (up to 25%) and post-Storm-Elliott winterization investments made by ~400 of PJM's ~700 gas plants.
- This modeling error is estimated to have cost PJM's 66 million ratepayers ~$12B in overpaid capacity auctions across 2025-2027, with disproportionately large cost impact from very small volume corrections (e.g., $6.7B saved from procuring just 14MW less power) due to PJM's steep, right-angled supply curve.
- PJM's capacity market is structurally unusual: it prices its entire generation fleet (new and existing) in one auction with one-year contracts, unlike other markets that separate new-build (long-term contracts) from existing capacity — this design amplifies small modeling errors into huge price swings when demand grows, which is directly relevant to datacenter-driven load growth.
- PJM's upcoming "emergency auction" (Sept 30–Oct 21, 2026) plans to sign 11-15 year power contracts (through 2043) for 6.8GW to backstop expected datacenter demand growth, but with no committed counter-parties — meaning residential ratepayers could again bear the cost if forecasted datacenter load doesn't materialize.
- Many datacenter developers are reportedly bypassing PJM entirely, pursuing power in other markets or building behind-the-meter generation, due to PJM's slow interconnection queues, lack of cost clarity, and unattractive terms in the backstop auction design.
- PJM's governance is described as deadlocked ("vetocracy") — reform requires two-thirds support across five member sectors, and even winterization/cold-air crediting proposals that passed technical task forces were blocked at the committee level, despite PJM's own hired consultant (E3) confirming the modeling bias.

## Quote

> PJM's model includes errors that we estimate have cost all of its 66 million residents a total of $12B between 2025 and 2027 alone.
