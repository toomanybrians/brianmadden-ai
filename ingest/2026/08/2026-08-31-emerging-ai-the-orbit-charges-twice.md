# The Orbit Charges Twice

## Insights

- Major players disagree on what orbital AI compute is actually for: SpaceX's IPO filing names inference as the workload, Starcloud's whitepaper argues for training, and Google's Project Suncatcher also targets training but bases its case on inter-satellite bandwidth rather than economics.
- The debate reduces to which of three physical links (intra-spacecraft, inter-satellite, and satellite-to-ground) each proponent prices and generalizes from — a framing device the piece uses to explain why arguments talk past each other.
- Actual operating orbital compute as of August 2026 is minimal: ~40 Nvidia Orin processors on 10 Kepler satellites, one H100 on a single Starcloud satellite, and 12 Chinese Earth-observation satellites — a stark contrast to headline ambitions.
- Regulatory filings claim ~1.16 million satellites applied for (SpaceX, Starcloud, Blue Origin, Cowboy Space combined) versus ~14,500 currently active satellites; none of these applications have been approved, and "accepted for filing" is being misreported as approval.
- Skeptics (ABI Research's Andrew Cavalier, Gartner's Bill Ray, AWS's Matt Garman) argue orbital LLM training/inference is currently uneconomic, with Cavalier suggesting Earth-observation preprocessing as the more viable near-term use case.
- Kepler's CEO Mina Mitry, despite running what's described as the largest operating compute cluster in orbit, resists the "data center" label and believes current orbital workloads skew toward inference over training.
