# good post, make sure to subscribe to this

## Insights

- Political scientist Jeff Ding's "diffusion theory" (from *Technology and the Rise of Great Powers*) argues national economic leadership in tech revolutions comes not from inventing a leading sector but from diffusing general-purpose technologies (steam power, electricity, computing) more broadly and quickly than rivals — a process that unfolds over decades. Applied to AI, this implies frontier-model dominance may be the wrong thing for companies to optimize for.
- "Skill infrastructure" — education/training systems that widen the pool of people able to work with a technology — is the key enabler of diffusion, per Ding; institutions that scale engineering skill matter more than institutions that produce breakthrough inventions.
- Paul David's "Dynamo and the Computer" analogy: early factory electrification saw giant motors bolted onto old steam-era shaft-and-belt layouts with no productivity gain; real gains came decades later when engineers redesigned plants around distributed small motors. Implies AI's economic value depends on reorganizing work, not just deploying bigger models, and hints at a future of many small/specialized (possibly open-source) models rather than one giant centralized model.
- Diffusion is described as an organizational problem, not a technical one — the know-how to redesign work around a technology accumulates person-by-person, team-by-team, inside practitioners' own contexts (citing Bessen's *Learning by Doing* and Herman's *Freedom's Forge*).
- Because frontier models improve so fast, model access itself isn't a durable advantage — organizational skill/know-how is the only asset that "survives each model generation."
- Proposed enterprise AI operating model (via Ethan Mollick and Dan Guido/Trail of Bits): needs leadership modeling hands-on use, a "lab" that converts individual discoveries into shared tools, and "the crowd" (everyday staff) who generate most applied discoveries. Guido adds: standardize the toolchain, write explicit rules explaining the risk model behind policies (not just warnings), and build a staged "capability ladder" (resistance → tool comfort → proactive adoption/sharing → creating new tools).

## Quote

> "It is ordinary engineers, not heroic inventors, who matter." — Jeff Ding, quoted by Tim O'Reilly
