# The Host Pays the Empire

## Insights

- Sovereign AI/cloud data centers function less like real infrastructure and more like geopolitical "bases": patrons (US, China) place hardware in host countries as alignment signals, not because of proven compute demand.
- A census of 59 sovereign AI/cloud programs across 47 non-major countries found no disclosed power purchase agreements, 42% never past the announcement/MOU stage, and most investment figures are rounded PR numbers rather than documented contracts.
- Control persists after installation through a three-layer "armory": revocable export licenses, hardware/software dependency (CUDA updates, spare parts, RMA) that lets clusters degrade if cut off, and an unverified/disputed possibility of chip-level kill switches.
- Financial outcomes are poor almost regardless of funding source — Kenya's Chinese-financed Konza center generates ~$1M/year in declining revenue against a $180M loan; Papua New Guinea's earlier Huawei-built center was later called a "failed investment" with broken security.
- Payment direction in base/infrastructure deals follows bargaining power, not a fixed patron-pays-host rule; poor countries with strategic geography (like PNG or Cold War-era Kyrgyzstan) can extract payment by playing patrons against each other, while others (like Kenya) end up paying and getting little.
- China's "Digital Silk Road" model emerged as accidental, revenue-driven lending later rebranded as strategy, while the US AI-export push has funding capacity but no coordinated country-targeting strategy; both are now demanding exclusivity without paying for it.

## Quote

> A sovereign facility whose business case is based on serving foreign customers is not true sovereignty; it is simply an export processing zone with more effective marketing.
