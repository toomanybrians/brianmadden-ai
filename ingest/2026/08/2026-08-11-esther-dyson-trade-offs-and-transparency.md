# Trade-offs and transparency

## Insights

- Onix's model narrows scope deliberately (Small Language Models trained on one expert's vetted content) rather than building a broad general-purpose assistant with guardrails bolted on — a design bet that starting small and constrained beats starting large and restricting after the fact.
- The platform reframes expertise as a scalable asset: each professional invests significant ongoing time (hours/week for 6+ months, continuing) to encode their own methodology, letting one expert effectively serve far more people than a 1:1 practice allows.
- Revenue-sharing (70% to the expert) and non-exclusivity, with deletion of the model/data if the expert leaves, is positioned as an aligned-incentive structure ("if the experts do not make money, we do not make money") — a possible template for how AI platforms could partner with knowledge workers rather than replace or extract from them.
- Hard scope limits are treated as the core safety mechanism: no diagnosis, no prescribing, restricted to the expert's own approved material, with escalation to human providers for clinical matters — contrasted against unconstrained "AI companion" or advice bots.
- The piece juxtaposes this constrained, mission-aligned business model against a much larger and more profitable pattern (unregulated peptide sales, AI-driven "pill mills") where product-sales incentives corrupt advice quality — illustrating how business model choice, not the AI technology itself, determines whether AI-mediated expertise serves or exploits users.
- Affordability remains unresolved: the founders float ideas like buy-one-give-one models or insurer partnerships, but subscription-based access still risks limiting who benefits from scaled expert advice.

## Quote

> If the experts do not make money, we do not make money. They are not our clients, they are our business partners.
