# The website that created an AI clone of its editor in chief

## Insights

- Every (a ~30-person AI media/product company) built an AI copy-editing agent by training on 30,000 historical edits from its editor-in-chief, back-testing prompts against her past work to replicate her judgment; the tool improves automatically as she corrects its output.
- The company frames this not as replacing an expert but as "compounding" — extracting one person's tacit taste/skill into a tool that scales her judgment across the org so she can focus on higher-level work.
- Every doubled headcount (15 to 30) over a year despite aggressively automating, illustrating what Shipper calls an "AI paradox": AI is trained on the "residue" of already-solved problems, so applying it to novel situations produces plausible-looking but flawed output ("slop"), requiring more human experts to correct and productize it.
- Shipper argues this dynamic raises both the floor (more people can attempt expert-level tasks) and the ceiling (experts can attempt previously-impossible projects), increasing total work rather than eliminating it — engineering "moved up a level" rather than disappearing.
- On AI-assisted writing, Shipper distinguishes between narrative/personal writing (where authorship should remain human, regardless of how much AI assists) and informational content meant to be "AI-read" (where AI co-authorship is more acceptable).
- Shipper claims most professional writers already use AI in their process but hide it publicly — a stated "dirty secret" — and compares current stigma to historical resistance to typewriters and other new writing technologies.
- On dependency risk: building products on third-party frontier models creates constant risk of being obsoleted by the labs' own feature releases; Shipper sees durable value in being an independent, trusted evaluator ("arbiter") of model quality, since model makers themselves aren't seen as objective about their own products.
