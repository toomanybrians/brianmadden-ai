# Enough Debate about the AI Jobpocalypse. We Need To Plan for the Messy Middle.

## Insights

- Molly Kinder (ex-Brookings, now founding CEO of a new org on AI's jobs impact) frames a "messy middle" between two extremes: "reality one" (labor data shows minimal disruption today) and "reality three" (a distant post-AGI world of zero jobs/UBI). The messy middle is a bounded, nearer-term period of concentrated, painful job losses without economy-wide collapse.
- She argues the disruption pattern will invert COVID: instead of low-paid, in-person workers bearing the risk, it will be "laptop class" cognitive workers — those whose jobs can be done "locked in a closet with a computer" — who become vulnerable as AI commoditizes cognition.
- Cites a Deming/Summers historical chart showing occupational share shifts (agriculture → blue-collar → white-collar) and poses the open question of whether AI causes the "blue line" (professional/managerial work) to bend downward for the first time since computers began boosting knowledge work ~50 years ago.
- Identifies office/administrative/clerical/customer-service roles (15–18 million workers, disproportionately women without college degrees) as acutely exposed — a segment she calls the "Invisible Disruption," describing these jobs as a rare dignified, upwardly-mobile middle-class foothold for non-degree-holding women; cites a tech CEO privately predicting 60–70% of back-office staff gone in 2–3 years.
- Argues recent college graduates may be hit first, since AI targets entry-level "cut your teeth" tasks, creating a generational conundrum: no clear path to gain experience when employers have less incentive to pay for training, compounded by high student debt and no existing retraining infrastructure for the newly-skilled.
- Rejects "just become a plumber/electrician" as a solution — trades can't absorb the volume of displaced knowledge workers without collapsing their own wage premium (training takes ~4 years, scarcity is part of what makes those jobs good) — and separately rejects UBI/"universal high income" as politically and fiscally unrealistic given current aversion to taxing wealth and cutting social programs.
- Warns the knowledge-class disruption could produce a bigger political shock than deindustrialization: this population has status, voice, and access to political power, and its erosion undercuts the tax base and consumer spending that much of the broader economy depends on.

## Quote

> Imagine if this is a bit the face of what is to come. It's not every job in the economy, but it's some of the most coveted.
