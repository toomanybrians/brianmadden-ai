# Who Owns the Automated Future?

## Insights

- Frames current knowledge-work automation as a continuation of a decades-long pattern: manufacturing's share of employment peaked in the 1950s, then absolute manufacturing employment peaked in 1979 and declined — AI/robotics are now applying the same dynamic to service and white-collar work.
- Argues technology doesn't inherently "create new jobs" in a way that guarantees human participation — it creates new supply and demand, but humans have only historically (not necessarily permanently) been the mechanism connecting the two.
- Breaks household income into three channels — wages, government transfers, and capital returns — and argues that as wages weaken, the economy must shift toward much broader, distributed capital ownership rather than relying mainly on transfers.
- Distinguishes UBI (solves the income/aggregate-demand problem) from ownership (solves the power problem): UBI can be revoked by whoever controls the tap, whereas ownership confers legal/physical control and voting power over productive assets.
- Proposes concrete mechanisms for broadening ownership, e.g., sovereign/state wealth funds capitalized by companies paying a portion of taxes in capped equity stakes (5–10%) over time, giving the public both returns and governance voice.
- Positions this thinking as part of a "Post-Labor Economics" framework, with a forthcoming related concept called "Structural Realism."

## Quote

> UBI helps solve the income problem, but ownership helps solve the power problem.
