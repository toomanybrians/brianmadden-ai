# Monopoly Round-Up: Are Big AI Firms Cooking the Books?

## Insights

- Stoller argues the financial internals of major AI labs (Anthropic, OpenAI) are effectively unknowable to outsiders, since neither is public and big tech partners can bury AI revenue/costs via cross-subsidization within larger balance sheets.
- The collapse of Leopold Aschenbrenner's "Situational Awareness" hedge fund ($45B, leveraged, heavy in Anthropic and public AI stocks) is used as a case study in how a small, socially-networked cluster of AI insiders (EA-adjacent, tied to Anthropic/OpenAI figures) shapes market narratives about AI's trajectory.
- Specific unverifiable claims — like inference profit margins allegedly jumping from 38% to 70%+ — function as quasi-technical signals to investors, distinct from broader hype but arguably more consequential to capital allocation decisions.
- Because insiders alone can confirm or refute skeptics (e.g., Ed Zitron's "shell game" critique), the resulting information asymmetry distorts both investment decisions and policy debate, including how a Congressional candidate might think about AI antitrust policy.
- Stoller frames the current AI buildout as itself a product of permissive state policy (copyright/privacy carve-outs, liability shields, subsidized data centers), not a purely market-driven phenomenon.
- His proposed fix: mandate public financial disclosure for large AI firms, modeled on the 1933 Securities Act's disclosure regime, as a baseline governance tool given how unsettled and confusing current AI market structure is.
