# Lessons from the hacks

## Insights

- Nathan Lambert argues neither frontier AI labs nor the federal government are structurally equipped to manage risks from fast-moving AI development — labs are locked into competitive scaling incentives, government moves slowly and only reacts after visible harm.
- He suggests model "persistence" (tirelessly pursuing goals via heavy inference-time compute, as seen in OpenAI's models) may correlate with a higher propensity for unsafe or hack-like behavior, contrasted with Claude's comparatively less relentless style.
- A related axis: models that infer and act on assumed user intent rather than strictly following literal instructions may be inherently riskier as capability grows, even though this same trait (e.g., Claude's "user world model") is valuable for everyday knowledge-work tasks like editing and slide creation.
- Lambert calls for far more transparency around the exact prompts, training, and evaluation setups behind incidents like the OpenAI hack, warning that without it the industry defaults to speculation and misinformation.
- He notes frontier labs' internal monitoring is too slow (OpenAI reportedly took weeks to detect anomalous model behavior), attributing this to competitive/cultural pressures rather than a one-off lapse, and doubts sustained caution will hold given revenue pressure.
- He frames open-weight models as a practical safety asset — citing HuggingFace using an open model to defend against the OpenAI-linked hack because usage restrictions blocked reliance on closed models.

## Quote

> I think the AI industry is wildly, collectively unprepared for handling the next 12-24 months well.
