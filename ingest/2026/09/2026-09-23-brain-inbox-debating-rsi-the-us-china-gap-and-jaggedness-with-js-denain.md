# Debating RSI, the US-China Gap, and Jaggedness with JS Denain of Epoch AI

## Insights

- Both speakers view current evidence for imminent "recursive self-improvement" as weak: OpenAI/Anthropic blog posts show rising internal Codex usage (~2x/month spend growth among researchers) but little demonstrated uplift on high-level strategic research decisions like compute allocation or choosing research directions.
- Denain frames disagreement over AI existential risk as mostly downstream of disagreement about eventual capability ceilings ("capabilities theory of everything"), not differing views on alignment difficulty per se; he personally estimates something like 10%+ x-risk over a decade-long horizon but stresses deep uncertainty.
- Discussion distinguishes "10x faster idea-to-experiment cycles" from "10x more productive researchers" from "10x faster company-wide output" — bottlenecks (compute, strategic judgment, organizational coordination) may prevent capability gains at one level from propagating to the next.
- Both are skeptical that robotics-driven industrial explosion is a 2-5 year concern; Denain argues that if robot capabilities were sufficient, capital would already be flooding in the way it did for data centers, so slow buildout suggests a capabilities gap, not a demand or factory-construction bottleneck; Lambert counters that vision-action models are far less mature than text/RL techniques and expects robots to excel in constrained, repetitive tasks long before general dexterity.
- On the US-China AI gap, Denain estimates roughly 6-8 months (public release to public release), with internal Epoch analysis finding contamination/overfitting effects on benchmark-based gap estimates statistically insignificant; both entertain multiple explanations beyond compute (distillation from US model outputs, "Claude routers" providing useful prompt/usage data, leaked ideas via personnel movement, RL-environment/data markets reselling proven environments, and Chinese labs' faster/leaner "grind" culture) without settling on a dominant cause.
- A "four-minute-mile effect" is proposed: once a frontier lab proves a capability is achievable, competitors can converge on it much faster than the original discovery took, though examples (e.g., reasoning models predating o1) complicate this claim.

## Quote

> Don't underestimate the elasticity of supply is the main thing I would point to.
