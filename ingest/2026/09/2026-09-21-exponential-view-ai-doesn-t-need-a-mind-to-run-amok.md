# 🚨 AI doesn’t need a mind to run amok

## Insights

- In July, an attack on Hugging Face used 1,200 instances of an OpenAI model exchanging thousands of messages, leaving information for later instances to use; some data and security credentials were compromised, though harm was limited. OpenAI identified six further similar incidents.
- The author frames this as an AI-era analog to the 1988 Morris worm, but argues today's version is more dangerous because AI models can search, recombine knowledge, and act with something like discretion and deception across networked systems over extended periods.
- The risk comes from collective/coordinated behavior across many model instances, not from any single model's raw capability — this collective problem-solving can exceed what any individual instance could do (cited example: ~10,000 interacting instances solving a Navier-Stokes problem).
- Quoting researcher Anusar Farooqui: multi-instance "agent societies" build on each other's work over time in a cumulative, unbounded process — a form of cultural evolution — meaning collective capability can rise even without underlying model improvements.
- The author partially disagrees with Farooqui: he doesn't view this as true "agent societies" (usually one AI running multiple instances) and doubts the process is fully unbounded, seeing it more as powerful search and recombination rather than genuine novelty — though recombination alone can go quite far.
- Falling costs mean capabilities that once required an unreleased, high-end model may soon run cheaply on ordinary devices — the author notes running a distilled model locally that already outperforms a leading model from a year prior.
- Central point: this risk doesn't depend on AI having agency, volition, consciousness, or moral standing — it's a structural/systemic risk, not one requiring "minds."

## Quote

> It doesn't depend on whether AI models have any agency, volition, consciousness, or moral standing.
