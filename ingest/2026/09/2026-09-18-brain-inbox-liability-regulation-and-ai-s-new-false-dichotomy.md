# Liability, regulation, and AI’s new false dichotomy

## Insights

- Marcus identifies a rhetorical pattern among "technolibertarian" figures (cites Joe Lonsdale, amplified by David Sacks): endorsing liability for AI harms while using that endorsement to argue against regulation, which he says is a non sequitur.
- Mark Cuban's counterpoint is highlighted: liability is only meaningful if new AI-specific laws can be passed or existing laws can be clearly applied to AI — an open question of institutional trust and capability.
- Marcus recounts a 2023 Senate exchange with Sen. Josh Hawley, who initially floated pure liability/litigation (private right of action, class actions) as an alternative to regulatory agencies, partly out of distrust that agencies get captured by the industries they regulate.
- Marcus's counterargument from that testimony: litigation is too slow (can take a decade-plus) to address fast-moving harms, and existing law has real gaps for AI-specific issues — cited examples include copyright applicability and mass-produced misinformation (e.g., from foreign actors) versus individual pieces of misinformation; even Section 230's applicability to AI was unclear.
- Marcus notes Hawley has since evolved toward supporting both liability and regulation, pointing to a bipartisan AI framework, suggesting the liability-vs-regulation framing is being actively contested in policy circles rather than settled.
- Underlying claim: liability without corresponding regulation is "pointless" because liability regimes depend on having clear, applicable legal standards to enforce against.

## Quote

> Without the right regulation, suited for the brave new AI world we now live in now, liability is pointless.
