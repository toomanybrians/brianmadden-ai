# The Future Belongs to the Dilemma's Innovator and Companies Willing to Disrupt Themselves

## Insights

- Solis distinguishes iteration (doing the same things better), innovation (new things creating new value), and disruption (new things at scale that obsolete the old) — and argues most current AI strategies are iteration mislabeled as transformation.
- Most enterprise AI use today automates existing tasks (emails, summaries, code, monitoring, compliance reporting), which yields efficiency but not new value, leaving organizations vulnerable to competitors who use AI to build what wasn't possible before.
- Companies get disrupted not from lacking technology but from lacking "permission" to become something new; that permission comes from defining identity around purpose (what you stand for) rather than product category — illustrated via Nintendo, Michelin, Lego, Netflix, Amazon, Apple, Tesla.
- Cites OpenAI's "capability overhang" concept and Microsoft's Work Trend Index (only 1-in-4 AI users say leadership is clearly aligned on AI) as evidence of a widening internal AI-capability divide that becomes a leadership problem before it becomes a market problem.
- Proposes a "Chief Workflow Officer" role (developed with ServiceNow's Dave Wright) as a missing leadership function needed to redesign workflows end-to-end across silos rather than sprinkling AI onto isolated tasks.
- Frames a four-stage AI evolution — generative (creation), agentic (coordination/action), physical (embedded intelligence in machines/environments), autonomous (bounded action with reduced human oversight) — arguing each stage just scales old processes unless workflows are reimagined first.
- Argues human qualities (empathy, judgment, critical thinking, psychological safety to be wrong) become more, not less, important as AI capability increases, citing Google's team-performance research and warning against outsourcing judgment to confident-sounding AI.

> If you define yourself by what you make, you inherit the limits of what you made.
