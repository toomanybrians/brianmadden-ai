# The Shape of What Isn’t There

## Insights

- Fields Medalist Terence Tao (with other mathematicians) issued a statement warning of "severe misalignment" of AI in mathematics, arguing that AI tools risk skipping the messy process—wrong turns, flawed models, social dialogue—where real mathematical insight is generated.
- Tao's "hike vs. helicopter" metaphor: AI can fly you directly to a solution (a "waterfall"), but you learn nothing about the terrain, miss incidental discoveries, and don't build the shared maps/understanding a research community needs.
- Kepler's decades of flawed theorizing (nesting Platonic solids in planetary orbits) before arriving at elliptical orbits is cited as an example of "productive failure" that AI shortcuts would bypass entirely.
- Argument distinguishes AI "using" negative space (contrastive learning, negative prompts, attention masks, dropout — treating absence as computable structure) from "having" negative space (absence being felt/meaningful for a conscious subject) — AI does the former well, not the latter.
- Claim: AI has an unknowable "null space" — training data never encountered, concepts flattened during compression, context masked by architecture — but unlike a mathematical null space, this blind spot is invisible to the system itself, unlike a human's felt sense of absence or ignorance.
- Speculative extension: AI's null space could shrink if people document lived, messy journeys (drafts, wrong turns) rather than only polished/finished outputs, giving AI "vocabulary" for such experience — though the piece argues vocabulary is not the same as lived experience.
- Applied to knowledge work broadly: when AI generates fluent commentary/analysis, it may be substituting "having" (retrievable content) for genuine encounter with what a text or problem withholds — a distinction relevant to how AI-assisted work differs from human sense-making.

## Quote

> These AI tools, they can be like helicopters that will just fly you directly to this waterfall... but you learn nothing about how to get there. — Terence Tao
