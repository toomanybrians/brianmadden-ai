# A Philosopher’s Guide to AI Welfare

## Insights

- Researchers propose consciousness, sentience, and agency may not co-occur in AI the way they do in biological organisms — a system could have high agency without sentience, or vice versa, requiring each property to be assessed separately rather than assumed to travel together.
- Three evidence types for AI consciousness are proposed — behavioral, internal (architecture/interpretability), and developmental (training data/process) — each with a characteristic flaw: behavioral evidence can be "gamed" or mimicked, internal evidence risks misapplying biology-derived concepts (e.g., "global workspace"), and developmental evidence is undermined by AI's different constraints (e.g., scaling) versus biological evolution.
- Sentience is proposed to plausibly require both consciousness and evaluative signals (e.g., from reinforcement learning) interacting in the right way — meaning an AI could have RL-based evaluative signals without being sentient, or be sentient through non-RL mechanisms.
- Agency is broken into three levels — minimal (goal pursuit via environment interaction), intentional (beliefs/desires/means-end reasoning), and rational (reflective evaluation of those beliefs/desires) — and is easier to study functionally than consciousness but harder to justify morally, since agency without subjective experience may or may not matter ethically.
- The "correct" unit of moral analysis for AI is unresolved: candidates include the full model (like a species), a persistent "model-persona," a single conversational instance, an "instance-persona," or a single forward pass (a token-generation event, potentially analogous to a momentary "experience") — the piece argues for treating multiple levels as relevant simultaneously rather than picking one.
- The field is described as shifting from treating AI moral status as a "negligible but nonzero" future possibility to something requiring active assessment and policy response now, given the pace of AI development.

## Quote

> Deciding whether AI systems merit moral consideration might thus require reasoning under both empirical and normative uncertainty.
