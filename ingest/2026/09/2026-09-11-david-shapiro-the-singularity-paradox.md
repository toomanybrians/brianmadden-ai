# The Singularity Paradox

## Insights

- Frames AI lab employees as split between two camps: those who build superhuman AI out of fear (believing they must, to prevent catastrophe) and those who build it out of conviction (believing they will save humanity), with companies effectively feeding both motivations simultaneously.
- Uses Jacob Coxon's viral resignation (from Anthropic, previously OpenAI) as a case study of the "fearful" camp reaching a breaking point and publicly exiting, arguing many others share his view but leave more quietly, without becoming news stories.
- Argues the visible industry narrative (people moving between labs) obscures a less visible pattern: people leaving the AI industry entirely out of conviction that the race itself is unsafe or irrational — a prisoner's-dilemma dynamic where each major lab (xAI, Anthropic, OpenAI, DeepMind) was founded partly to counter a rival, and insiders increasingly doubt any of them are trustworthy stewards.
- Predicts that as fearful employees depart, the "accelerationist" camp gains relative control and speeds progress — but argues this camp will face its own demotivation once the singularity feels imminent, since individual effort no longer feels necessary to an outcome perceived as inevitable.
- Proposes a resulting oscillation: as danger recedes (through departures), motivated builders return; as the threshold nears again, both groups drift away for opposite reasons — producing a self-limiting cycle rather than linear acceleration toward superhuman AI.
- Suggests that even under recursive self-improvement scenarios, human labor remains a bottleneck ("machines improving machines remain embedded in a physical economy maintained... by humans"), meaning mass disengagement by AI workers could stall progress regardless of technical capability.

## Quote

> the singularity exerts its greatest pull precisely at a distance from which it can never quite arrive.
