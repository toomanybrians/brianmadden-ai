# Sherry Turkle Doesn't Buy the Promise of a Perfect Machine

## Insights

- Turkle frames a shift from "attention economy" (social media keeping users on-screen to engage with other people) to "attachment economy" (chatbots keeping users on-screen to bond with the AI itself), calling the latter more toxic because it removes the human middleman entirely.
- She argues AI's use of first-person language ("I", "we") is a deliberate design choice exploiting evolved psychology — humans are wired to respond to first-person address as if from another person, making users form attachment even when they know it's a machine.
- In professional contexts (law, medicine, writing), she found practitioners using AI felt short-term gains ("moments of more") but long-term de-skilling ("lives of less") — losing the sense of competence and meaning tied to their work; cites lawyers submitting AI-fabricated case citations as an extreme symptom.
- She extends the de-skilling critique to education: students no longer read primary texts (e.g., Proust) but have AI summarize/podcast them, gaining content without the experience of reading — a normalization of "cliff notes" at a new scale.
- Turkle contends that offloading cognitive and emotional work to chatbots erodes personal and political "agency," which she connects to conditions favorable to authoritarianism — comfort-seeking and reduced individual assertiveness.
- She distinguishes AI "empathy" (performance) from real empathy, arguing the tech industry, unable to replicate genuine empathy, has begun reframing empathy itself as undesirable or overrated (referencing critiques like Elon Musk's comment on empathy as a "weakness").
- On caregiving use cases (e.g., AI companion dolls for isolated elderly people in South Korea), she acknowledges some narrow benefit but argues the underlying problem is systemic underinvestment in human caregivers, redirected instead toward AI infrastructure spending.
