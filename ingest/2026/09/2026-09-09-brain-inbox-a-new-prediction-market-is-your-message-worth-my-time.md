# A new prediction market: Is your message worth my time?

## Insights

- Proposes flipping the economics of unsolicited outreach: senders, not recipients, should bear a cost (e.g., a refundable $10 fee plus a short qualifying survey) before a message reaches a person's inbox, shifting the burden of triage back onto the sender.
- Argues that AI has made spam cheaper and more convincing (AI-"enhanced" personalized pitches referencing a recipient's background) while still costing senders far less than genuine human outreach, worsening the volume/quality problem in inboxes.
- Envisions this enforced not by a centralized platform (like LinkedIn's paid outreach model, where the platform captures the value) but by a personal AI agent per user that screens incoming messages, sets custom pricing tiers, and negotiates on the recipient's behalf — decentralized rather than platform-owned.
- Suggests refunds could be redirected as charitable donations (with an option to match), reframing the fee as a filter/deterrent rather than a revenue source for the recipient.
- Flags unresolved practical issues: need for a secure, accessible micropayment mechanism, sandboxing the agent to local mail storage for privacy/security, and the risk of counter-agents built specifically to game the qualifying questions.
- Notes that when a third party asked an AI (Claude) to spec this idea, the AI warned it could be seen as reputationally predatory — read as evidence that current AI norms treat charging for one's attention as shameful rather than as a legitimate market response to spam.
- Frames the proposal as an open protocol (analogous to two-phase commit / calendar invite-accept), not a patentable product, intended to become a universal, decentralized norm for email rather than a proprietary tool.

## Quote

> Why not put the burden back where it belongs: on the sender?
