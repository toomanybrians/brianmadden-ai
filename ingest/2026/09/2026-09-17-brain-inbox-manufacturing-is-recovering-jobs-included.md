# Manufacturing is recovering, jobs included

## Insights

- US manufacturing output has shifted from two decades of stagnation (roughly flat since 2000) to sustained ~4% annualized growth in 2026, the strongest since 2018 excluding the COVID rebound; employment gains only started this year, adding 58,000 jobs after two years of decline.
- The recovery is concentrated almost entirely in "advanced manufacturing" (chemicals/pharma, machinery, computers/electronics, transportation equipment) — R&D-heavy subsectors up 3%+ in output over the past year — while the rest of manufacturing (apparel, furniture, textiles, paper, etc.) remains flat or shrinking despite tariff protection.
- Advanced manufacturing jobs pay significantly more: $43.54/hour average vs. $32.92 in the rest of the sector (and $37.75 private-sector average) — meaning the manufacturing "pay premium" is now really an advanced-manufacturing premium, driven partly by a shift toward engineering/technical occupational mix.
- Job growth is expected to stay modest permanently because these are the most capital-intensive factories in the economy; semiconductor output grew 15%/year for two years while semiconductor payrolls actually fell, illustrating how automation decouples output growth from headcount growth.
- Much of the buildout's job creation shows up in construction (87,000 jobs added this year building plants/data centers/substations) rather than on factory floors.
- A new wave of AI-driven and humanoid robotics is just entering factory pilots (following a two-year slump in robot installations), which the author expects will further thin production-floor jobs while skewing remaining roles toward better-paid engineers and technicians.

## Quote

> The sectors adding jobs pay a third more than the sectors losing them.
