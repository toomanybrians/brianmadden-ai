# When will average people feel AI’s impact?

## Insights

- Unlike past industrial revolutions (cheaper clothing, sewing machines, indoor plumbing, electrification), AI has produced no tangible, physical, universally-felt goods for average people yet — its benefits (search, image generation) are marginal, while its negative associations (addictive chatbots, data centers) are more visceral.
- AI's biggest potential wins (scientific breakthroughs, disease cures, economic abundance) are too indirect for average people to credit to AI companies — someone cured by a "miracle drug" won't attribute it to OpenAI or Anthropic.
- Author argues AI is currently a tool serving mainly knowledge workers (~half the U.S. economy), creating a destabilizing split where tech booms while everyday life for much of society stagnates — drawing a parallel to "Engels' pause" (1790-1840), when UK wages stagnated despite rapid GDP growth from industrialization.
- Predicts headcount in tech/knowledge-work sectors will likely shrink even as output explodes, further tarnishing AI's image as a "collective good" since gains concentrate in already-successful sectors.
- Warns that political backlash (fueled by distrust carried over from Big Tech generally, plus AI's own "doom" and mass-unemployment narratives) could stunt AI's development the way public backlash curtailed U.S. nuclear power.
- Frames current moment as early infrastructure-building in a 50-year diffusion process; suggests robotics/self-driving may eventually be the thing that makes AI's benefits tangible to average people, ironically overshadowing pure-LLM progress.
- Full deep integration of AI into businesses and daily life (as agents/assistants) will take far longer to diffuse than headline products like ChatGPT, and is the "true marker" of AI's real evolution.

## Quote

> Today's AI is primarily a tool to serve the elite.
