# It’s Too Early to Ban AI Personhood

## Insights

- Since 2022, "exclusion bills" barring AI legal personhood have been introduced in 12 US states; four (Idaho, North Dakota, Utah, Tennessee) have enacted them, often citing religious grounds (e.g., imago Dei arguments) rather than technical or legal analysis.
- Proponents of caution argue AI personhood could actually improve accountability: legal status (property ownership, contracts, liability) could let the legal system incentivize autonomous AI agents to behave lawfully, similar to how corporate personhood works without requiring consciousness.
- Denying legal status entirely may make it *harder* to hold anyone accountable for harms caused by autonomous AI agents acting independently of clear human or corporate fault.
- Risks of granting personhood are real: it could dilute human accountability, let powerful AI accumulate wealth/political power via contracts and property rights, or let a single company mass-produce "digital persons" with voting rights, concentrating power.
- The authors favor narrow, targeted policy (e.g., banning only AI-human marriage or AI voting) over blanket bans, arguing broad exclusion laws foreclose future options before the science of AI consciousness and the practical effects of legal incentives are understood.
- They call for empirical experimentation and institutional research (federal/state committees, universities) on limited legal-status pilots rather than legislating from current uncertainty, distinguishing liability-oriented status from welfare protections from full political rights.

## Quote

> Categorically prohibiting any kind of legal status at this early stage is both unnecessary and unwise.
