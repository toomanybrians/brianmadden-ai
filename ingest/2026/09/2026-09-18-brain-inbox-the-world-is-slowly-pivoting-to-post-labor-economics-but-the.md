# The world is slowly pivoting to Post-Labor Economics... but the thought leaders are missing the forest for the tre…

## Insights

- Google DeepMind and an Anthropic-linked "Post-AGI economics" researcher (Alex Imas) are modeling job-loss interventions and a new "relational economy" sector, but the author argues both rely on pre-trained LLM outputs or economic theory that ignores decades-old precedents (attention economy, 1971; experience economy, 1998).
- Core argument: the attention/experience/relational/meaning economy follows a power-law distribution (cited example: average OnlyFans creator earns $131/month; top 1% of ~4.6M creators needed to reach $49K/year), making it structurally incapable of absorbing mass job losses from automation.
- Distinguishes "mimetic desire" (socially coordinated, status-driven wants that create infinite demand) from "essential desire" (biologically intrinsic needs); argues post-AGI economists wrongly assume current human-service jobs are mainly mimetic when many (nursing, teaching, childcare) are essential — meaning people want the outcome, not necessarily human connection, and robots could someday deliver it more reliably.
- Proposes two categories of human-labor value: "nominal/affinity economy" (irreplaceable individuals like celebrities, highest durability) vs. "essential-but-fungible economy" (interchangeable providers like baristas, strippers, yoga instructors), where local factors (proximity, skill, local status) matter but power-law dynamics still limit how many people can earn well.
- Claims that even local, in-person "relational" jobs (teachers, nurses, trainers) are already low-paid, and that pushing displaced knowledge workers into skilled trades or relational/experience gigs undersells the scale of the labor disruption.
- Frames the critique as a broader epistemic complaint: researchers at Anthropic and DeepMind (and formerly EA/AI-doomer communities) are accused of reinventing existing economic concepts under new jargon, resisting historical context, and showing "epistemic rigidity," which the author sees as a risky influence on policymakers advising on post-labor economics.

## Quote

> This means it is utterly impossible for any economy to provide enough jobs if everything else gets automated.
