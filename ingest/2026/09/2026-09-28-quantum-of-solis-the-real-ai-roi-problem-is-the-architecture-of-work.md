# The Real AI ROI Problem Is the Architecture of Work

## Insights

- AI infrastructure investment is projected at $10.3 trillion (2025–2032), ~3.6% of GDP annually — larger than historical build-outs of highways, electrification, or railroads, with five hyperscalers alone spending ~$4.2 trillion through 2029.
- Citing Chamath Palihapitiya's analysis: median AI spend is ~$12.50/employee/month against ~$8,500 average monthly cost, meaning AI needs to generate only ~0.15% productivity gain (roughly three minutes/week) to pay for itself — yet aggregate productivity gains remain hard to detect.
- Task-level productivity gains (e.g., an analysis done in 20 minutes instead of two hours) often evaporate at the workflow level because downstream steps — approvals, handoffs, weekly review cycles — remain unredesigned; the piece distinguishes task-level, workflow-level, and enterprise-level productivity as related but not interchangeable.
- References a 2026 experiment with 515 startups given identical AI tools: a subgroup also shown examples of how firms reorganized work around AI generated 1.9x the revenue of the control group overall, with gains concentrated among top performers — suggesting AI amplifies organizational capability differences rather than being differentiating on its own.
- Argues the real bottleneck isn't human productivity but "organizational architecture" — org charts, functional apps, departmental budgets, and approval layers built around old assumptions (scarce intelligence, concentrated at the top) — and proposes shifting from "Org Chart" to "Work Chart," organizing people, agents, systems, and decision rights around outcomes (e.g., lead-to-cash, idea-to-product) rather than departments.
- Proposes a "Return on Intelligence" (ROI∞) formula as a complement to Chamath's AI ROI formula: ROI∞ = (Realized Value × Learning Velocity) ÷ (Time-to-Outcome × Resistance to Change), framing AI value as organizational speed of sensing/deciding/acting/learning, not just task efficiency.

## Quote

> Automation applied to an efficient operation magnifies efficiency, while automation applied to an inefficient operation magnifies inefficiency.
