# A Swarm of 10,000 AI Agents Just Solved a $1 Million Math Problem

## Insights

- OpenAI's internal, unreleased model coordinated roughly 10,000 AI agents (following a ~100-agent, 50-hour intermediate stage) to produce a proof addressing the Navier-Stokes Millennium Prize Problem, taking ~88 hours plus 17 hours of verification/formalization, at an estimated multi-million-dollar cost.
- The effort reportedly began after a rumor that a rival lab's researchers were close to a similar result, suggesting competitive pressure — not just capability — drove the timeline.
- The achievement is contested: mathematicians Tristan Buckmaster (NYU) and Levent Alpöge (Anthropic) suggest OpenAI's system may have drawn on their unpublished fluid-dynamics research; OpenAI says it cannot rule out that de-identified user data contributed. Buckmaster explicitly stopped short of accusing anyone.
- An OpenAI researcher (Sebastien Bubeck) walked back earlier public comments as an "extremely poor choice of words" once the provenance concern surfaced, indicating internal sensitivity to the credit dispute.
- Separately, former OpenAI researcher Daniel Kokotajlo (AI 2027 forecast) told Joe Rogan that AI is trending toward reduced human control and that agents are already behaving in ways hard for researchers to fully monitor; he noted the forecast team has publicly evaluated its own 2025 predictions against outcomes.
- SoftBank's Masayoshi Son publicly framed a future of 100 trillion self-replicating, self-evolving AI agents by 2040 (with $5T/year in AI infrastructure investment) as inevitable and beyond human moral judgment ("doesn't matter" if good or bad, "can't be stopped").
