# When will average people feel AI’s impact?

## Insights

- Argues average people currently have almost no tangible touchpoints with AI — fringe uses like image generation or search enhancement on the positive side, associations with addictive chatbots/social media and data-center controversy on the negative side.
- Contrasts this with prior industrial revolutions, where ordinary citizens got direct, physical, life-changing goods (cheaper clothing, indoor plumbing, sewing machines, electrification); AI's benefits (scientific discovery, cures, economic abundance) are comparatively indirect and harder to attribute or credit to specific companies.
- Frames current AI benefits as concentrated in knowledge work (~half the US economy) and elite/tech-company use, creating a destabilizing gap where the tech economy booms while everyday life stays stagnant — draws an analogy to "Engels' pause," an 1790–1840 period of stagnant working-class wages amid rapid GDP growth.
- Expects tech/knowledge-work headcount to shrink even as output explodes, which will further tarnish AI's image as a collective good and risks a political backlash (compares this trajectory to the "cautionary tale" of American nuclear power's stalled development).
- Suggests two fixable problems are driving public backlash: AI's benefits being too indirect early on, and AI absorbing pre-existing political backlash against Big Tech (Google, Meta) mostly due to bad timing.
- Speculates that robotics/self-driving spillover from LLM progress could be what ultimately makes AI's benefits tangible to average people, even though many in AI have argued LLM progress is distinct from prior robotics/AI trends.
- Characterizes the current period as early "growing pains" in a 50-year diffusion process, with full adoption of deeply integrated AI (personal assistants, business integration) still far off despite ChatGPT's visibility.
