# I distilled myself, and you should too

## Insights

- The author built a "skill" (published on GitHub) that continuously distills his own public writing, talks, and workflows, and set up automation to keep it updated — designed so others can query it to "summon" him, ask questions, and reuse his workflows.
- Motivation: his own agents were bottlenecked on his personal judgment, constantly escalating decisions or making choices he'd have to correct; distilling his judgment into a reusable artifact aims to reduce that bottleneck and free his time.
- He frames distillation as inherently partial — only the markdown-expressible parts of a person can be captured, and argues this codifiable layer was never the real competitive moat anyway. The moat, in his view, is in how one acquires knowledge, derives new insight, and builds trust with people.
- He released a one-click importable bot tool so anyone can distill themselves or other public figures, and demonstrated it by building bots of Sam Altman, Elon Musk, Dario Amodei, and Mark Zuckerberg from public statements, then having them "debate" AI dynamics in a simulated multi-agent conversation.
- The simulated debate (explicitly a constructed exercise, not real statements) converges on a consensus that no single company or government should control access to advanced AI, that dangerous capability shouldn't be released unrestricted, and that safety/monitoring bars should gate deployment of more powerful systems — while leaving the mechanism (open weights vs. API access) unresolved.
- Closing framing: self-distillation is presented as a way to scale oneself and improve agent alignment/outcomes, with the repeatable/codifiable part of a person treated as low-value work worth delegating.

## Quote

> My moat cannot be just a bunch of markdowns that haven't been written down. That's not defensible.
