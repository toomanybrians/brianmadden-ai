# We don’t have the nouns yet

## Insights

- The argument centers on a naming problem: future jobs created by new technologies are often unimaginable in advance because the vocabulary to describe them doesn't yet exist — cybersecurity, a field employing 1.25M Americans today, barely appeared in text before the 1970s.
- Cites Autor et al. finding that the majority of U.S. employment in 2018 existed in job categories absent from the 1940 Census — evidence that job-category churn over decades is the norm, not the exception.
- Frames technological change through Stuart Kauffman's "adjacent possible": each invention enables further inventions and business models in a chain (rocketry → satellites → GPS → navigation/rideshare apps → new maintenance/design jobs), making long-range prediction of derivative work nearly impossible.
- Notes current AI-related hiring is concentrated in the most describable categories: LinkedIn data shows 1.3M new AI-related jobs globally (2023–2025) including data annotators and forward-deployed engineers; Lightcast found generative AI skill requests outside IT grew 9x from 2022–2024; cites a T-Mobile "AI Cost Management Analyst" role as an example of adapted existing roles.
- Cybersecurity's growth pattern suggests a possible transition path for workers: a 2025 survey found 36% of cybersecurity professionals moved into the field after first taking on security responsibilities within an existing IT job — suggesting adjacent-task accumulation as a route into new fields.
- Explicitly cautions against uncritical optimism: past technological transitions produced real job loss, lower earnings, and difficult occupational moves, and new opportunities don't necessarily emerge in the same places or for the same displaced workers.
