# AI Agents Are Ready for Work, the Real Question Is Whether We Are

## Insights

- Wharton's "Blueprint for AI Agent Adoption" frames agent adoption around three psychological frictions: perceived competence (can it do the job?), trust (can I trust it?), and delegation of control (am I still in control?) — capability alone doesn't drive adoption.
- Research cited suggests users prioritize competence over warmth/empathy in AI agents; people want problems solved, not performative helpfulness.
- Counterintuitively, trust increases when agents disclose their limitations rather than hide them — cited findings include a 22.1% increase in belief in future accuracy from seeing past successful outcomes, a 55% vs. 43% preference for AI labeled as "learning," and 12% higher trust in precise vs. rounded numeric recommendations.
- When AI does too much work upfront with no user input, people report less engagement and weaker psychological ownership of the outcome — full automation can undermine rather than build trust.
- Autonomy has a "moderate" sweet spot: too little feels like added work, too much feels like loss of control; cited stats: control concerns drove 26% of adoption decisions, privacy concerns 31%, personalization could lift adoption up to 20%, and reliance on AI recommendations rose from 39% to 48% under higher pressure.
- The piece argues organizations should design/manage agents like employees (job descriptions, permissions, escalation paths, performance metrics) and treat delegation as an explicit, deliberate discipline rather than an implicit default.
- Frames the deeper leadership shift as moving from "human-in-the-loop" to "human-above-the-loop," and from automation-focused questions (cut costs, speed tasks) to reinvention-focused questions (what work should no longer exist, what new outcomes are achievable).

## Quote

> Giving up tasks is not the issue; employees do not want to lose their identity or give up agency.
