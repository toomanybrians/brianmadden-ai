# AI existential risk probabilities are (still) too unreliable to inform policy

## Insights

- AI existential-risk probability estimates lack any of the three valid bases for forecasting (inductive, deductive, or track-record-validated subjective judgment): there's no reference class comparable to human extinction from AI, no theoretical model of the underlying process, and no way to measure forecaster skill on rare, unique tail events.
- In the Existential Risk Persuasion Tournament, AI experts' median extinction-risk-by-2100 estimate (3%) was roughly 8x higher than forecasting experts'/superforecasters' median (0.38%), with ranges differing by 100x or more — despite months of structured debate among credentialed participants.
- Scoring rules used to evaluate forecaster skill are structurally insensitive to overestimation of rare tail risks (it would take on the order of a trillion events under Brier scoring to statistically detect systematic overestimation), while underestimation is punished severely — creating an asymmetric incentive for forecasters to skew estimates high.
- Multiple plausible selection-bias mechanisms push both AI-researcher and forecasting communities toward inflated x-risk estimates: AI researchers self-select into believing AI is world-altering, and forecasters with pre-existing x-risk concerns are more likely to enter the forecasting/EA-adjacent community in the first place.
- Utility-maximization framing (a la Pascal's Wager) can make even small probabilities of extinction dominate policy reasoning, which the piece argues is precisely why it's dangerous to treat unvalidated probability estimates as inputs to costly, freedom-restricting policy like restricting open AI model releases.
- The author distinguishes x-risk forecasting from milestone/capability forecasting (e.g., benchmark performance, economic impact), which he considers more tractable and legitimate for policy use, since those events aren't unique/rare and forecaster skill can actually be measured against outcomes.

## Quote

> The less actual evidence there is to draw upon, the more the risk of groupthink.
