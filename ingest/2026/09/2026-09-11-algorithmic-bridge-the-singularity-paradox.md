# The Singularity Paradox

## Insights

- Frames AI lab employees as split into two motivational camps: those who stay to prevent catastrophe (believing their presence is the only thing stopping doom) and those who stay because they want to build superhuman AI and believe it will be salvific.
- Uses Jacob Coxon's viral resignation from Anthropic as a case study of the "safety-motivated" camp reaching a breaking point and publicly stating that people building AI believe it could kill everyone by the end of the decade.
- Argues that departures like Coxon's are common but under-reported, since media favors "lab-hopping" stories (e.g., Sutskever, Murati, Karpathy) over people who leave the AI industry entirely out of despair or disillusionment.
- Characterizes the current AI-lab landscape as a prisoner's-dilemma race (xAI vs. Anthropic vs. OpenAI vs. DeepMind) where each company was founded distrustful of the others, and insiders increasingly recognize this dynamic as absurd rather than principled.
- Proposes a theory that as safety-minded staff exit, labs accelerate unchecked toward "the singularity," but proximity to that threshold then demotivates the accelerationist camp too (their urgency dissolves once the outcome feels foregone), producing a self-limiting oscillation rather than a runaway trajectory.
- Concludes that progress toward superhuman AI can never fully arrive because it only motivates people at a specific psychological "distance" — close enough to matter, far enough to still require effort — creating a perpetual near-approach rather than actual arrival.

## Quote

> The people building AI earnestly believe that it could kill us all by the end of the decade.
