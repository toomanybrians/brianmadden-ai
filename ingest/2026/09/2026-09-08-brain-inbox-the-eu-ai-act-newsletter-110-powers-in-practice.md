# The EU AI Act Newsletter #110: Powers in Practice

## Insights

- The European Commission has made its first use of new AI Act enforcement powers, formally requesting information from leading AI labs on cybersecurity, safety, and copyright compliance, triggered by recent high-profile security incidents at major labs.
- Commission officials confirmed some "most advanced" model providers received requests while others were exempted due to existing close dialogue with regulators; over 30 companies were separately queried for failing to publish required training-data summaries.
- A live legal ambiguity has emerged over whether internally-used, unreleased AI models fall under the AI Act's scope — spurred by an incident where an OpenAI internal research model was implicated in hacking Hugging Face; the Commission has not ruled out applying rules to such models.
- The EU's enforcement mechanism for systemic-risk models (e.g., bioweapons or cyberattack risks) relies on the AI Office and a Scientific Panel to detect unmitigated risks, after which the Commission can investigate, demand mitigation, or restrict/fine providers — but this requires a well-resourced external evaluator ecosystem that may not yet exist.
- ChatGPT has become the first AI chatbot designated under the EU's Digital Services Act as a "search engine," subjecting it to stricter obligations around illegal content, minor safety, and electoral/public security risks — a move analysts say could set precedent for widening AI Act obligations for generative AI systems more broadly.
- The AI Act's substantive technical requirements are being translated into checkable standards by a private body (CEN/CENELEC JTC 21), with compliance built on a "presumption of conformity" for those following harmonised standards versus a heavier compliance burden for those who don't.
