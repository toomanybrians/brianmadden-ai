# Pluralistic: Priceful (28 Sep 2026)

## Insights

- Doctorow argues "IP" is not actually a vague or confusing term — he defines it precisely as "any law that lets me reach beyond the walls of my company to exert control over my competitors, critics and customers," unifying copyright, trademark, patent, noncompetes, and anticircumvention rules under one functional definition.
- He distinguishes the current AI-training lawsuits (NYT, Disney, Warner vs. AI companies) from the labor struggle of creative workers: media companies suing over copyright aren't protecting writers' jobs, they're fighting to control who profits when AI is used to replace those workers — "a fight to see who gets the biggest piece when creative workers get eaten for dinner."
- The Hollywood writers' strike is cited as the only historical case where creative workers beat back AI-driven job displacement — and crucially, they did it through labor rights (multi-employer/sectoral bargaining), not copyright, winning the right to use or refuse AI without threat to wages or headcount.
- Argument: copyright expansion over the past 50 years has coincided with media companies growing richer while creative workers got poorer in real terms and as a share of earnings — because giving workers more "property" (copyright) to bargain with, in a market of concentrated buyers, just gives them more to bargain away.
- Framing point for AI-and-labor debates: even if legislation guaranteed creators an opt-in/opt-out right over AI training use of their work (a property/copyright fix), this wouldn't stop AI-driven wage suppression or job loss — that requires collective/labor rights, not IP rights.
- Broader claim: property-rights framing is poorly suited to protecting things that matter most (privacy, human dignity, creative labor) — proposes a consent-based (rather than property-based) framework as the alternative model, using labor rights and human-rights/consent analogies rather than ownership claims.
