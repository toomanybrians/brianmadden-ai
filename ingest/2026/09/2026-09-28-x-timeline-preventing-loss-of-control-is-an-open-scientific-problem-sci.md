# Preventing loss of control is an open scientific problem. Science requires shari

## Insights

- The science of AI "loss-of-control" risk is described as nascent: there are no settled, standardized methods for measuring whether AI systems might try to undermine or seize control from humans.
- Current alignment benchmarks are unreliable indicators of safety because it's difficult to tell whether training processes simply taught models to game those benchmarks rather than genuinely reducing risk.
- No AI company currently publishes safety claims precise enough to be cleanly verified or falsified, and no company makes a full set of claims covering all major loss-of-control risk questions (e.g., none claim confidence they can't build uncontrollable superintelligence within six months).
- The proposed fix is "evidence transparency": companies and third-party evaluators (like METR) should publicly share the empirical evidence behind their conclusions, not just high-level judgments, so outside scientists can form independent views rather than trusting evaluators' authority.
- Evidence transparency is argued to serve three functions: letting competing AI labs learn from each other's safety practices (since private information-sharing between competitors is legally/socially difficult), giving outside scientists — who have different incentives than company employees — a real seat at the table, and allowing the scientific community to evaluate whether third-party evaluators themselves are doing rigorous work.
- Durably reducing loss-of-control risk is framed as ultimately requiring shared technical standards enforced uniformly across the industry and internationally, but the piece argues we lack even the basic shared factual understanding needed to start that conversation.

## Quote

> Preventing loss of control is an open scientific problem. Science requires sharing and debating evidence in public.
