# Should we encourage AI use in writing-intensive classes?

## Insights

- Deming (Harvard College dean) proposes a "barbell" AI policy: ban AI where enforceable via proctored, pen-and-paper "blue book" exams, and openly encourage AI use where take-home writing assignments can't realistically be policed.
- He argues ambiguous middle-ground policies ("AI for brainstorming but not drafting") are unenforceable and unfair — they punish honest students while those willing to cheat quietly gain an edge, splitting classes in exactly the wrong way.
- AI detection tools (e.g., Pangram) have a structural limit: they catch AI-generated prose but can't catch AI-generated *thinking* later rephrased in a student's own words — demonstrated by an experiment where an AI-drafted argument, human-rewritten, scored 100% human despite containing none of the writer's original ideas.
- Proposed alternative assessment design: require an "AI-proof" first draft under observed conditions, then let students iterate with both teacher and AI feedback — preserving independent thinking while still allowing AI-assisted refinement.
- Cites a Carnegie Mellon study finding student writing improved similarly from AI feedback and peer feedback (with repeated AI feedback outperforming both) — but only when students completed an unassisted first draft before receiving AI input.
- Frames the core purpose of writing pedagogy as developing thinking, not producing text, and argues writing instruction will matter more, not less, as AI becomes ubiquitous.

## Quote

> Punishing the conscientious and rewarding the willing to chance it is a bad policy, whatever its intentions.
