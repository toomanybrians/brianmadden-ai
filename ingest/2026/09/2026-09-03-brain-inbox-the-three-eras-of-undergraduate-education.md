# The Three Eras of Undergraduate Education

## Insights

- Deming frames undergraduate education in three eras: (1) scarce-knowledge custodian (Harvard's founding library/printing press monopoly), (2) curator of specialized expertise (Eliot's elective system, Lowell's concentrations, 20th-century specialization), and (3) an emerging era where AI commodifies expertise itself.
- Cites research (Harvard physics tutor study led by Greg Kestin; a Taiwan study) showing AI tutors that personalize and sequence problems based on prior student work more than double learning gains and increase engagement — a stronger effect than MOOCs ever achieved, because MOOC content wasn't personalized.
- Argues MOOCs (Coursera, edX) failed to disrupt higher ed despite huge hype in 2012 because they lacked personalization and because universities do more than transmit knowledge — they curate expertise, which AI can now also do.
- Proposes a "defense" response to AI: shift assessment back toward supervised, pen-and-paper exams and in-class quizzes since take-home work and unenforceable AI bans create unsustainable dilemmas for honest students; favors "AI encouragement" over detection for papers, with accountability via oral defense/presentation.
- Proposes an "offense" response: universities should lean into communal, in-person learning that AI can't replicate — social/relational skills (persuasion, negotiation, giving/receiving criticism, trust-building) and moral virtues (justice, honesty, integrity) that are only formed through unchosen, constrained relationships with others.
- Frames this as a return to the medieval "collegium" model — a self-governing community forming intellectual and moral capacity for vocations serving society — rather than either a pure knowledge-repository or expertise-credentialing institution.

## Quote

> AI will eventually commodify expertise. But expertise alone doesn't make for a great education, or even a good one.
