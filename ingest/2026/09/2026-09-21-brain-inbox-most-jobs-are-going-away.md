# Most jobs are going away

## Insights

- Introduces "essential demand" (human labor that IS the product, so removing the human changes the offering) vs. "derived demand" (human labor as an incidental input to an output the market doesn't care about the source of) — citing Alfred Marshall's 1890 articulation of derived demand.
- Identifies four categories of essential demand: **Presence** (paying for a human to physically perform something, e.g. massage, live performance), **Provenance** (value attached to something because a human made it, e.g. handmade/artisanal goods), **Liability** (wanting an accountable human "throat to choke," e.g. CEOs, doctors, judges, elected officials — previously called the "statutory economy"), and **Affinity** (value tied to a specific, nonfungible name/persona, e.g. celebrities or specific commentators — previously called the "nominal economy" or "Taylor Swift Effect").
- Argues essential demand has always been a narrow slice of the total economy, and most labor — including most historical jobs like home construction — is derived demand, meaning automation doesn't change the value of the output, only its cost (human labor cited as 30–50% of construction costs).
- Claims essential demand, while somewhat elastic, is far less elastic than discretionary spending overall — e.g. wealth increases don't proportionally increase consumption of human-provided services like massages.
- Frames the argument as underpinning a broader thesis (elaborated in a linked whitepaper) that most jobs — being derived-demand jobs — are vulnerable to being automated away without loss of perceived value to the buyer.

## Quote

> Essential demand for human labor is when the human is part of the product or service.
