# Evidence about risk should be transparent

## Insights

- The science of measuring "loss-of-control" risk in AI systems is nascent: there are no settled, standardized methods to determine whether increasingly capable models are trying to undermine or seize control, and it's unclear whether reported alignment benchmark results reflect real safety or just benchmark-gaming.
- Current AI company safety claims are too high-level and imprecise to be meaningfully "verified" or "audited" — no company has even published a claim set that covers all major loss-of-control risk questions (e.g., confidence they won't build uncontrollable superintelligence within six months).
- The author argues the field should prioritize generating more and better evidence about risk (both from companies and third-party investigators) over trying to prematurely "verify" safety cases or pacing commitments — this evidence generation should function like open scientific inquiry, not compliance auditing.
- Proposed norm: "evidence transparency" — third parties should publicly share the empirical evidence behind their conclusions (not just verdicts), so outside scientists can form independent judgments rather than trusting an evaluator's authority.
- Evidence transparency is argued to have three benefits: it lets competing AI companies learn from each other's safety practices via public artifacts, it brings in outside scientists with different incentives (they bear catastrophic risk but don't gain from faster development), and it allows the scientific community to evaluate whether third-party evaluators themselves did rigorous work.
- Full transparency isn't fully achievable due to capacity constraints and IP redactions (illustrated by a Hugging Face report example where methodology was shared but not prompts/code), but it should remain the guiding goal.
- Durably reducing loss-of-control risk is framed as ultimately requiring shared technical standards enforced uniformly across the industry, including internationally — with the stringency of such standards described as fundamentally a political question.

## Quote

> External scientists will generally have to trust that third party evaluators like METR are not outright lying.
