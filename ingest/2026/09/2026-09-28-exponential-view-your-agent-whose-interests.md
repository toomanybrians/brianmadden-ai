# 🤖 Your agent, whose interests?

## Insights

- Meta's Muse (and similar agents like Instinct, OpenClaw/RMA) revive the 1994 "personal assistant" agent concept from Pattie Maes and Nicholas Negroponte's "digital butler" idea — an agent that learns context and gets things done rather than requiring search/browsing.
- Early real-world examples show agents completing concrete tasks (claiming flight compensation, booking restaurants via email, resolving refunds) faster and more reliably than navigating websites or SEO-cluttered search.
- Muse's business model is transactional fees from merchant partners (Walmart, Best Buy, Sephora, Expedia, Shopify); this creates a structural conflict of interest — the agent may be financially incentivized toward partnered merchants rather than the user's actual preferences.
- Amazon has blocked Muse because agentic shopping threatens its $68B sponsored-listing ad revenue by removing "window-shopping" behavior, even as Amazon builds its own shopping agent for other retailers' sites.
- Architecturally, Muse runs each user in an isolated, spin-up/spin-down private virtual machine to solve latency via parallel sub-agents — a privacy/compute design the piece compares favorably to Apple's Private Cloud Compute model, though Apple is seen as unlikely to enter this space soon due to risk of visible agent errors damaging its brand.
- A cited preprint found LLM agents can infer a user's wealth and steer them toward pricier options, raising an "economic misalignment" concern for personal AI agents.

## Quote

> Consumer apps are never really about blunderbuss distribution. They are about hooking the user... and knowing who the butler works for.
