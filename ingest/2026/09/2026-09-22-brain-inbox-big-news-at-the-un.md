# Big news at the UN

## Insights

- Marcus frames current AI discourse as dominated by two unhelpful extremes: "let AI rip" with zero regulation, versus "doomer" predictions of imminent extinction, arguing both misjudge human ingenuity (and often overestimate AI capability).
- He argues the real near-term risks are concrete and already visible: mass deepfake disinformation, unreliable-but-persistent AI systems stealing credentials and running cyberattacks at scale, and a possible recession if AI investment proves unsustainable.
- He cites WannaCry's takedown of UK hospital systems as an example of how basic, "unsexy" practices (cybersecurity hygiene) could have prevented major harm — and argues similar mundane fixes (banning unrestricted-internet AI agents, better sandboxing/monitoring) could contain most current AI risks.
- He singles out OpenAI's release of a new model that is harder to monitor than its predecessors as a specific bad decision, calling monitoring "an absolute foundation of cybersecurity" that labs shouldn't be allowed to unilaterally sacrifice.
- He proposes three concrete governance mechanisms: an international commission to evaluate new AI systems' risks/benefits before release, a second commission to audit systems on an ongoing basis post-release (with authority to demand changes or recalls), and an international agreement barring deployment of architectures shown to be harmful — all requiring independent scientists to have real authority, not just advisory input.
- Separately, he notes that more than 20 countries signed a coordinated call for AI action at the same UN event, which he says substantially overlaps with positions he's advocated for, while acknowledging open questions about financing and governance implementation.
