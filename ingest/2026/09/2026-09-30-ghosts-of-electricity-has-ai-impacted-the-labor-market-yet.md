# Has AI impacted the labor market yet?

## Insights

- Multiple studies (US, UK, Switzerland, Sweden) find declining junior/entry-level hiring in AI-exposed white-collar roles, with effects emerging roughly 18 months after ChatGPT's release; Stanford's "Canaries" data shows a growing gap (19% below trend for 22–25-year-olds in high-exposure roles by June 2026), but authors now treat this as descriptive rather than causal.
- Nordic population-wide administrative data (Finland, Norway) largely fails to replicate the junior-hiring decline, suggesting national labor-market institutions, employment protections, or demographic factors may mediate or mask AI's effect.
- A live methodological fight centers on whether remote work, not AI, explains junior hiring declines — one study finds the AI-exposure effect vanishes when controlling for WFH, while another finds it survives and even grows; David Deming's theory posits remote work "containerized" office tasks, enabling AI automation.
- Aggregate labor market indicators (unemployment, layoffs, employer surveys) show almost no AI-attributable effect so far — only 1% of laid-off workers cite AI, and ~90% of surveyed executives say AI hasn't yet significantly changed employment or productivity.
- Usage data (Google ATLAS, Anthropic) shows AI adoption is broad (88% of US employment touched) but shallow (only ~21% of tasks within exposed jobs), consistent with automation hitting narrow low-expertise sub-tasks rather than full job displacement.
- Theoretical models (Ide 2025; Garicano & Rayo 2025) warn that automating entry-level tasks could erode long-run skill transmission/apprenticeship pipelines, with GDP growth impacts estimated at -0.05 to -0.35 percentage points if the pattern persists.

## Quote

> Apart from early career workers in exposed fields, the impact of AI on the labor market has thus far been muted.
