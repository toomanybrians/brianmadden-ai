# It’s Too Early to Ban AI Personhood

## Insights

- Since 2022, twelve US states have introduced "exclusion bills" barring AI from legal personhood (marriage, property, corporate office, liability); four (Idaho, North Dakota, Utah, Tennessee) have enacted such bans, some explicitly asserting AI is not conscious or sentient as a matter of law.
- Some legal scholars (Salib, Goldstein) argue limited AI personhood — letting AI systems own property or enter contracts — could actually improve accountability, since it's currently hard to pin liability on a human or corporate entity when an autonomous AI agent independently causes harm.
- The piece draws an analogy to corporate personhood: AI systems, like corporations, don't need to be conscious to respond to legal incentives (e.g., losing compute access as a deterrent functions like a fine or asset seizure).
- Risks of AI personhood cited include: reduced human accountability, AI-human marriage recognition, rogue agents using legal/property rights to accumulate power, and mass-replicated AI "persons" gaining outsized collective political influence (e.g., voting rights).
- Authors argue exclusion bills conflate corporate and natural personhood, are passed with little engagement with consciousness science or policy analysis, and risk foreclosing future options since laws are hard to reverse.
- Recommendation: hold off on blanket bans, treat different bundles of rights (liability status vs. welfare protections vs. political rights) as separate questions, and use targeted small-scale pilots/proofs of concept — via bodies like the National AI Advisory Committee — to build empirical understanding before legislating broadly.

## Quote

> But categorically prohibiting any kind of legal status at this early stage is both unnecessary and unwise.
