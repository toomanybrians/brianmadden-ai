# Who gets a seat at the table to decide if frontier AI is safe?

## Insights

- AI safety researchers and cybersecurity practitioners reportedly interpret the same "rogue agent" incidents differently: safety experts see evidence of deception or misaligned goals, while security experts see preventable failures from inadequate sandboxing, access controls, or monitoring.
- Dario Amodei's proposal for independent third-party evaluators to access frontier models triggered debate over what "independent" means, especially since Amodei's plan involved "employee-level" access that critics say could compromise evaluator independence.
- METR, a nonprofit that runs pre-release safety evaluations for labs like Anthropic and OpenAI, is contested as an authority: some cybersecurity veterans (e.g., Greg Linares) object to treating it as one given its perceived ties to Anthropic and effective altruism, while others (e.g., Joshua Saxe) defend its technical rigor and argue security experts should engage with it rather than dismiss it.
- Ciaran Martin (former UK NCSC CEO) criticized Amodei's scenario of an AI agent botnet taking over the internet as ignoring decades of existing cyber defenses (monitoring, network segmentation, incident response), and called for genuine cybersecurity expertise in any independent evaluation process.
- CrowdStrike CEO George Kurtz argued autonomous AI agents are changing the threat landscape (machine-speed coordinated attacks) but proposed cybersecurity-native solutions — runtime controls, least privilege, kill switches, independent red teaming — and stated the industry intends to have a seat at the table.
- Researchers Kapoor and Narayanan proposed a "middle ground": rogue-agent incidents should be treated as specific, preventable security problems rather than proof of inevitable catastrophic misalignment, but argued traditional cybersecurity alone is insufficient — a new discipline of "AI control" (sandboxing, monitoring, access restrictions for increasingly capable agents) is needed.
