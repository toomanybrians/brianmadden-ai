# How the AI Act Was Negotiated and What Comes Next (with Brando Benifei)

## Insights

- The EU AI Office gained full enforcement powers over general-purpose AI models on August 2, 2026, but operates with only ~36 staff in its AI Safety Unit — far smaller than bodies like the UK AI Security Institute, which is not even a regulator.
- The recent "digital omnibus" revision of the AI Act simplified some procedures for smaller businesses and delayed high-risk use case rules (linked to unfinished technical standards), but Parliament pushed back on most deregulation attempts and preserved GPAI oversight; a new prohibition on CSAM and non-consensual "nudification" content was added, prompted partly by incidents involving Grok-like systems.
- Benifei argues Europe's AI competitiveness gap predates the AI Act and stems from fragmented capital markets, high risk-aversion among investors, and limited compute infrastructure — not the regulation itself; he says companies' real friction point is data-access/GDPR-related rules, not the AI Act.
- A key structural problem is fragmented implementation across EU member states despite unified legislation — unlike the US federal/state model — which creates uncertainty; he advocates for a unified EU digital enforcement agency to reduce this fragmentation.
- He frames recent AI incidents (agent hijacking, capability jumps, cybersecurity/loss-of-control concerns) as validation that systemic-risk regulation was necessary and not merely theoretical, and calls for the EU to build technical enforcement capacity via the upcoming multiannual budget.
- He sees a coming "fight around public opinion," describing industry-aligned commentators as spreading confusion to undermine regulatory credibility, and calls on independent researchers, journalists, and civil society to strengthen enforcement through independent testing and reporting.
