# Executive Briefing: Your backup job says it ran. Could your team actually restore what it needs?

## Insights

- AI is enhancing attacker capability in a meaningful way: it can investigate unfamiliar systems, find usable access points, test approaches, adapt after failures, and continue — essentially automating the iterative work of an intrusion.
- Major cloud providers (Google, Microsoft, AWS) have documented cases of AI agent frameworks operating inside live intrusions — doing investigation and adaptation — with existing permission structures cited as a limiting factor on how far such agents can get.
- Supplier/vendor relationships often grant far broader access than organizations realize — citing Optimizely, Smartsheet, and PLAID as examples where a vendor can reach code running in visitors' browsers or credentials unlocking other services.
- Restoring a service or recovering lost data isn't the same as full recovery — ConnectWise, the British Library, and Jaguar Land Rover are cited as cases showing substantial remaining work even after systems/data are nominally restored.
- AI is also already functioning as a defensive tool: Mozilla used a tool called "Claude Mythos Preview" to find and fix 271 Firefox bugs, illustrating a model's output being converted into tested repairs at scale.
- The piece frames current infrastructure risk as compounding: decades of interconnected, unevenly maintained software with poorly understood dependencies are now facing faster, more capable AI-assisted attacks, with failures reaching into physical-world systems like food safety and medical supply integrity.

## Quote

> We've put ordinary life on top of these systems, and we're discovering how difficult they are to secure while the attacks become faster and more capable.
