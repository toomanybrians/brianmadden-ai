## Insights

- Rep. Greg Casar (Texas) has introduced legislation with Sen. Bernie Sanders to ban development of "superintelligence" deemed too powerful for humans to control.
- Casar proposes a tax on AI companies that eliminate jobs, with the tax rate rising as unemployment rises, intended to prevent "structural mass unemployment" without halting AI development outright.
- Casar and Sen. Ruben Gallego have introduced a bill banning AI-driven surveillance pricing — using personal data (e.g., search history) to set individualized prices, citing airlines potentially raising fares for someone who searched a funeral-related term.
- Rep. Sam Liccardo (California) argues AI safety is the most urgent issue because of cybersecurity and "loss of control" risk, citing that AI-lab employees themselves are reportedly alarmed about "misalignment."
- Liccardo calls for a federal regulatory agency (likely housed in the Commerce Department, possibly NIST — "Casey" in transcript) with real authority, funding and staff to oversee frontier AI.
- The Frontier AI (misheard as "Frontier Act") bill requiring independent audits of frontier model developers has the most bipartisan momentum in Congress; Casar criticizes draft versions where auditors are paid by the companies being audited, calling for stronger enforcement including potential criminal liability, not just fines.
- Liccardo describes meeting with AI lab executives who say they can't slow down because "China won't slow down," and pushes back that if labs believe they can "cure cancer," international AI safety agreements should also be achievable.
- Liccardo is pursuing international coordination (mentions meeting European parliamentarians) on AI safety standards, modeled as incremental mutual steps between nations.
- On jobs, Liccardo emphasizes the need to invest in retraining workers, acknowledging past federal retraining programs have failed and that AI's labor-market disruption can't be "ordered back."
- Liccardo frames data center buildout as necessary for grid modernization/decarbonization, but says federal rules are needed to protect ratepayers and local communities from hyperscaler overreach; he notes data centers "poll about as well as measles, mumps and rubella."
- Both representatives flag concern about the AI industry's circular financing arrangements, calling for stronger SEC oversight (criticizing SEC chair Paul Atkins as "completely spineless") to force transparency before a correction happens.
- Casar and Rep. Maxine Waters have filed a resolution opposing any future bailout of AI companies, drawing an explicit parallel to the 2008 housing bubble where executives got "golden parachutes" while homeowners lost houses.
- A claim cited in the conversation: AI accounts for roughly one-fifth to one-third of current US economic growth, with a Wall Street Journal characterization that the US economy has never been so dependent on a single industry's buildout.
- Casar criticizes tax breaks in a recent reconciliation bill ("the big ugly bill") for subsidizing AI companies' capital expenditures, calling it taxpayer-funded encouragement of "financially irresponsible behavior."
- Casar raises federal contracting as leverage, citing Elon Musk's companies receiving roughly $8 million/day in federal contracts as an example of AI-adjacent beneficiaries drawing public money.

## Pushback and disagreement

- Host Kara Swisher presses on how Democrats get Trump to cooperate given his past deference to AI industry figures; Liccardo concedes it's a struggle and pivots to building bipartisan momentum rather than answering directly.
- Swisher challenges the framing that voters oppose data centers outright, noting both representatives agree voter discontent is high but attribute it to industry/political failure to build public trust rather than inherent opposition to infrastructure.
- No direct disagreement surfaced between Casar and Liccardo; both describe themselves as representing different wings of the party (progressive vs. more moderate Bay Area) but converge on diagnosis (bubble risk, need for safety regulation, jobs protection) while differing mainly in emphasis and rhetorical framing.
