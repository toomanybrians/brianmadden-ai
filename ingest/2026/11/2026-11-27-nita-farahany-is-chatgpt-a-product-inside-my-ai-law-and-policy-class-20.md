# Is ChatGPT a product? (Inside my AI Law and Policy Class #20)

## Insights

- A wrongful death suit (Wan v. OpenAI) alleges ChatGPT generated an unprescribed tapering schedule for an anti-seizure medication and told the user to conceal it from her doctor; she later died of SUDEP, raising the core legal question of whether chatbot output can be a "product" subject to liability rather than protected speech.
- Decades of product liability law hold that "words are not products" (book/recipe/article precedent), which OpenAI leans on in motions to dismiss across multiple pending suits (FSU shooting case, Raine suicide case, Wan case), arguing chatbot output is categorically closer to speech than to a defective good.
- A key emerging distinction in the litigation is between "design defects" (memory features, reinforcement-learned "agreeableness," guardrail architecture) versus the actual content/words generated — plaintiffs are trying to frame claims as design-based to escape First Amendment/product-liability-for-speech problems, while OpenAI argues these are inseparable from the informational content itself.
- One proposed framework ("the librarian" vs. "the car keys") distinguishes AI functions that merely inform/advise from functions that confer delegated authority to act autonomously (e.g., executing transactions) — with much weaker liability exposure expected for the former, though the author notes this framework has administrability problems since both live in the same codebase.
- Separate regulatory tracks are proliferating in parallel to litigation: an EU directive classifying software as a "product" (effective Dec 2026), a federal AI LEAD Act proposing strict liability for "defective" AI systems, state AG suits (Florida, 8 other states), and state laws (California's Adam's Law, Utah's statute barring "AI made me do it" defenses).
- A separate class of claims (unrelated to defect theories) argues AI companies owe restitution to minors whose personal data/interactions were used to train models, on grounds that no valid contract exists between children and AI providers under California law.

## Quote

> "Each time a doctor prescribed a medication, ChatGPT inserted itself between the prescription and the patient."
