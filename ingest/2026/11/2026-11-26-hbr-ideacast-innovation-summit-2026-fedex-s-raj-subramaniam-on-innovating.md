# Innovation Summit 2026: FedEx’s Raj Subramaniam on Innovating at the Speed of Global Commerce

## Fit with Brian's work

**Fit: partial.** Most of the conversation is about FedEx's logistics network, tariffs, and global trade; one stretch on AI strategy touches Brian's enterprise-AI and knowledge-factory thinking.

- FedEx started building a data platform in 2020 — well before the AI hype cycle — specifically to organize insights from 18 million daily packages before pointing AI at it. This is a real-world precedent for Brian's knowledge-factory argument: curate the canonical data layer first, then let AI generate value from it, rather than pointing AI at raw sludge.
- Subramaniam's claim that "AI transformation has to be a CEO-led transformation... not a technology play, but a fundamental business play" aligns with Brian's "AI-first memo" posts but frames it top-down, which sits in tension with his worker-led-adoption thesis — worth noting as a counterexample from a traditional, highly regulated, physical-operations company rather than a confirmation.
- The customs classification example (a shipper's single $200 Google-sourced code no longer works post-de-minimis; now needs ~200 codes by size/weight/material, handled via a Gen AI tool trained on FedEx's own shipping history) is a concrete instance of institutional/tacit knowledge being encoded into an AI tool as part of a workflow — a small-scale analog to the knowledge-factory pattern (accumulated proprietary history becoming the substrate an AI tool draws from).
- The three-layer AI framework described (network optimization → customer-facing predictability → supply-chain-wide orchestration) is a domain-specific maturity ladder, loosely parallel in shape to Brian's cognitive stack or 7-stage roadmap, but built around logistics outcomes rather than worker cognition — worth flagging as a structurally similar but substantively distinct layering, not evidence for his stack.

## Insights

- Subramaniam says FedEx began building a unified data platform in 2020, two years before the generative AI wave, because "the fuel for AI is data" and the company recognized the value of its insights on global supply chains early.
- FedEx frames its AI investment in three tiers: (1) network efficiency via deep learning models optimizing routing, (2) improved customer value proposition through predictive visibility (cited as a reason FedEx became the largest healthcare-industry transportation provider over three years), and (3) aspirations toward broader supply-chain orchestration, citing external research estimating $1.9 trillion in global supply-chain waste.
- A concrete Gen AI use case: after the U.S. "de minimis" exemption ended in August, a shipper who previously used a single Googled customs classification code for neon signs now needs roughly 200 different codes depending on size, weight, and material; FedEx built a Gen AI tool using its own shipping history to help customers navigate this complexity.
- Subramaniam argues AI transformation must be CEO-led and top-down because "the boundaries between business and technology are fast disappearing" — not a technology initiative but a core business strategy decision.
- On managing AI organizationally, he says companies need systematic frameworks defining objectives, execution strategy, and required talent, alongside heightened attention to cybersecurity risks introduced by AI — stated at a high level without specifics.
- FedEx expanded physical capacity (new facilities/routes in Osaka, Vietnam, Singapore, India, Dublin, Chile) in response to shifting trade patterns, which he attributes to combining bottom-up intelligence from its existing global network with human "experience and judgment," not AI-driven decisioning per se.
