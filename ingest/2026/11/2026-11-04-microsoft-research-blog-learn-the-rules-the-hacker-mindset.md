# Learn the rules: The hacker mindset

## Insights

- DARPA's Cyber Grand Challenge (2016, discussed at its 10th anniversary) was the first all-machine hacking tournament, built on "expert systems" technology rather than machine learning — autonomous tools that found and patched vulnerabilities without human intervention during the match.
- Today's successor efforts (e.g., AI Cyber Challenge) run "cyber reasoning systems" that are powered by actual ML models and layered on top of the older expert-system toolchains, which now function as an execution "harness" for the models.
- A central claim: cybersecurity's prior equilibrium rested on the scarcity of high-end offensive capability (cost to mount a sophisticated attack was high); AI models have "completely upended" that cost equation, changing the offense/defense balance.
- Security capabilities are framed as inherently dual-use rather than offensive or defensive by nature — the same tool (encryption, remote access) is malicious or legitimate purely based on who controls it, which is why the red-team/blue-team split is described as artificial.
- A lesson from competitive hacking: teams with high aggregate technical skill still waste effort re-solving already-solved problems when they lack coordination/social intelligence — raw IQ doesn't substitute for organizational awareness of who's doing what.
- A described defensive tool (Project Freta) addresses "tampering" — attackers disabling or spoofing monitoring systems rather than evading them — by pulling security telemetry directly from cloud hardware so it can't be altered by compromised software.

## Quote

> "That cost equation has been completely upended by the capabilities of models." — Mike Walker
