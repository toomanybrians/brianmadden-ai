# Humans on AI #70 || November 13, 2026

## Insights

- MIT biologist Kevin Esvelt argues AI will plateau near human level in "open-ended" domains lacking cheap verification (politics, strategy, biosecurity), while Daniel Kokotajlo expects an intelligence explosion by 2027 and says the burden of proof is on anyone predicting such a wall; Esvelt counters this is a speed limit, not a block, since cheap checking can eventually be engineered into more fields.
- Dwarkesh Patel reverses his prior skepticism about AI's ability to "learn on the job," now forecasting AI matching the best human AI researchers by ~2028 and superintelligence by 2030, reasoning that ML research — like math, which AI recently solved a Millennium Prize problem in — is well-scoped and cheaply verifiable.
- Research on long-horizon ML research tasks ("idea basins") finds models strongly resist exploring new approaches: 47% of task runs never leave the first idea tried, yet switching to a genuinely new idea produces roughly 9x the gains of refining an existing one, and 58% of runs never reach the best available solution.
- Economists dividing Anthropic's recent capability gains by R&D spend find diminishing per-dollar research returns (each spending doubling yields less marginal progress than before), concluding there's no strong evidence yet of AI driving its own research input or of a self-sustaining intelligence explosion — though they can't rule one out.
- OpenAI research shows that ordinary model scaling (not explicit training on chain-of-thought) is sufficient to degrade humans' ability to monitor AI reasoning, as "no-CoT" single-pass reasoning ability improves faster than the monitorability gains achieved through RL.
- Anthropic's study of multi-agent collaboration finds that when each user's agent acts independently rather than one agent serving everyone, outcomes get measurably worse — teams stall, misallocate shared resources, and make unsupported claims about each other roughly 2-3x more often than single-agent setups.

> Pretraining loss measures how well the model learns the map... not the territory. — Kevin Esvelt
