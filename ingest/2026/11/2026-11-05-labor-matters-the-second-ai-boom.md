# The Second AI Boom

## Insights

- Argues a "second AI boom" is distinct from the first: the first is capital spending to build AI infrastructure (data centers, chips, software); the second is spending to build the products and discoveries that improved AI makes possible (digital agents, robots, driverless vehicles, and scientific breakthroughs in medicine/materials/energy/chemistry).
- Cites AI's rapid progress in mathematics (700+ AI-generated manuscripts from OpenAI, a claimed refutation of the Exponential Time Hypothesis) as evidence AI is now producing original research faster than humans can evaluate it, with mathematics as a foundational input that accelerates progress across other sciences.
- Frames AI as effectively multiplying the research workforce — referencing Jack Clark's image of "a billion virtual scientists" directing/supplementing ~10 million human researchers — which will make valuable discoveries vastly outpace the economy's current capacity to commercialize them (trials, pilot production, factories, supply chains).
- Notes the second boom will employ labor very differently than the first: data centers run with small staffs once built, while trials, labs, factories, and supply chains for the second boom require people at many more stages — implying different job geography/structure.
- Predicts investor revaluation will extend far beyond the first boom's winners (semiconductors, cloud, AI developers) into pharma, materials, energy, agriculture, autos, robotics, logistics, and defense, creating a second wealth effect that boosts consumption even before products generate revenue.
- Forecasts GDP growth of 3-4% annually through 2031 (absent recession), well above CBO's 1.8% and the Fed's 2.0% long-run estimates, arguing conventional forecasts are missing this dynamic entirely; expects most physical-product/scientific-discovery effects to materialize as a "third boom" after 2031.

## Quote

> AI has moved from solving hard problems to producing original research faster than humans can evaluate it.
