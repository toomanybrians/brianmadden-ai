# Banks are locking out AI Agents and shifting the losses to customers who let them in 🤖🏦; Revolut finally starts using its banking licences to lend 🏦🇬🇧

## Insights

- Major US banks (JPMorgan, Bank of America, Citi, Wells Fargo, Morgan Stanley, Schwab) began blocking AI agents that log into customer accounts via shared passwords, a wave of restrictions that started mid-October — before the Axios report on rogue agents, suggesting security framing may follow rather than precede the real motive.
- Regulators (Fed, OCC, FDIC) are reportedly backing the blocks privately and probing banks' ability to limit, audit, and shut down autonomous agents, even though no fully verified case of unauthorized agent-driven transfers has been documented.
- Under Regulation E, giving an AI agent your login credentials shifts fraud liability to the customer — Wells Fargo's new terms (effective Nov 24) treat an agent's actions as the customer's own "without further verification," and Jamie Dimon has acknowledged this liability transfer exists.
- Banks appear to be simultaneously blocking outside agents while building their own money-movement tools (JPMorgan's Smart Cash, Schwab's Charley), and some blocking banks are also design partners on Meta/Sierra's "Personal Agent Protocol" — positioning themselves to control and monetize the "official" agent access route rather than opposing agents outright.
- A scoped/tokenized, read-only or human-in-the-loop model (e.g., Mercury, where agents request payments a human approves) is emerging as the trust-building middle path, contrasted with fully autonomous password-based agent access.
- The piece frames whoever "vouches for" or certifies an agent as the party that ends up controlling payment flows — implying agent identity/certification infrastructure, not just AI capability, is the real battleground.
