# Who Will Make a Great Leader? There’s a Test for That.

## Fit with Brian's work

**Fit: partial.** The episode is a leadership-assessment science discussion; only the closing segment on managing AI agents touches Brian's territory, and it does so directly.

- The finding that manager skill with people correlates 0.81 with manager skill with AI agents is new, concrete evidence for the 2031 worker-shape forecast's premise that a knowledge worker becomes "one human plus N agents"—it suggests the orchestration skill isn't a new competency to be invented but largely the same social/management skill redirected at agents.
- This bears on the "agent-to-human ratio" scratchpad note and the cognitive stack's "worker" layer (intent and judgment): if managing agents draws on the same underlying skill as managing people, it complicates the idea that agent orchestration requires fundamentally new training, and instead suggests existing management-assessment tools could identify who will orchestrate agent fleets well.
- The side point that AI is making realistic simulation-based training cheap (comparing it to flight simulators and surgical training) loosely echoes the "coaching-as-output" scratchpad idea—AI building tailored practice environments rather than static documents—though the guest applies it to leadership training, not knowledge-work deliverables.

## Insights

- David Deming (Harvard economist, Dean of Harvard College) and colleagues ran repeated random-assignment experiments to measure individual management skill, then tested whether the same people's manager skill transferred to leading teams of AI agents on an identical task.
- The task used was the "hidden profile task": team members each hold partial information needed to solve a problem and must elicit and share it through dialogue; the same task was run with human teams and with AI-agent teams.
- Correlation between a person's measured skill managing human teams and their skill managing AI-agent teams was 0.81, which Deming calls very high (he compares it to the correlation between height and blocks-per-game in basketball).
- Separately, Deming found that promoting based on individual performance (the "Peter Principle" approach) beats random promotion or self-nomination, but underperforms a direct, purpose-built assessment of management skill itself—meaning management is a distinct skill not well-proxied by job performance or self-selection.
- People who volunteer for leadership roles are not better or worse managers on average than those who don't—wanting the role and being good at it are unrelated, and self-selection skews toward overconfidence and gender bias, which Deming frames as a case for structured assessment reducing hiring bias.
- Deming argues AI is driving down the cost of building realistic simulated environments for skills training (citing flight simulators and surgical training as precedents), making this kind of assessment and development more broadly affordable than before.
- Deming concedes his team's current leadership measures are still incomplete: they don't capture long-term trust, psychological safety, or whether people believe a leader will "have their back," which he considers understated rather than overstated in importance.
