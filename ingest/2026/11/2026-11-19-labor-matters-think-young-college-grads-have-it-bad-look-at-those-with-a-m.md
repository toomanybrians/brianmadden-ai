# Think Young College Grads Have It Bad? Look at Those With a Master’s Degree.

## Insights

- Unemployment for 22–26-year-olds with a master's degree has nearly doubled since mid-2024, averaging 6.7% over the past two years versus 3.5% pre-pandemic — a historic reversal, since master's holders had the lowest unemployment of any education group in that age range before the shift.
- Master's holders now have higher unemployment than bachelor's or associate's degree holders their age, a gap that never existed in the 2003–mid-2024 data; associate's degree holders' unemployment has barely moved and is back to pre-pandemic levels.
- The wage premium for a master's degree over an associate's degree fell from roughly two-thirds higher pay to less than half, driven mainly by master's holders' pay stalling then falling (~4% drop) rather than other groups' pay falling.
- Only about one in ten young master's holders now works in an occupation typically requiring a graduate degree, down from one in six — all employment growth among this group has gone into jobs that don't require the degree.
- The authors propose three partial explanations: a near-doubling in the number of young master's holders (790,000 to 1 million) without a matching increase in graduate-level jobs; weaker entry-level hiring (with AI cited as a likely contributing factor) reflected in employers demanding more experience; and a declining "signal value" of some master's degrees (e.g., MBAs) as top-college grads pull back from enrolling in them.
- The effect is concentrated in early-career workers — three-quarters of the unemployment rise comes from people entering/re-entering the labor force — while 27–34-year-old master's holders still retain a clear earnings/unemployment advantage, though the authors doubt this will hold if entry-level hiring stays weak.
- A 2025 budget law ties graduate programs' federal loan eligibility to graduates out-earning bachelor's-only workers in two of three years four years post-graduation, meaning the shrinking master's premium creates direct financial risk for university programs.

## Quote

> Outside a recession, we have rarely seen a trend in labor market data turn this fast.
