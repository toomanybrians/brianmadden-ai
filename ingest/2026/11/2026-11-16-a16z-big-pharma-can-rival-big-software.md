# Big Pharma Can Rival Big Software

## Insights

- The piece frames AI's near-term role in pharma explicitly as knowledge-work automation: LLMs/reasoning models are "accelerating (and often completing)" tasks done by scientists and researchers — literature review, experimental design, data analysis, regulatory filing and drafting FDA submissions.
- Pharma's valuation logic currently gives companies almost no credit for their drug-discovery engine (DCF models only value approved/late-stage drugs); if AI makes discovery more repeatable and predictable, the argument is investors may start valuing R&D infrastructure the way they value software platforms, shifting pharma's business-model economics closer to tech.
- Two distinct AI-era business models are emerging: "picks and shovels" vendors selling data/models/software to pharma and frontier labs (e.g., lab automation, predictive biology models, molecular design, reasoning agents), versus vertically integrated "full-stack" biotech companies that use AI internally to discover and own their own drug assets.
- Frontier AI labs are moving from tool-providers to direct participants in drug development — one lab opened a wet lab and is developing its own drug candidates for neglected diseases; another acquired a company to build an agentic layer for executing biotech programs.
- Structural barriers specific to biology are named as reasons AI progress won't be a simple repeat of software/language gains: biology lacks internet-scale training data, is a far more complex multilayered system than language, and — unlike math or code — can't be fully validated in silico, requiring long wet-lab and clinical feedback loops.
- The analogy offered for where AI-for-biology currently stands is "GPT-3 level of market maturity" — single models beginning to generalize across many biological tasks without heavy task-specific retraining, with meaningful improvement expected over roughly the next five years.
