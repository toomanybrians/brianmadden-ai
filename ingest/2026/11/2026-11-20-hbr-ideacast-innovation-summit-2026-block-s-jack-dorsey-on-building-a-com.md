# Innovation Summit 2026: Block’s Jack Dorsey on Building a Company Around Intelligence

## Fit with Brian's work

**Fit: direct.** Dorsey describes Block's actual reorganization around AI-driven information flow, and several of his specific claims directly engage, confirm, or complicate arguments Brian is actively developing.

- Dorsey calls forward-deployed-engineer setups and harnesses like Claude Code or Codex "a gap in capability from the model itself" that will shrink as models improve — a direct complication of Brian's "follow the FDE money" thesis, which treats FDE investment as the next durable category of IT spend rather than temporary scaffolding.
- Dorsey's push on model efficiency (DESSA, a laptop-trainable voice model) and his claim that LLMs are "extremely inefficient" cuts against the compute-scarcity/token-governance thread — he argues efficiency gains could dissolve the scarcity problem rather than just raise the capability bar, a stronger claim than Brian's own local-model test suggested.
- Dorsey's framing of a company as "a technology" whose intelligence is normally illegible but can now be made "almost perfectly legible" by capturing every artifact independently echoes the knowledge factory's "canonical context layer is the new source code of the business" — convergent evidence from an operator, not a vendor.
- His account of what becomes valuable once building is easy — judgment, taste, a differentiated point of view paired with a capable model — supports the cognitive stack's claim that the brain layer (not agents/harnesses) is where value concentrates.
- Block's IC/DRI/Coach role structure is a live, named alternative to Brian's cognitive-owner/operator/curator taxonomy for how AI reshapes org structure — worth tracking as a competing real-world cut rather than a validation or contradiction.
- Dorsey's open-weights advocacy is framed around decentralization and security ("more eyes on a system"), a different argument than Brian's bubble-survival framing of open weights as the planning floor, but reinforcing the same conclusion from another angle.

## Insights

### Reorganizing around intelligence
- Block moved in 2024 from business-unit structure (Square, Cash App run as separate companies) back to discipline-based structure (engineering, design, finance) specifically to standardize tools, policies, and culture ahead of AI deployment — Dorsey frames this as removing duplication that would otherwise multiply AI vendor/tool sprawl.
- Dorsey argues a company "is a technology" for distributing information and accountability, tracing the org-chart playbook back through Google, Silicon Graphics, HP, GE, railroads, and Roman administration.
- Because Block is remote-first, nearly every interaction leaves a written artifact; Dorsey says this lets AI ingest the full record into one queryable surface, which he claims "diminishes the need for the communication hierarchy."
- The resulting structure has three roles: Individual Contributor (builds/operates), Directly Responsible Individual (strategic, owns a roadmap, accountable), and Coach (replaces "manager," assigned to help an IC/DRI master their craft rather than command-and-control).
- Dorsey claims this reorg involved 2026 layoffs and was deliberately "disruptive and uncomfortable," and that he accepted short-term loss of credibility with staff in service of a longer-term outcome he believed in.
- He claims flatter, AI-legible organizations make retrospectives easier and more common — decisions, their context, and whether they were later judged wrong become visible company-wide — a practice he says "great organizations" already did informally but AI tooling can now make routine rather than exceptional.

### Forward-deployed engineers, harnesses, and alignment
- Asked directly about forward-deployed-engineer models, Dorsey says current harnesses (Claude Code, Codex, FDEs) exist because of model capability gaps, and that "those things become less necessary" as models improve, including the harness layer itself.
- He reframes the hard skill as alignment: knowing what you want the model to do, whether it fits company values/purpose, and having the "courage" to act on a differentiated point of view — he says this skill "hasn't necessarily been the most valuable in the past" or has been watered down.
- Dorsey's nightmare scenario for AI is concentration of power in a few labs or "pacing the frontier," not rogue agents per se; he frames open models/weights as the mitigation, arguing broad access lets more people build systems resilient to adversarial, capable models.
- On security generally: he argues no system can be made perfectly secure, so the goal is staying ahead of adversaries through continuous iteration ("life is motion"), paired with wide distribution of capability ("more eyes on a system").

### Compute, efficiency, and monetization
- Dorsey argues current LLMs are "extremely inefficient" and that massive compute/power isn't the only viable path, pointing to older deep-learning traditions around efficient world models.
- He cites Block's DESSA, a voice model trained entirely on speech sound (no language knowledge) using "very few resources," already runnable on a laptop and targeted eventually at phones, as proof efficient models can match capability without datacenter-scale compute.
- Dorsey describes Block's commercial angle as selling the connective tissue around open models — hosting, inference, compliance/security/risk integration into a company's existing systems — because customers will pay to "save time," not necessarily for the model itself.
- His personal information-gathering routine: three hours each morning reading research papers (physics, biology, engineering, math) via a site called Archive, used partly to judge what's valuable enough to build or pay for versus self-host.

### Innovation, judgment, and talent development
- Dorsey's innovation philosophy is explicitly Jobs-to-be-Done/Clay Christensen: ship something, watch customers use it in unexpected ways (he cites Twitter's @-mention feature emerging from unplanned user behavior), then support and extend the pattern rather than design it upfront.
- He claims discipline-specific mastery (engineering vs. design) persists even as AI lets people cross into adjacent disciplines, because each discipline has a distinct way of framing and solving problems that AI tool access doesn't replicate.
- On developing judgment in less-experienced employees using AI tools: Dorsey frames it as habit formation (citing James Clear's Atomic Habits — small, incrementally larger practice sessions) rather than a structural or governance answer.
- Dorsey's three self-defined jobs as a leader: set the environment (purpose, culture, norms), ensure decisions get made with full context (not by him personally — "that's a single point of failure"), and periodically raise the bar on what the organization assumes is possible.
- His advice to leaders outside tech: build something yourself with AI before trying to deploy it organization-wide — "you're not going to be able to see this within your business... unless you can see it within yourself."
