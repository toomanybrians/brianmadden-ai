# Import AI 480: Self-sustaining space economies; AI for science progress, and Meta builds perfect hands for a superintelligence

## Insights

- Physical Superintelligence (PSI), using a non-public Anthropic model, produced a proof of the 2008 Aaronson-Ambainis quantum computing conjecture, with Lean verifying each step; the system then improved on the result twice on related open problems — Jack Clark frames this as evidence AI is now routinely extending the frontier of human knowledge, not just matching it.
- Meta Reality Labs published Tactus, a thin textile glove with capacitive tactile sensors and IMU pose tracking designed to capture fine-grained hand-manipulation data (force, grip) without disrupting natural movement — built for training robots/AI on dexterous manipulation.
- A 2016 NASA Kennedy Space Center paper outlines how to bootstrap a self-sustaining lunar manufacturing economy with surprisingly low launch mass (12–41 metric tons), progressing through six manufacturing "generations" from basic gases/alloys to local chip fabrication, using robotic "robonauts" that shift from teleoperated to increasingly autonomous as the colony matures.
- That same NASA paper underestimated AI progress, predicting "mouse-level" robot intelligence by 2030 and human-level by 2050 (per Moravec), whereas Clark notes current AI is already around "monkey-level" and approaching human-level in the 2020s.
- Core Automation (founded by ex-OpenAI/Google staff) demonstrated distributed training of a 32B-parameter MoE model across two data center clusters 9,000 miles apart with different GPU types (GB300 vs B300), syncing in staggered 50-step fragments — positioned as a step toward reducing dependence on single hyperscale data centers and eventually enabling orbital/satellite-based training.
- Core Automation argues the real bottleneck in AI scaling isn't chip supply but power, grid infrastructure, and land — and that stitching together disparate "compute islands" into unified training runs is the harder unsolved problem.

## Quote

> The biggest bottleneck in deployment of accelerated compute is not the chips themselves but power, grid equipment and land.
