# A plan for how the new Congress saves us from AI disaster

## Insights

- Legislative strategy for AI regulation faces a structural ceiling: 60 votes can overcome a filibuster, but overriding a presidential veto requires 67 Senate votes and 290 House votes — a much higher bar than current public concern likely supports without further "watering down."
- Four near-term levers exist even under a veto threat: bipartisan non-controversial bills (e.g., securing model weights from theft, export controls, chip smuggling enforcement), attaching provisions to must-pass funding bills, Senate confirmation leverage over AI-relevant nominees, and oversight hearings compelling sworn testimony from AI company staff.
- The piece argues current measures are insufficient — real safety requires slowing AI development while models are near human-level to allow testing and alignment work, and that without this "breathing room" misaligned superintelligent systems capable of concealing misalignment are more likely than not.
- Anticipates a future "warning shot" (cyberattack on infrastructure, uncontrollable rogue AI, AI-caused pandemic) as the likely catalyst for supermajority political will, and argues safety advocates should pre-draft legislation now rather than scrambling after the fact.
- Proposes concrete technical-policy mechanisms: banning "recursive self-improvement" by restricting companies to using only models at least six months old and already public; banning high-capability performance absent chain-of-thought transparency; and requiring minimum shares of compute devoted to external inference (dial-able like an interest rate) rather than blanket training bans.
- Flags that the US AI regulatory body (CAISSI) is drastically underfunded/understaffed compared to the UK's equivalent (AI Security Institute) — roughly 50x less spending adjusted for economy size — creating a capacity gap that could force US reliance on foreign assessments during a crisis.
- Warns that concentrating more regulatory power in the executive branch raises its own risks of abuse (e.g., politically motivated strong-arming of companies), and that any emergency federal AI legislation should avoid preempting state-level AI rules already in effect.
