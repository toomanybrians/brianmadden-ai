## Insights

- Helsinki logician Jan von Plato spent since 2017 manually deciphering 1,009 pages of Kurt Gödel's 1940s notebooks (written partly in obsolete Gabelsberger shorthand), progressing at roughly one page per week.
- After enlisting Claude in September (on the suggestion of computer scientists met at an AI/theorem-proving conference), the AI produced a comprehensive 650-page digest of the entire corpus within a couple of days — a synthesis task von Plato calls "impossible to do for human beings" because humans can't hold all cross-references across a thousand pages in mind at once.
- Claude's analysis surfaced that Gödel had proved hundreds of previously unknown theorems, including dozens anticipating results not discovered by others until the 1970s–80s, and 14 unexplored logic themes.
- Von Plato tasked Claude with checking whether Gödel's notes contained a proof of the independence of the axiom of choice (a result for which Paul Cohen won the 1966 Fields Medal, which Gödel claimed to have already proved but couldn't reconstruct); Claude found Gödel was "almost" there, missing one correct definition he'd actually used two years earlier.
- Claude extracted 663 candidate theorems from the workbooks; when checked in the formal proof assistant Coq, ~400 verified as stated, ~200 needed repair/refinement, and ~50 were false — matching marginal notes where Gödel himself had written "falsch!" (incorrect).
- An outside expert (Joel David Hamkins) cautions that attribution is complicated — "how much is Claude and how much is Gödel is a job for the historians" — and that having related ideas in notes isn't equivalent to having completed/claimed the proof.
