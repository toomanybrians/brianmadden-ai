# The education of an AI skeptic

## Insights

- Reviewing 75+ studies (mostly RCTs) on AI in education, the author found 49 broadly positive, 16 mixed/null, and 10 negative — a reversal of his earlier (Jan 2026) pessimism about AI's effect on learning.
- The consistent pattern across positive studies: AI helps when it's scaffolded (e.g., answers withheld until a student attempts the problem first, or feedback staged after independent work); unscaffolded/unsupervised use tends to hurt outcomes.
- Writing-related findings split in a notable way: AI assistance improves the quality of a given piece of writing (especially for weaker writers), but *unrestricted* AI use during drafting can make a person's *later, unaided* writing worse — a "cognitive debt" effect — while "think first, AI later" sequencing avoids this and even helps.
- One contrarian finding: in a Middlebury College study, students given ChatGPT access for a writing task didn't just offload — they reallocated time toward research/conceptualization and performed better on unaided tests and essays a week later, suggesting AI can free cognitive bandwidth rather than erode it, depending on design.
- Scaffolding/motivation case studies (Harvard physics, Chilean economics course) show that AI tutors have no effect until students are explicitly taught how to use them productively — mere access isn't enough.
- Pre-registered studies (14 of 75 confirmed) showed almost identical positive/negative ratios to the full sample, used as evidence against major publication bias in this research area.
- Frames current real-world AI-in-classroom practice (uncritical acceptance or unenforceable bans) as the actual source of poor outcomes and media negativity, distinct from what well-designed/scaffolded AI use produces in controlled studies.

## Quote

> Unenforceable AI bans make it hard to [do] the right thing, and easy to do the wrong thing. It's not fair to students.
