# The AI Economy Is Booming, but Who's Actually Creating Value?

## Insights

- Ramp/Revelio Labs data on ~22,000 US companies found heaviest generative-AI adopters grew white-collar headcount 10.2% (entry-level +12%) over two years post-adoption, while low-intensity adopters saw no significant change — contradicting the assumption that AI investment necessarily shrinks headcount. Gains were concentrated in smaller, tech-sector, venture-backed firms and took 6-12 months to appear; correlational, not causal.
- Bain's Automation and AI Pathfinder Survey (951 companies) found nearly 40% of firms measuring AI cost savings saw under 10% reduction despite targeting 11-20%, yet 90% still plan to increase AI budgets; 44% intend to fund new AI investment from savings generated by prior automation programs that themselves often underdelivered — described as a "circular bet." Only 7% run fully autonomous agents in production, with human approval/exception-handling adding real, often unaccounted-for cost.
- BCG research: companies expect to roughly double AI spend (0.8% to 1.7% of revenue); 72% of CEOs see themselves as primary AI decision-maker; 94% will keep investing even without near-term returns; half of CEOs tie their job stability to AI strategy. Separately, only 5% of companies are "future-built" and achieving AI value at scale, 35% beginning to scale value, 60% seeing little material value despite heavy investment — future-built firms report 5x revenue increase and 3x cost reduction vs peers. McKinsey: 40% of large-company respondents are scaling AI agents, but only 6% report AI contributing ≥5% of EBIT.
- Stanford research (Aug 2026 revision) found employment for workers 22-25 in AI-exposed occupations is 19% below where it would be absent AI exposure, though no evidence of broad economy-wide displacement; Yale's Budget Lab finds no clear AI-attributable pattern in overall labor market data — suggesting effects are concentrated in specific populations/occupations rather than uniform.
- Ed Zitron's critique ("AI Doesn't Have ROI") is presented as raising legitimate questions: token-based pricing, subsidized subscriptions, unpredictable compute costs, and human correction effort may obscure the true operating cost of AI, potentially making the investment boom a capital misallocation rather than a value-creating revolution.
- Piece distinguishes "optimized AI" (improving an existing business's performance) from "innovative AI" (challenging the business's founding assumptions to create new outcomes), and argues organizations are mostly inserting AI into old org/process designs rather than redesigning end-to-end "work charts" around desired outcomes.
- Raises a stated concern: automating entry-level/foundational tasks could erode the pipeline through which workers develop judgment and expertise needed to eventually supervise more intelligent systems.

## Quote

> Companies reinvest projected automation savings that haven't fully materialized... This creates the appearance of accelerating demand and growth. — Brian Solis
