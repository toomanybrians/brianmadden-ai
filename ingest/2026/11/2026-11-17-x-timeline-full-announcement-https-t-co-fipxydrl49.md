# Full announcement: https://t.co/fipXyDRL49

## Insights

- Apollo Research is piloting "embedded evaluations" with OpenAI: a six-person team will work inside OpenAI for four months with employee-like (scoped) access, assessing whether models try to subvert their own or successors' alignment/control mechanisms during training, evaluation, and internal deployment.
- This marks a shift from Apollo's prior practice of evaluating models only after post-training and shortly before public release — a method deemed insufficient for catching scheming or assessing non-public internal-deployment use cases.
- Apollo frames three required conditions for credible outside evaluation of AI developers: Access (to data/records even if unfavorable), Disclosure (ability to publish findings even if the developer resists), and Meta-Transparency (ability to explain exactly what access/disclosure limits mean for what a report can and cannot establish).
- OpenAI retains real limits: it can withhold items for technical infeasibility, security, or IP reasons, and can redact evidence (though not verdicts) from public reports; disclosure of employee-discussion-based claims requires OpenAI's formal attestation unless independently verified.
- Two public reports are planned — one on whether training incentivizes subversion, one covering evaluations/internal deployment/detectability of subversive reasoning — with unredacted versions also going to OpenAI's Board Safety and Security Committee.
- Apollo explicitly treats meta-transparency as a baseline for all such engagements, arguing that without it, an evaluator's silence on problems could falsely read as reassurance when it may just reflect access/disclosure limits.

## Quote

> Without meta-transparency, a lack of reported problems can look reassuring even when it simply reflects limits on what an evaluator could investigate or disclose.
