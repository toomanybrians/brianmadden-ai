# How Washington keeps helping China catch up in AI | Luisa Rodriguez

## Fit with Brian's work

**Fit: partial.** The episode's core subject — US-China chip export policy and AI racing dynamics — sits outside Brian's enterprise/knowledge-work focus, but one thread (model distillation, frontier-model security, and compute/power constraints) feeds directly into his open-weight-planning-floor and compute-scarcity arguments.

- **Open-weight planning floor, fragility angle.** Brian treats Chinese open-weight models (Qwen, GLM, Kimi) as the "planning floor" that survives an AI bubble pop. This episode shows that floor is partly dependent on industrial-scale distillation from US frontier models (Alibaba reportedly exchanged 151M messages with Claude in three months), which is a geopolitical and technical dependency he hasn't fully priced into that thesis — if Anthropic-style anti-distillation defenses tighten further (already cutting harvestable data 90-99%, per the episode), the floor's capability trajectory could shift.
- **Compute-availability risk, new data point.** His "compute scarcity is physical, not just financial" thread (power-plant permitting, grid queues) gets a concrete number: a claimed 44-gigawatt US power shortfall by 2028 ("like adding another California"), against China adding roughly six times the US's new generating capacity over five years — sharpens his argument that compute-availability, not price, is becoming the constraint.
- **Frontier model security as unaddressed governance gap.** The claim that top AI labs sit around security level 2-3 out of a 1-5 scale (versus the 5 needed to deter nation-state theft) is adjacent to his "weights-as-black-box" and disclosure-blackout notes — different angle (espionage/theft vs. hidden capabilities) but same underlying worry that deployers and overseers don't actually know or control what's inside the systems they run.
- **Slowdown-as-policy-choice echoes his Amodei-pacing skepticism.** The episode's argument that "the size of the lead is a policy choice" and that a slowdown could be absorbed without ceding ground to China parallels Brian's open question about whether frontier-lab "pace the frontier" rhetoric is a genuine safety signal or a strategic/competitive move — this is a different actor (US policy, not labs) making a structurally similar claim, worth noting as a nearby data point rather than a direct contribution.

## Insights

- A researcher (Institute for Progress, per the episode) estimates distillation from American frontier models closes roughly half the compute gap between Chinese and US models — equivalent to giving Chinese labs four times the chips they actually have.
- Chinese AI companies' six leading firms earn collectively about 10% of what OpenAI and Anthropic earn combined, despite large user bases, because most open-source their best models rather than monetizing them directly.
- A Forethought researcher estimate cited in the episode: a leading US lab's compute edge could let it do AI research almost twice as fast as its Chinese counterpart — implying slowing down to redirect capacity toward safety/security work wouldn't necessarily cost the lead.
- Export-control enforcement specifics given as evidence it "works where enforced": requiring export permits in Malaysia reportedly cut Chinese chip-server imports from Malaysia/Singapore by about 80%.
- A technical lead on Alibaba's Qwen models (cited by name, spelling unverified: Lin Junyang) put the odds of China overtaking top US AI labs in the next 3-5 years at under 20%, citing compute as the limiting factor; Zhipu's chief AI scientist (Tang Jie) argued the gap may be widening because many of the best US models stay private.
- China is described as running a different race — ubiquity and physical deployment (354,000 industrial robots installed last year, 59% of world total; 97% of humanoid robots sold globally in H1 this year made by Chinese companies) rather than frontier-model supremacy.

## Pushback and disagreement

- The host directly challenges the "can't slow down, China will catch up" argument by noting its loudest proponents (NVIDIA's Jensen Huang, former White House AI czar David Sacks) have financial incentives that align suspiciously well with their policy positions — Huang sells the chips in question, and Sacks reportedly held stakes in 449 AI-related companies while shaping policy.
- The host weighs the counter-argument (made by researchers, not just industry) that cutting China off from chips/models accelerates China's push for full self-sufficiency, citing a CSIS analysis that broad US controls have already sped up China's effort to de-risk from American technology — then concludes China was pursuing self-sufficiency since 2015 regardless, so the timing argument still favors maintaining controls now.
