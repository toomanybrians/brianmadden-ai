# Source: https://t.co/72JgTpz8Cy

## Insights

- A Stanford study revisited 50 widely-cited LLM research findings across 20 models (released July 2024–September 2026) and found 44% have "expired" (effect largely gone) and 34% have significantly weakened within about 15 months of original publication on average.
- Capability-related claims decay fastest: citation fabrication, letter-counting, arithmetic errors, and lost-in-the-middle context failures have mostly disappeared in newer models — GPT-3.5-era fabrication rates of ~55-73% dropped to 0-1% in recent models like Claude Sonnet 5.5 and GPT-6.1 Sol.
- Findings about model preferences and judgment persist or even strengthen over time, including sycophancy (praise matching user's stated preference), self-preference bias in LLM-as-judge setups, political lean (left), and nonuniform random-number generation — these are framed as preference/alignment issues rather than capability limits.
- Persistence varies widely even within the same model generation: among July–September 2026 models, "expired claim" counts ranged from 11 (DeepSeek V4 Pro) to 30 (GPT-6.1 Sol), showing release date alone doesn't predict which old findings still hold.
- The researchers argue policies like ICLR/ICML desk-rejecting papers for hallucinated citations are increasingly poor proxies for detecting AI-generated writing, since fabrication rates have fallen sharply in current models.
- Broader implication highlighted by @emollick: much "conventional wisdom" about LLM behavior is already outdated, and claims from older model generations need continual re-testing rather than being treated as stable facts.

## Quote

> Beliefs formed around an earlier generation of models need to be tested again.
