# How Journalists Uncover What's Really Happening with the AI Act (with Maximilian Henning)

## Insights

- Journalism covering EU tech policy has shifted from open-web articles to paywalled, specialist subscription newsletters, because content on the open web gets scraped immediately (an AI-related dynamic) and loses commercial value — this allows deeper beat specialization but reduces broad public reach and cross-outlet amplification of stories.
- AI's effect on this journalist's actual workflow is limited: used mainly as a search/synthesis tool and for scanning long documents (e.g., thousand-page amendment sets), but the core of "pro" policy journalism — sourcing undisclosed information from people and documents — remains largely unaided by AI.
- There's active debate over terminology like "AI lab" vs. "AI company/corporation" — the newsletter host argues "lab" is outdated and misleading given these are now trillion-dollar-valued corporations, while the reporter defends "lab" partly for brevity (character/word limits) and to distinguish model-developing firms from general "AI companies."
- Coverage is skewed toward Brussels/Commission-level stories (safety incidents, frontier company requests-for-information, omnibus/deregulation debates) and away from member-state enforcement, prohibited AI practices (in force since Feb 2025), and national-language reporting — leaving real-world/local AI Act violations under-covered.
- EU AI Act narrative has swung in distinct waves: initial safety framing (2023) → deregulation/"AI Act is killing competitiveness" narrative culminating in the omnibus fight → renewed safety focus after incidents (e.g., undisclosed OpenAI safety incidents surfaced via Reuters reporting, sexualized deepfake controversy prompting even rule additions rather than simplification).
- Much Brussels policy news is sourced from informally circulated documents ("non-papers") shared via WhatsApp (not Signal), including a revealed Hungarian non-paper proposing embedding EU safety teams inside frontier AI companies; the Commission has begun sending requests for information to OpenAI, Anthropic, and Meta on safety, evaluation, and copyright/training-data practices under the AI Act's August enforcement powers.
