# Was I wrong about open models & cyber risks?

## Insights

- Author revisits his earlier claim that open-weight model risks were "fine," prompted by a wave of critical vulnerability disclosures ("Black October") and incidents like Binance's hack, testing whether open models drive cyber harm.
- Argues GLM-5.3, not Claude Mythos, turned out to be the model that crossed the feared capability threshold, yet no mass societal destabilization followed — suggesting earlier alarm (e.g., from Anthropic, Rob Wiblin) was overstated relative to what's materialized.
- Cites Epoch AI's use of GLM-5.3 to find a real Firefox exploit as evidence that discovering vulnerabilities is only one step; turning a found exploit into actual societal harm requires additional conditions (delivery, motive, capability alignment).
- Contends current evidence points to closed models playing a bigger role in recent hacks than open models, and that threat-actor behavior (existing "business" incentives/MOs) better predicts who exploits new vulnerabilities than model openness does.
- Frames the real dynamic as an industry-wide forced transition to a new, more adversarial security posture — expensive and disruptive, but closer to an acceleration of existing exponential cyber-vulnerability trends than a sudden step-change caused by open-weight AI specifically.
- Notes that in the near term, compute costs and limited availability make open models less practical for attackers than closed ones, though he expects this cost gap to narrow as compute becomes more abundant.
- Frames cyber risk as a broader societal/industry problem that AI companies alone can't solve, and as the first of many AI-driven transitions requiring coordination beyond the tech industry.
