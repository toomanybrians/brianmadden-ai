# The image comes from one of my first posts on OneUsefulThing right after the rel

## Insights

- Mollick frames the post-2 centuries of technological change as a "Long Singularity" — a century-plus-long period of continuous acceleration rather than a single sudden event.
- Argues the 1950–2023 stretch has been unusually stable compared to the 1903–1969 stretch: of 270 jobs in the 1950 US census, only elevator operator has been eliminated by technology, whereas 1903–1969 saw entire industries appear and vanish.
- Uses an age-equivalence comparison (someone born in 1903 was 66 in 1969; someone born in 1957 is 66 in 2023) to argue 2023 feels more like 1957 than like a moment of 1969-style upheaval — suggesting recent decades have been comparatively "lazy" in disruption despite appearances of fast change.
- Suggests AI progress may be running at an order of magnitude per year, markedly faster than Moore's Law's two-year doubling pace, which could break this long period of relative stability.
- Notes a personal/informal experiment (Typical.me) where an AI trained on someone's tweets fabricated plausible-sounding citations, fooling even the author — illustrating how convincingly AI can hallucinate in a person's own voice.
- Concludes that the future is genuinely unpredictable at this stage (can't confidently say if/when AI replaces specific professions like radiologists), and advises flexibility and experimentation as the main sound response.
