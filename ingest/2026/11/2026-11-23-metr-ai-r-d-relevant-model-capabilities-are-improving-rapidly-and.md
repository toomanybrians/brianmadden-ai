# AI R&D-relevant model capabilities are improving rapidly and are getting harder to measure

## Insights

- METR argues current public models already match or beat skilled humans on well-specified, cheaply-verifiable tasks (coding, optimization), which undercuts prior safety arguments based on "models just aren't capable enough yet."
- On tasks with large pools of human experts (programming contests, NanoGPT speedrunning), frontier models hit 96th–98th percentile but still lose to a handful of top humans — strong but not superhuman across the board.
- Models show a consistent weakness in "judgment": tasks with slow or fuzzy feedback loops, where quality can't be cheaply verified, remain a clear gap versus human experts — including models overrating work by equally or more capable models when acting as judges.
- A new "Lifecycle Cost" metric (cost to build/run/score a task as an RL environment) correlates with model difficulty — costlier-to-verify tasks are harder for models even controlling for task length, suggesting benchmarks may overstate real-world readiness since labs likely train on similar cheap, verifiable environments.
- Time-horizon capability (how long a task a model can reliably complete) is doubling roughly every 3.6 months across combined suites, consistent with recent OpenAI-reported internal research trends; naive extrapolation puts models at 200-hour task horizons by mid-2027.
- Using an IRT-based capability index (MCI), METR finds no clear overall acceleration across the public frontier, though some AI-developer-specific trend changes appear (methodologically uncertain) — meaning the "takeoff speed" question remains genuinely unresolved from current evidence.

## Quote

> We think that current public evidence is insufficient to rule out drastic AI R&D acceleration in the next couple of years.
