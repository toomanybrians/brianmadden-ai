## Insights

- A speaker cites exit polling: 56% said AI and data centers were a crucial or major factor in their midterm vote; 76% opposed a data center being built in their community; 57% said AI would do more to hurt society versus 24% who said it would help.
- The backlash was geographically concentrated in states with heavy data-center buildout — Texas, Ohio, Georgia, Arizona — and politicians who campaigned directly against data centers (cited: Husted in Ohio) saw it work as a cross-partisan wedge issue.
- A speaker estimates roughly 45 gigawatts of compute capacity was expected to come online in 2026, with 10-15 gigawatts of that now in question specifically because a Texas-based moratorium on data center construction may not be lifted as investors had assumed.
- Investors reacted with what one speaker calls catastrophizing — fears of constrained, more expensive compute capacity hurting not just frontier labs but the broader "AI trade" ecosystem (chip makers, neo-clouds, resellers) — though Nasdaq futures reportedly dropped and then bounced back the next morning.
- One speaker argues a slowdown in data-center buildout isn't necessarily bad for markets: current CapEx races reflect a "collective rationality problem" where firms overbuild to avoid being left behind, and pacing construction to match actual economic usefulness (diffusion, adoption) rather than a race to superintelligence could be healthier for the sector.
- The same speaker distinguishes two bad outcomes — creating "dangerous AI before economically useful AI" (bad) versus a slowdown that forces better alignment between build-out speed and adoption (not necessarily bad) — and says it's "not at all obvious" current deceleration falls into the harmful category.
- A speaker argues AI's political unpopularity isn't purely a messaging problem: AI's basic public pitch ("might kill everyone, will probably take your job, but might give you cheaper energy") is inherently unappealing, independent of who delivers it.
- One speaker notes that venture capitalists often argue people's "revealed preference" (heavy usage of Facebook, Amazon, ChatGPT) proves underlying trust, but argues the election results show usage and trust/approval are not the same thing — people can be heavy users and still want the industry restrained.
- A speaker flags a specific data point for 2028: AI-safety-aligned money (effective-altruist and Anthropic-adjacent donors, newly wealthy from an anticipated Anthropic IPO) is emerging as a serious political funding force within the Democratic primary, potentially putting Dario Amodei or OpenAI-aligned figures in play as political actors.
