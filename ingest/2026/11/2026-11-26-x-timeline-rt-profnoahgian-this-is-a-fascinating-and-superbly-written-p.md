## Insights

- Anthropic's Levent Alpöge used internal, unreleased Claude models plus large compute allowances to independently solve a string of famous unsolved math problems in 2026, culminating in a claimed proof of the Riemann hypothesis — a problem experts like Terence Tao had said was decades away from AI's reach.
- Alpöge describes his method as acting as a "liaison" between swarms of Claude agents and human mathematician collaborators: he directs the models toward a problem, the agents generate thousands of pages of machine-native ("math-slop") output, and he translates that output (with LLM help) into human-readable proofs — a workflow he calls "AI psychosis."
- This points to a new mode of knowledge work where a single domain expert with privileged model access and compute can out-produce traditional large research teams, working across far more subfields than any one human could master unaided.
- The piece documents direct corporate rivalry shaping the research itself: OpenAI allegedly raced Anthropic's informal effort to claim the Navier-Stokes result first, then dumped 700+ additional proofs, which Alpöge felt scooped his own in-progress work and fed his distrust of leaks between companies.
- Alpöge's achievements and unconventional public disclosure style (terse social posts instead of papers) have polarized the mathematics community — some see him as advancing a healthier human-AI collaborative model, others as recklessly accelerating the displacement of traditional mathematical labor and norms.
- Tension exists over institutional framing: Anthropic and Alpöge call his math work a "hobby" separate from his job, but outside mathematicians dispute this given his use of internal models and company compute resources.
