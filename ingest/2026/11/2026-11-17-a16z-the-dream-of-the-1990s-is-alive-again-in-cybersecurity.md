# The Dream of the 1990s Is Alive Again in Cybersecurity

## Insights

- Argues AI coding/research models (cites Opus 4.5/4.6) have radically changed the economics of offensive security: finding and exploiting vulnerabilities in widely-audited, foundational software now takes orders of magnitude less expertise, time, and cost than before.
- Predicts a return of self-propagating "Internet worm" attacks, but with commercial motives (stealing AI inference API keys, ransomware) replacing the self-propagation-for-its-own-sake goals of early-2000s worms.
- Claims enterprises face a new "patching problem" analogous to Windows XP-era exposure: AI compresses exploit development time faster than organizations can test and roll out patches, forcing faster automatic-update practices despite breakage risk and unmaintained legacy software.
- Suggests a market opening for startups building "hardened infrastructure" components (an OpenBSD-like model) and for AI-native security categories: agentic-malware-focused endpoint security, agent-specific identity/authorization tools, and open-source supply-chain protection against risks from agentic coding pulling in packages indiscriminately.
- Recommends security teams shift from periodic/compliance-driven pentesting to continuous, no-scope red-teaming, and reframe "defense in depth" around detection/response speed, since AI-enabled attackers can now bypass even fully-functioning layered controls.
- Frames this as a full architectural "reboot" of the security stack comparable to the cloud transition, driven by AI changing enterprise technology use across identity, endpoint, and software supply chain simultaneously.

## Quote

> Opus 4.5/4.6 marked the beginning of a radical change in the economics of offense.
