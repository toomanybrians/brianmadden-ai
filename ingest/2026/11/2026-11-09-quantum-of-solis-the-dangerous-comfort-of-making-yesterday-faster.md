# The Dangerous Comfort of Making Yesterday Faster

## Insights

- Solis names an "iteration trap" (coined with Dave Wright in *Infinite*): organizations aim powerful new AI at existing workflows — summarizing, producing, reporting, support — and mistake visible efficiency gains for genuine transformation.
- Distinguishes three tiers: iteration (doing the same things better), innovation (doing new things that create new value), and disruption (new things at scale that make old things obsolete) — most current AI deployment sits in the iteration bucket.
- Uses "better candles don't become light bulbs" and the Kodak example to argue that success creates operating systems (budgets, metrics, org charts) that reject anything not resembling the past, even when companies had early access to disruptive tech.
- Argues companies that define themselves by purpose/value delivered (Michelin, Lego, John Deere, Dyson, Tesla) retain permission to evolve into new categories, while those defined by current product portfolios just iterate on it.
- Draws on the iPhone origin story (Jobs reportedly excluding phone-industry experts — "We're not building a phone") to illustrate how domain expertise can become a boundary rather than an asset for reimagining a category.
- Cites Google's research on team performance to argue psychological safety — not intelligence or pedigree — is the key differentiator enabling people to challenge assumptions and explore new ideas without punishment.
- Proposes a practical discipline: continuously scan for weak "signals," ask what assumption they challenge and what opportunity they open, then run small 30–60 day experiments rather than large transformation programs.
- Poses four self-audit questions for leaders: what are we optimized to protect, where are we mistaking legacy for performance, what are we underinvesting in because it threatens the current model, and where are we calling something "high risk" when it's really "hard to change."
