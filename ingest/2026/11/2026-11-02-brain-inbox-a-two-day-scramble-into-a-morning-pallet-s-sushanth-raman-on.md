# "A two-day scramble into a morning": Pallet's Sushanth Raman on AI agents in a supply-chain crisis

## Insights

- Disruption costs often hide in contract timing mismatches: fuel or input costs can rise immediately while customer pricing adjusts on a separate schedule, creating a margin gap that's easy to overlook — even in domestic-only supply chains.
- AI agents are framed as useful for assembling scattered operational data (BOMs, inventory, POs, contracts) to quantify exposure and compute "days of cover," but deliberately kept out of decisions involving scarce allocation or costly commitments — those stay with named humans.
- A concrete production deployment (Knight-Swift) reclassifies the economics of automation: handling 20,000+ shipment emails/day didn't speed up trucks, but it flattened handling cost during volume spikes and freed staff for exceptions — framed explicitly as service improvement, not cost-of-delivery improvement.
- Agents are described as needing built-in epistemic rules for conflicting data sources (carrier email vs. tracking feed vs. internal system), escalating discrepancies rather than resolving them unilaterally — governance means predefined autonomy boundaries, traceability, and human override.
- A risk specific to multi-company AI adoption: if many firms' agents converge on identifying the same alternate supplier/capacity, that convergence itself can worsen scarcity — raises the need to distinguish quoted vs. reserved vs. confirmed capacity before acting.
- Readiness for contingency plans is proposed as testable: simulate a multi-week delay with real data, time the team's actual decision speed, and verify backup suppliers have shipped real recent volume (vs. "dual sourcing on paper" that shares the same upstream risk).

## Quote

> Deciding which key account gets shorted, or approving a substitution, stays with named people. — Sushanth Raman
