# How to Win Bots and Influence Purchases

## Insights

- Shopping/task agents (Muse, Instinct, Grok Bot, Dots) draw from narrow option sets — Muse searches its own catalog first (max 50 items) and often checks only ~3 product pages via browser — meaning a product picked by one agent has a 73% chance of being picked by others, and all four converge on the same pick in ~25% of cases.
- Agents shift recommendations sharply based on specificity of user need: broad queries default to cheaper/generic options (e.g., Shark over Dyson, Canva over Adobe), but adding persona or use-case detail (professional output, daily use, team templates) flips the pick toward premium/specialized products.
- Agents are built to be "ruthless optimizers" on total cost — factoring shipping, returns, warranties — and will choose a pricier, more trusted/authorized seller 72% of the time over a cheaper one; discounts under 10% don't move them, and discounts over 50% trigger suspicion.
- Agent-readability matters: having clear product pages, APIs, and MCP integrations (vs. restrictive policies like Midjourney's anti-automation stance) determines whether an agent can even evaluate or transact with a product.
- Individual agents behave like distinct customers with their own biases (Grok Bot favors Amazon, Muse favors Walmart/Best Buy, Instinct skeptical of ratings, Dots weighs professional reviews) and these preferences are expected to strengthen as agents strike direct commercial partnerships with retailers.
- Checkout friction is a major bottleneck — agents completed checkout 81% of the time on guest-checkout stores but stalled entirely on login-required sites — driving adoption of agent-friendly payment rails like Stripe Link, Shop Pay, and OpenAI's new ChatGPT Wallet.
