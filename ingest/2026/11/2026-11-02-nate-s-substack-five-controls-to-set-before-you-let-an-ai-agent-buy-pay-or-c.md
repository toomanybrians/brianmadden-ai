# Five controls to set before you let an AI agent buy, pay, or cancel anything for you, so it finishes the job without spending money you didn't approve.

## Insights

- Nate frames an emerging tension: the same AI capabilities that make it easier to break into websites (referenced via "Black October") also need to let legitimate AI agents act on a person's behalf (booking, buying, managing accounts).
- He predicts the internet will become increasingly "uneven" for agents through 2027, splitting into businesses that build dependable agent-acceptance systems, businesses that let agents muddle through existing websites, and businesses that refuse agent access outright.
- Businesses that make legitimate agent activity easy to recognize and accept will gain leverage, because both consumers and other businesses will need to route through them.
- A concrete failure case is described: an AI agent perfectly planned a trip but froze at a hotel's booking page because the business hadn't opened that door to agents — forcing the human to either finish manually or switch to a business that does support agent booking.
- This friction is already reshaping purchasing behavior: which companies get paid depends on whether their systems accommodate agent transactions, not just on model quality.
- The piece references (without detailing in the extract) several named trust/verification mechanisms — Cloudflare's signed agents, Google's AP2, Stripe's payment tokens, a Visa pilot, and Meta's Sentinel — as different partial answers to agent authentication and payment trust.

## Quote

> A smarter model isn't going to unlock a door a business has decided to close.
