# Iron Makes Iron

## Insights

- METR's study found Anthropic is improving model capabilities on "well-specified tasks" at roughly twice the rate measured between March 2024 and October 2025, and Anthropic's S-1 disclosed it may soon meet its own Responsible Scaling Policy threshold for "dramatically accelerated progress attributable to AI."
- The author distinguishes "weak recursion" (AI used as an input to build better AI, a historically common pattern — like iron making iron or software making software) from "strong recursion" (AI autonomously planning and executing the development of its own successor without human oversight); he argues current evidence only supports the former.
- The METR findings describe acceleration on tasks specified by human researchers under human-set objectives — a sign of weak, not strong, recursion — and the acceleration appears tied substantially to scaled inference compute rather than pure algorithmic self-improvement.
- Key bottleneck for strong recursion is "taste" — discernment in open-ended research (framing problems, allocating scarce compute, judging promising directions) — which has improved much more slowly in AI systems than measurable skills like coding or cybersecurity.
- Proposes that governance should target entities/companies (compute allocation transparency, share of unreviewed merged code, etc.) rather than individual models, since strong recursion is a firm-level phenomenon involving capital allocation and management control, not just model capability.
- Warns that conflating weak and strical recursion risks policy overreach — potentially banning ordinary AI-assisted R&D in fields like biomedicine and chemicals design — and draws a parallel to how centralized nuclear regulation stifled civilian benefits while weapons stockpiles still grew.

## Quote

> No amount of technological progress is worth the forfeiture of practical human control over AI companies.
