# The avalanche that hit math is coming for AI development

## Fit with Brian's work

**Fit: partial.** The episode is a solo essay on AI research automation and recursive self-improvement (RSI) timelines—not enterprise knowledge work—but one thread, the pace and shape of frontier capability acceleration, bears directly on several of Brian's open questions (the pop timing, the Move 37 generalization speed, compute scarcity); the rest (mathematical proof mechanics, evolutionary analogy, continual-learning technicalities) doesn't engage anything he's building.

- **Directly feeds "How fast does the Move 37 moment generalize?"** (his open question). Dwarkesh's math trajectory—2023 models couldn't reliably add 4-digit numbers, 2024 barely passed high-school competitions, 2025 IMO gold, 2026 a solved Millennium Prize problem—is concrete evidence for how fast a domain can flip from "toy" to "superhuman," which is the exact mechanism behind his "which phase hits the VP of Marketing" question.
- **Complicates the bubble-pop planning-floor thesis.** Brian's published position is to plan for open-weight/Sonnet-class models as the floor that survives a pop, treating further frontier progress as a bonus not a dependency. Dwarkesh's claim that AI labs must quadruple R&D compute and double workforce yearly just to hold pace, plus Anthropic/OpenAI's reported internal token spend exploding (one example: $1,000/day to $7,000/day per heavy researcher in three months), is new fuel for the unsustainable-investment read behind "the pop is coming"—it reads as evidence the chase is real and accelerating, not slowing.
- **Cuts against "if AI progress stopped today" framing.** That published position (if progress stopped, 5+ years of enterprise transformation remain) assumed a plausible stall. Dwarkesh argues the opposite is likelier near-term: a "spark" scenario compressing 3-6 years of progress into one calendar year by 2027-28, or a software-only singularity. Doesn't invalidate the enterprise-readiness argument, but it's a data point Brian hasn't weighed against it.
- **Adds a number to the compute-scarcity thread.** The claim that AI labs need to quadruple R&D compute and double headcount annually just to maintain current progress rates is a concrete mechanism for why compute demand could outstrip supply even before any second-brain-driven enterprise demand spike—relevant to his token-scarcity argument but about lab-side compute, not enterprise-side.

## Insights

- Dwarkesh states his median estimate for AI as good as the best human AI researchers at AI research is 2028, assuming labs proceed without self-imposed or government-imposed slowdown; he puts 25% probability on this not happening until after 2032, largely hinging on whether continual learning gets solved.
- METR (an independent evaluator embedded three months inside Anthropic) reportedly found Anthropic's AI progress rate is 2x what it was a year ago, with a majority of that acceleration attributed to Anthropic's own use of AI for R&D (claim per Dwarkesh, sourcing METR).
- OpenAI reportedly reported its heaviest-using researchers (top 10%) went from ~$1,000/day of internal coding-agent spend to over $7,000/day between early and late summer 2026.
- Anthropic reportedly reported Claude now leads 26% of its own AI R&D work, up from under 1% in February 2026.
- A month before the episode, OpenAI reportedly solved a Millennium Prize problem in 88 hours—cited as evidence of rapid, verification-heavy math capability growth, though Dwarkesh notes this is disproportionately in verification-laden areas (counterexamples) rather than generating new theory.
- Dwarkesh frames two possible post-automation trajectories: a "software-only singularity" (AI swarm improves itself without more compute, reaching physical hardware limits within months) versus a "spark" (3-6 years of progress compressed into one year, then stalling until more compute is built)—he assigns roughly 50/50 odds between them.
- Even the more modest "spark" scenario would compress a jump as large as "GPT-3.5 to GPT-6 Astra" into roughly 12 months, starting around 2027-2028, per Dwarkesh's framing.
- Dwarkesh cites the AI lab Magic as having pretrained a model matching leading open-source models (comparable to DeepSeek-class) using half the compute of the original GPT-3 training run—roughly $500,000 at today's prices—as evidence pretraining costs keep collapsing.
- Dwarkesh revises an earlier position: he previously doubted automated AI research was near because models lack continual/on-the-job learning; he now believes scaling in-context learning (effectively multi-month-long sessions) may substitute for true weight-updating continual learning, without a conceptual breakthrough being required.
- He raises and partially dismisses three bottleneck objections: continual learning (addressed above), the "verifiable proxy" problem (finding what to measure for new paradigms, which he argues past paradigm shifts like the transformer were actually visible via existing benchmarks), and data scarcity (which he argues AIs will solve by generating and compiling their own training data, including domain-specific RL environments and simulated backtests of historical events).
