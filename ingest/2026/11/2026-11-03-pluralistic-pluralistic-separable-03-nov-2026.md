# Pluralistic: Separable (03 Nov 2026)

## Insights

- Doctorow argues our ability to perceive AI-generated content as "fake" lags behind its production — just as audiences initially couldn't distinguish Edison phonograph recordings from live performers, we initially couldn't distinguish AI text/images from human-made work, but both perceptual gaps close with exposure.
- He frames this as a pattern of "inseparability going separable": before chatbots, writing always implied a writer and paintings always implied a painter, so we automatically imputed human intent/creativity to AI outputs — a phenomenon he calls the real "AI hallucination" (citing Mark Fisher's "eerie" — intent without an intender).
- Using mathematician Fenner Stanley Tanswell's work and YouTuber Giordano Scarciotti, he distinguishes mathematical "solutions" (faits accomplis) from mathematical "insight" — the scaffolding connecting a solved problem to the broader field (e.g., Euler's Seven Bridges problem producing graph theory, not just a route map). AI models increasingly produce the former without the latter.
- He discusses Terence Tao as a model "centaur" — a mathematician who drives the research agenda while using a chatbot as assistant — contrasted with "reverse centaurs," workers reduced to cleaning up after/approving AI-driven output without real agency.
- Applies the centaur/reverse-centaur distinction to radiology: AI-assisted (centaur) radiologists improve diagnostic quality but process fewer cases, which isn't profitable for AI vendors; the industry instead pushes reverse-centaur models (mass AI output, human rubber-stamping) that increase speed at the cost of safety.
- Warns of a self-fulfilling prophecy: if AI companies successfully convince students/early-career professionals (mathematicians, radiologists) that their fields are being automated away, people won't enter those fields, creating an actual shortage of experts needed to supervise or collaborate with AI — a real-world harm from the hype itself.

## Quote

> Centaur radiology requires more radiologists, not fewer.
