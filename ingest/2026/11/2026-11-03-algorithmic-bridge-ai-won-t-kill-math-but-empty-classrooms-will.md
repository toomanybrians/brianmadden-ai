# AI Won't Kill Math But Empty Classrooms Will

## Insights

- An MIT survey (Oct 29, 268 early-career mathematicians across ~60 institutions) found ~70% report declining odds of pursuing an academic math career since mid-July, vs. 9% who see brighter prospects.
- Same survey: 55% dislike using AI for research (31% like it); 76% expect tenure to get harder (7% easier); 86 respondents would feel "very unhappy" if AI solved their active research problem vs. 6 "very happy"; 77% feel less motivated vs. 10% more motivated.
- The survey closed before OpenAI's Oct 6 release of solutions to 372 problem families, meaning current sentiment likely understates actual pessimism.
- Author frames this as a leading indicator of disciplinary death-by-attrition: discouraged young researchers leaving the field removes the mentors needed to sustain or rescue it later, a compounding effect rather than a one-time shock.
- Cites Yale economist Pascual Restrepo's paper "We Won't Be Missed": in an AGI economy, human wages tie to the cost of replicating labor with compute, and labor's income share trends toward zero.
- References Google DeepMind economists on asymmetry: human expertise is scarce while AI capacity is replicable "at the rental rate of compute," undercutting comparative-advantage arguments for human reallocation.
- Argues against "democratization" optimism (e.g., cheap AI subscriptions letting anyone tackle open problems) and against comparative-advantage-based reassurance — framing math's unraveling as a preview/"prognosis" for other knowledge fields, not an isolated case.

## Quote

> Human expertise is scarce, whereas AI can be replicated "at the rental rate of compute."
