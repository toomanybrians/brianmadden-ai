# The AI backlash needs a plan

## Insights

- Democrats' midterm wins were driven partly by backlash against data centers and AI accelerationism: voters approved bans/restrictions on data centers in at least 38 jurisdictions, and pro-AI super PACs (including ~$500M from Elon Musk) largely failed to move outcomes.
- The piece argues AI regulation risks repeating social media's regulatory failure: ~4 years of congressional hearings and investigations (Cambridge Analytica, Frances Haugen's documents, antitrust reports) produced almost no federal legislation, leaving courts and state AGs to do the real regulating via design-liability lawsuits and settlements.
- Three specific failure modes are named for AI regulation to avoid: (1) lack of a coherent goal — AI safety issues "branch fractally" across privacy, bias, bio-risk, job automation, and power concentration, making it easy to talk past each other; (2) lack of a backup plan if federal legislation stalls or gets preempted, especially since states have already built out AI safety law while federal preemption looms; (3) siloed advocacy — 800+ local anti-data-center groups, chatbot-harm parents, catastrophic-risk safety PACs, and pro-jobs building trades unions rarely coordinate, letting industry lobbyists exploit divisions.
- Key difference from social media era: Section 230 reportedly doesn't shield chatbot outputs, and frontier labs already face dozens of active lawsuits over user harm — suggesting AI companies are more legally exposed than social platforms were.
- Capability growth is accelerating faster than social media ever did: a cited METR review of internal Anthropic progress found the pace of capability growth on well-specified tasks is roughly 2x what it was from March 2024 to October 2025.
- In response to the political backlash, OpenAI and Google issued new community-facing data center commitments (no NDAs for local officials, covering power costs, community councils, local investment) — OpenAI also called for federal rules on power costs and data center NDAs, though critics note its pledges apply only to self-owned sites (just one planned in Georgia).
