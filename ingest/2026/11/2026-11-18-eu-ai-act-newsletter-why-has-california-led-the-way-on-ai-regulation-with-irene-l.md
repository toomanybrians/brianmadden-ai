# Why Has California Led the Way on AI Regulation? (with Irene Liu)

## Insights

- California's prominence in AI regulation stems from four factors: its massive economy (now 4th-largest globally, $4.5T+ output), concentration of frontier AI labs/talent/capital, a history of filling federal regulatory gaps (e.g., CCPA as a GDPR-like privacy baseline), and single-party political alignment across the Assembly, Senate, and governor's office that eases (but doesn't guarantee) bill passage.
- SB 1047 (2024) passed both legislative chambers but was vetoed by Governor Newsom, reportedly due to being overly comprehensive and facing heavy industry/VC lobbying against it; SB 53 (2025), a narrower successor shaped partly by a report from experts Fei-Fei Li, Jennifer Chayes, and Tino Cuéllar, was signed into law with requirements for transparency reporting (safety cards), critical incident reporting, and whistleblower protections.
- Legal profession described as rapidly transformed by AI adoption (tools like Harvey, Legora), reversing lawyers' historic slowness to adopt new technology; Stanford Law is building "Legal Engineering" programs to ensure law students/professionals are AI-fluent.
- A "California effect" is emerging analogous to the EU's "Brussels effect" — other US states (New York, Michigan) and companies use California's AI laws as a compliance baseline; any future federal AI legislation would likely preempt state laws, but none has passed yet.
- In the 2026 US midterms, anti-AI sentiment was channeled mainly into votes against data center projects rather than AI-safety-specific legislation — framed as a concrete, controllable target for broader public anxiety about AI (jobs, safety) rather than necessarily reflecting informed views on data centers themselves.
- On genuinely independent AI expertise: most top AI talent and academics are embedded within industry labs (especially in California), making it structurally difficult for policymakers to access non-industry-influenced expertise when crafting regulation.
- Comparing the EU AI Act and California's approach: the EU is seen by some in California's industry as having "acted too early," before generative AI existed, raising questions about the law's adaptability; Liu expressed general support for risk-based regulatory frameworks where obligations scale with risk level.

## Quote

> "It is an interesting time... the frontier labs that are working on this state-of-the-art frontier technology have no regulation whatsoever." — Irene Liu
