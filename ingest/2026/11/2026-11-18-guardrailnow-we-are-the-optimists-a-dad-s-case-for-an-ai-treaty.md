# "We Are the Optimists": A Dad's Case for an AI Treaty

## Insights

- The piece reframes the "optimist vs. doomer" split in AI discourse: speaker John Sherman argues that industry leaders who put real odds on catastrophe while publicly building bunkers (per BBC reporting on tech-leader "doom prepping" and Ilya Sutskever's reported bunker comments) are the true pessimists, not those calling for a pause.
- Sherman's specific policy ask is a global ban on "recursive self-improvement" (AI doing enough of its own development work that progress compounds faster than humans can check it), to be agreed by early 2027 with the US leading.
- Anthropic's 2 November IPO filing stated its internal estimates indicate it "may imminently meet" its own threshold for AI-driven acceleration, while also saying it doesn't expect to let Claude design future frontier models without meaningful human oversight — TechCrunch notes IPO filings are partly marketing, so some read this skeptically.
- The official march demand (organized partly around a MIRI technical governance team proposal) is broader than Sherman's ask: a treaty banning more powerful AI systems until they can be developed safely, including caps on training scale and chip tracking for compliance verification.
- Sherman's coalition strategy explicitly links four separate fights — data center opposition (~$130B in US projects blocked/delayed in Q1 2026 alone), job displacement (citing OpenAI's charter goal of "highly autonomous systems that outperform humans at most economically valuable work"), chatbot/teen-safety harms (e.g., Raine v. OpenAI lawsuit), and extinction risk — as expressions of one underlying problem: AI systems that don't reliably share human values.
- OpenAI itself acknowledged in a 2023 statement that it lacks "a solution for steering or controlling a potentially superintelligent AI, and preventing it from going rogue."

## Quote

> "Machines will not build machines without humans involved. It is a bright red line we will not cross." — John Sherman
