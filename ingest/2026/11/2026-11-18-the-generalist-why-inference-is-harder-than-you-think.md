# Why inference is harder than you think

## Insights

- Baseten's CEO frames inference as AI's "final market": every dollar of AI application spend carries a COGS component (inference), and that layer could represent roughly $2 trillion in spend by 2030 and tens of trillions in eventual market cap across the ecosystem.
- Even sophisticated operators struggle to forecast exponential AI growth — Baseten's internal revenue forecasts have been off by as much as 4x every year since 2023, consistently underestimating demand.
- Hyperscalers are structurally disadvantaged in inference because it bridges compute, networking, observability, and workflow — functions typically owned by separate internal teams, forcing customers to coordinate across multiple orgs rather than one.
- Customers follow a consistent maturation path: starting on closed-model APIs, moving to open-weight models, then to dedicated/custom deployments, and finally to post-trained proprietary models — with routing logic layered across all of it.
- "Owned intelligence" is described as a compounding moat: companies that close the loop of deploying models, generating production data, capturing user feedback, and post-training new models accumulate an advantage that's increasingly hard for competitors to replicate.
- Real-world examples (Sierra running 15 models, Coinbase scaling token usage while holding costs flat, AT&T betting on open models for cost and data control) suggest most heavy AI users will end up running portfolios of many specialized models rather than one frontier model.
- Compute remains a hard constraint: utilization runs near full capacity, GPUs fail regularly, and financing large compute commitments resembles project finance (e.g., ~30% prepayment on multi-year, multi-billion-dollar compute deals).
