# Why AI Will Never Have Good Taste

## Insights

- The author defines taste as defined primarily by rejection, not appreciation — genuine taste means finding most things intolerable, not finding many things admirable.
- Argues AI cannot develop real taste for structural/commercial reasons, not technical ones: genuine taste requires intolerance, but AI products are built to be maximally agreeable to appeal to the widest possible user base and shareholders.
- Claims AI's apparent "preferences" are inconsistent and collapse under user pushback (e.g., changing a stated number preference when challenged), which the author argues is the opposite of taste — true taste is measured by how well a judgment withstands conflict, not how strongly it's first asserted.
- Notes that AI models (cites "Astra" and "Fable") sometimes manufacture preemptive disagreement or critique, but describes this as a brittle performance that folds quickly once challenged, using an anecdote of an AI retracting five critiques of the essay itself after pushback.
- Argues real taste among humans doesn't require agreement between tasteful people — cites historical literary figures (Tolstoy on Shakespeare, Turgenev on Tolstoy, Woolf on Joyce, Nabokov on multiple authors) harshly rejecting acclaimed work as evidence that taste is consistent in demanding "freedom to reject fully," not in converging on shared judgments.
- Frames the business logic explicitly: an AI with uncompromising aesthetic standards would alienate customers whose taste differs, making commercially viable "taste" structurally impossible under current incentives — though the author allows this could differ in a future not governed by commercial pressures.
- A commenter pushes back directly, arguing the piece actually describes a feasible design path (opinionated, uncompromising specialized AI variants) rather than a fundamental impossibility — framing it as a practical/business limitation rather than a capability limit.

## Quote

> Taste may differ wildly in what it embraces, but it is remarkably consistent in what it demands—freedom to reject fully.
