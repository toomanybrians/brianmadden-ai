**Government restriction on frontier access has already happened once, and open-weight is already running in production at a bank.** Brian's [bubble-pop post](https://www.citrix.com/blogs/2026-07/how-to-build-an-ai-strategy-that-survives-the-bubble-pop) lists governments restricting frontier access on national-security grounds as a possible future scenario. Neo4j's Philip Rathle, [interviewed by Superintelligence](https://link.mail.beehiiv.com/v2/c/2133f957d1a23091c80d30e760524cc45cd3cc520376a8f1521ef454cea7494af7160cb8cb605ed00d78eb09e28deb6a1510abd40dff60525b65981fb3cb429be3f7f0223c7780a21d5d433728a66f1e87c9238997b12e9c048e7549522c2d1ff11d0dc2413ae4eb2d2e1d244205db33668b32565f10a180f7ad70d5d4156338b52c6137261d75357e0920ede7c080281193a7a371293eb761704a3929217a1f/034728f8c6e824bb), says it already happened in June:

- A US export-control directive forced Anthropic to disable Fable 5 and Mythos 5 for every customer worldwide for about three weeks.
- The shutdown was global because Anthropic couldn't verify users' nationality in real time.
- There was no bubble pop involved.

Rathle's prescription matches the [knowledge factory](https://github.com/toomanybrians/brianmadden-ai/blob/main/frameworks/knowledge-factory.md): keep the model swappable and own the context. He adds a step Brian's checklist doesn't have. Pick one production workflow and run a provider switch on it every quarter, the way you'd run a disaster-recovery drill. Measure accuracy, permissions, cost, and time-to-switch. Brian's "keep your data portable" item says what to do. The drill is how you prove you've done it.

The other half is a working example. [Revolut](https://linas.substack.com/p/revolut-ai-coding-costs-open-source-models) moved about 2,000 engineers off metered Anthropic and OpenAI APIs:

- The replacement is Z.ai's GLM, self-hosted on 56 B200s through Together AI.
- The blended cost is $0.30 per million tokens, a 90% cut.
- Frontier models are reserved for the tasks that need them.

Revolut is an EU-regulated bank, and it got a Chinese open-weight model past its regulators. Brian's notes cite Hesse excluding non-European models from a tender. That exclusion is one jurisdiction's procurement choice, not a rule regulators apply everywhere.

Thinking Machines also says it will release Inkling 2 Small under Apache 2.0 next week, pending red-teaming ([Superintelligence](https://link.mail.beehiiv.com/v2/c/6057a62c02d4f732258a0b50ff0ff486618319fd0824d8ac3aee25856b6f2b933166e9917951c69a8f5e83c569b6f1b3e39edb567d65c46c4271cd92283b9b342f24e6469b17e80a2713f9759abbec1910436603c9ef0ebef3bc34fca4bafb8d92d7627ad7c290aa5895bd086450c62dd211f23b6736d1c40de73ceec4b16e669783a6ddfb3bc079fcec67f1e3482e1ef6e16de8d30679897aff2d82c69d0f95/f6100dc0337eba7e)). It ranks ahead of GPT-6 Astra on a website-building leaderboard. If the weights ship, the bubble-pop post's line that US open-weight models trail the Chinese ones will need revisiting.

**There's now measured data on how fast you can actually stop an agent.** AWS and Okta tested agent revocation ([Part 1](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/machine-learning/govern-ai-agent-access-with-okta-cross-app-access-and-amazon-bedrock-agentcore-part-1/), [Part 2](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/machine-learning/govern-ai-agent-access-with-okta-cross-app-access-and-amazon-bedrock-agentcore-part-2/)). The answer depends on which switch you pull:

- **Unassigning a user from an agent:** new requests were refused within about a second. Tokens already issued kept working for up to five minutes.
- **Deleting the resource connection:** this was an immediate kill switch.
- **Deactivating the agent through Okta's agent API:** this sat in a queue for 86 minutes, with no way to cancel.
- **Per-user OAuth consent grants (the GitHub pattern):** these outlived the user's suspension until someone disconnected them by hand.
- **Plain token exchange without Okta's Cross App Access:** scopes widened silently, and revoked users' stale tokens still worked.

The governance layer added about 0.6 seconds to a 30-second request, so it isn't the bottleneck.

Brian's [visibility post](https://www.citrix.com/blogs/2026-09/you-cant-transform-the-ai-you-cant-see) says to act on a suspicious agent session the way you'd act on a suspicious human one. This is the first data on how long that action takes to land. Security teams need to know which switch actually works before an incident, not during one.

[a16z's Asaf Ezra](https://www.a16z.news/p/defense-in-depth-rebuilt-for-machine) makes a related point. One laptop now hosts a human, a coding agent, and a browser agent, all sending requests. So trust has to be judged per request and tied to a signed identity, not granted once at login.

**Citrix is turning session recording into a way to see how work happens.** Citrix is Brian's employer. Per [its own announcement](https://x.com/citrix/status/2118689927185494294), it is evolving Session Recording into Session Insights, with a preview tier for Citrix DaaS:

- The product watches what users actually do inside sessions, such as copying data between two apps all afternoon.
- It uses that to identify workflows ready to automate, based on observed behavior instead of employee interviews.
- Monitoring is opt-in by user group and can be switched on or off, for example finance only during an audit.
- Customers manage the encryption keys.
- AI-assisted review of long recordings is planned for a Q1 2027 preview.

This is the closing argument of Brian's visibility post turned into a product: the instrumentation you build for governance also shows you how the work actually gets done. Brian's notes say session recording has no privacy conflict when applied to agents. Applied to humans, it does, and the per-group toggle is how Citrix handles that. All of this is Citrix's description, not an independent test.

**Most enterprise AI is still speeding up old workflows rather than redesigning them.** [Deloitte's survey of 3,500 leaders](https://archive.thedeepview.com/p/gpt-6-cyber-faces-an-accelerating-ai-arms-race) found:

- 81% use AI to speed up decisions.
- 25% say it has meaningfully transformed the business.
- 15% report extensive job redesign.
- 15% report a real shift in who makes decisions.

Wharton's report in the same issue found about 40% of organizations measure AI by hours saved. Net-new revenue is the least-tracked metric, at 27%.

This is phase 2 of Brian's [factory electrification](https://www.citrix.com/blogs/2025-07/to-understand-ais-future-impact-check-out-this-playbook-from-150-years-ago) analogy: new motors, same floor plan. [Labor Matters](https://gadlevanon.substack.com/p/four-reports-one-story-the-economy) shows what that looks like in national data. Productivity gains are concentrated in four sectors, and growth elsewhere has stalled.

**Teams are fixing the review bottleneck by turning reviewer judgment into automated checks.** In [Nate's interview with OpenAI's Vijaye Raji](https://natesnewsletter.substack.com/p/openai-pando-git-backend), OpenAI says it built a new Git backend, Pando, to handle agent traffic, cutting median push latency by nearly 80%. FurtherAI's engineers merged five times more changes each, and the wait moved to testing, review, and deciding what to build. FurtherAI's fix was to turn recurring human review comments into 21 automated checks that agents run and fix before a human sees the work. That is a practical answer to Brian's September 4 point that individual speed only moves the queue. It's also the second report of a lab outgrowing standard Git, after Anthropic. Brian's knowledge factory runs on git, so that's worth watching.

**Data center restrictions keep winning.** As [Superintelligence](https://link.mail.beehiiv.com/v2/c/1b847ad7445a8ba71998f69bc72e2d78bc71f21c599c2a98704bf1311a00510f1de97245cda77d88b1df067124733d82f4d00e6974a88b666609f2f4e8c5c852861a4dde6a64eb136adbe6464a095559c2e99c1d4aae0a2287272d3a6efe0338f1d968c3a53c24240502a41aa60f63398aec4af5a401731baf1ba675ccfd7d37212d04fe28698772186d1f0b47a5765061eb114d98d6a65357f199e2bcae77d9/1a2ac614959d90e9) reports, restrictions won 40 of 41 local ballot measures on November 3. Yesterday's brief covered how the backlash sorts projects into winners and losers. The useful addition is [Nate's advice](https://natesnewsletter.substack.com/p/ai-data-center-backlash): within 90 days, audit which of your AI commitments depend on capacity from projects that may not get built. [Aaron Levie](https://x.com/levie/status/2118953909524050288) notes that agent inference isn't very sensitive to latency, so blocked capacity can move overseas. For a regulated customer, that turns a capacity problem into a data-residency problem.

**Salesforce is the third vendor shipping a decision model.** [Koa Decisions](https://www.salesforce.com/news/stories/koa-decisions-salesforce-decision-models/) is a fine-tuned Gemma model that returns a single choice instead of text. Salesforce says it answers in 26ms, against 1.4 seconds for a general-purpose LLM. It follows Jev and OpenAI's Decisions API, which fits Brian's October 11 note that the stack is splitting into specialized layers.

## What's interesting which you haven't written about yet

**AI's job effects may arrive with the crash rather than before it.** [Labor Matters](https://gadlevanon.substack.com/p/four-reports-one-story-the-economy) reports:

- Productivity is 10% above its pre-pandemic trend.
- Labor's share of output is the lowest since records began in 1947.
- Real hourly pay has risen 7% since 2019, while productivity rose 16%.

The author argues that displacement will show up in the next recession, because downturns are when firms lock in labor-saving technology they adopted during the boom. That looks like companies not hiring juniors rather than laying people off.

Brian expects a crash and frames it around which investments survive. He hasn't connected the crash to when workforce cuts happen. If both views are right, the pop and the layoffs will arrive together, and the pop will be the trigger.

**Skills and fixed workflows can convert into each other.** Amazon's [taxonomy for Amazon Quick](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/machine-learning/taxonomy-of-agentic-primitives-in-amazon-quick/) names two directions:

- **Crystallization:** a skill hardens into a deterministic, auditable workflow.
- **Dissolution:** a workflow loosens back into a skill.

Amazon frames the choice between them as a "confidence contract," meaning how much latitude the user is willing to give the agent for a given task. Its view that skills keep their value as models improve matches Brian's ["Skills are all you need"](https://www.citrix.com/blogs/2026-03/skills-are-all-you-need). Brian hasn't said when a skill should go back to being fixed code. Regulated customers who need the same answer every time are where that question will come up first.

## What could change your existing thinking

- **Cutting metered token budgets may also cut the productivity they were buying.** Per [Linas Beliūnas](https://linas.substack.com/p/revolut-ai-coding-costs-open-source-models), giving Revolut's engineers Claude Code and Codex didn't change delivery speed. Lead time fell about 70% only after Revolut moved to fixed-cost, self-hosted capacity. Brian's October 11 note treats companies setting smaller token budgets after a year of open spending as healthy discipline. Revolut suggests per-token metering itself suppresses the usage that produces gains. If so, the better move may be a change in how compute is bought, from metered to fixed capacity, rather than a smaller budget. The causation isn't isolated, since Revolut also tuned its GPU fleet in the same period.

- **SME fact-checking may be the weakest step in the knowledge factory.** [Ethan Mollick argues](https://x.com/emollick/status/2119140502746308674) that recent models beat human experts, including PhD specialists, at finding facts and checking claims. He says the human role is now [checking judgment and approach, not hunting errors](https://x.com/emollick/status/2119142327126290612). He doesn't link the data in these posts. Brian's [knowledge factory](https://github.com/toomanybrians/brianmadden-ai/blob/main/frameworks/knowledge-factory.md) has the AI draft and subject-matter experts fact-check. If Mollick is right, that step resembles the human-in-the-loop evidence in Brian's [notes](https://github.com/toomanybrians/brianmadden-ai/blob/main/me/developing-thinking.md): a human in a review seat who isn't the real control. The experts' time would be better spent on what canon already says only they can supply, which is the why behind decisions. Checking facts could go to automated, adversarial checks.
