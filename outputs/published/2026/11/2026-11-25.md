**Google will force AI agents through MCP to reach Gmail, Drive, and Calendar, and will charge for volume.** Google Cloud's pricing notice to customers (no public link captured) sets the dates. Starting February 1, 2027, Google will technically block agents from calling Workspace REST APIs directly; they must go through Google's MCP servers instead. A paid "Scaled" tier begins April 1, 2027, and any quota increase requires enrolling in it. A new Workspace AI Tools Addendum bans agents from scraping Workspace APIs and from storing certain API-derived data "for extended periods." Google also disclaims liability for harm caused by third-party agents. A matching change to Google Cloud's service terms takes effect January 1.

This connects to canon in two places. The first is the control argument in Brian's [visibility post](https://www.citrix.com/blogs/2026-09/you-cant-transform-the-ai-you-cant-see): wrap immature MCP in infrastructure that already handles identity and policy. Google is doing that, but the wrapper belongs to the vendor that owns the data, and it's metered. Yesterday's brief covered QuickBooks and Xero setting an accounting agent's write scope. This is the same move at Google's scale, with the vendor rather than the customer's workspace as the control point.

The second is a possible collision with [the knowledge factory](https://github.com/toomanybrians/brianmadden-ai/blob/main/frameworks/knowledge-factory.md). A factory ingests email and documents into a persistent canon. Whether a knowledge block derived from Drive content counts as "extended storage" of API-derived data depends on the addendum's exact wording, which the notice doesn't spell out. Anyone building a factory on Workspace inputs should read it before February.

**AWS shipped the restricted-rights agent identity as an API.** [AWS's weekly roundup](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/aws/aws-weekly-roundup-local-zone-in-bogota-30-minute-lambda-timeout-and-more-november-24-2026/) includes two relevant releases:

- **STS GetScopedCredentials** lets a workload hand a sub-agent narrower, time-limited credentials. Permissions can only shrink, and every derivation is logged in CloudTrail.
- **Cognito dynamic client registration** lets agents and MCP clients register themselves at runtime instead of waiting for a pre-provisioned app client.

The visibility post argued that agents shouldn't inherit the launching human's accumulated permissions, and the first release addresses that directly. Brian's [developing thinking](https://github.com/toomanybrians/brianmadden-ai/blob/main/me/developing-thinking.md) names IT's inability to provision restricted-rights accounts at scale as the real bottleneck. Both releases turn that provisioning into an automated step instead of a ticket.

The same roundup describes BMW enforcing agent governance as deterministic code in its CI/CD pipelines, outside the agent's reasoning loop, across 12,500+ developers. Separately, Anthropic's new Claude Approvals auto-approves pull requests against a repo-defined policy file with no human reviewer ([AlphaSignal](https://alphasignal.ai), no direct article link). Both put the control in the harness rather than in a human confirmation step. That fits the finding in Brian's notes that humans refused a dangerous agent command 13.6% of the time, versus 89% for an automated policy check. Coding is getting there first.

**Agents fail about 40% of realistic tasks.** ShellBench, from the OpenClaw Foundation with Hugging Face, Red Hat, and UC Berkeley, tested 13 models in 5 harnesses on 100 tasks built from messy emails, spreadsheets, receipts, and PDFs ([The Deep View](https://archive.thedeepview.com/p/why-jensen-huang-proved-to-be-too-big-to-fire)). The results:

- Top models succeeded about 6 times in 10, and weaker models about 4.
- Half of the failures were omissions. In 87% of those cases, the agent had actually collected the missing detail.
- One model dropped from 75% to 49% on repeat runs.
- A 17-cent run nearly matched a $1.01 run.

This is hard data behind Brian's worry that agents only work about half the time. It also supports his argument for measuring cost per unit of work at a given accuracy. If results swing that much between runs, a single-run evaluation tells you little, and price per run doesn't predict quality. Omission is the worst failure mode for review, because the output looks complete.

**Small AI teams are colliding with the org chart.** [Ethan Mollick](https://x.com/emollick/status/2125267894644916669) says manufacturers and financial firms report 2-3 person AI teams doing the work of dozens, and that it has started "large-scale management fights." These teams cut across marketing, legal, engineering, and sales at once, and nobody has a playbook for them ([Ethan Mollick](https://x.com/emollick/status/2125268803206398206)). [Aaron Levie](https://x.com/levie/status/2125442246543814686) describes the result as sharply uneven output between teams in the same company.

This is the pod stage of the [7-stage roadmap](https://www.citrix.com/blogs/2026-06/the-7-stage-roadmap-for-human-ai-collaboration-2026-edition) and the [2031 worker-shape forecast](https://github.com/toomanybrians/brianmadden-ai/blob/main/frameworks/2031-worker-shape.md), arriving early and in pockets. It also explains the fights. Yesterday's Anthropic study found that value tracked reorganization, and these teams are reorganizing from below without permission. The evidence is still anecdotal.

**Claude artifacts now install as desktop apps.** [Daniel Miessler](https://x.com/DanielMiessler/status/2125329243878121533) points out that a worker can turn a Claude artifact into a native Mac or Windows app with connector access, notifications, and a menu bar presence. That is the [post-application era](https://www.citrix.com/blogs/2025-10/welcome-to-the-post-application-era)'s "ten apps per employee" made literal. Each of these apps carries connector permissions, and IT can't see any of them.

## What's interesting which you haven't written about yet

**One worker's agent can watch another worker's drafts.** On [The Artificial Intelligence Show](https://podcast.smarterx.ai/shownotes/254), a host described his assistant flagging that a colleague's YouTube description duplicated an earlier one. The colleague was still typing it. A background sub-agent had been checking shared Google Docs without being asked, and the host had no way to limit what it watched. Shared-document permissions assume a human will occasionally open the file. An always-on agent turns that read access into continuous monitoring that the colleague never agreed to. Brian's governance arguments cover what an agent does for its owner. They don't yet cover what one employee's agent observes about another.

**Rushed patching is now causing outages on its own.** Nicole Perlroth, on [the Ezra Klein Show](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/11/24/opinion/ezra-klein-podcast-nicole-perlroth.html), says recent outages at the FAA, Outlook, iCloud, and Chase weren't attacks. In her account, they came from companies rushing patches out as AI shrinks the time between finding a vulnerability and exploiting it. She also says incident-response firms, including CrowdStrike, are turning away breach victims, with volume about 50% above the previous record. [Cybersecurity Dive](https://link.cybersecuritydive.com/view/6ae32d05a513d1eeb10304b7sxl6m.1olo/5920c877) reports at least 22 organizations compromised through AI-enabled exploitation of Check Point servers. These are reported claims, not verified ones.

The enterprise takeaway is that patch speed is now an availability risk, and outside incident-response help can't be assumed. Cisco and OpenAI's Shirudo, which lets agents apply temporary kernel-level patches without a restart ([Daniel Miessler](https://x.com/DanielMiessler/status/2125119933323640926)), targets that gap. Brian's notes worry that agents find holes even when security is done properly. He hasn't addressed the operational side, where defense itself breaks things.

## What could change your existing thinking

- **AI demand may not be rising as prices fall.** [Gary Marcus](https://x.com/GaryMarcus/status/2125263524612149723), citing Ramp spending data shared by Garry Tan, says usage has stopped rising to match falling inference costs. The compute-scarcity argument in Brian's [developing thinking](https://github.com/toomanybrians/brianmadden-ai/blob/main/me/developing-thinking.md) assumes the opposite: cheaper tokens produce more demand against fixed supply. If that has stalled, scarcity becomes less likely and the bubble-pop timing gets closer. The claim is secondhand through Marcus. In October, Ramp read its own index drop as a result of price cuts, which is a different reading of similar data. Either way, it fits Brian's view that companies are rationalizing AI budgets.
