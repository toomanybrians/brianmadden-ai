## What this confirms

Two data points continue the deflationary trend from yesterday's brief: [Meta and Google shipped competing agent models the same week with opposite efficiency philosophies](https://link.mail.beehiiv.com/v2/c/69f584f9b5456b57cefeaee5afc193231998449898e85335a4cd09169dea713e6073e351036b1a313b7a4893d8d5bea8d5cfdc0f02a50a9fa46f828fe2507f419863d1f74ff7dc4e77d1ae10db4fba1e459d9f7a1e967dd69e33b8cc7004efc592f3e11388f66e7b39af0f09aa49d488bf437d2dd400872e33ccf535253e6bb92f65fceca26e2b378f1444a92c7f6a4b1bcd4c82451c39717f514eeec438734a/3734e9760ec60557), and [Google priced Gemini 3.8 Flash at a fraction of Opus 5 and GPT-5.6 Sol while beating both on domain-specific and agentic-coding benchmarks](https://archive.thedeepview.com/p/gemini-3-8-s-edge-is-intelligence-per-dollar). Already covered yesterday: token costs falling and budget-tier models leapfrogging a five-day-old shortlist. Today adds the same pattern one layer up — labs competing on tokens-per-outcome, not just price-per-token — which is exactly what [the bubble-pop planning post](https://www.citrix.com/blogs/2026/07/20/how-to-build-an-ai-strategy-that-survives-the-bubble-pop/) means by "capabilities up, costs down, no end in sight." Google reportedly scrapped internal Gemini 3.5 Pro candidates for not beating Flash badly enough — a real signal that efficiency, not frontier size, is becoming the competitive axis for at least one major lab.

[CrowdStrike's new Agentic Identity Provider](https://archive.thedeepview.com/p/gemini-3-8-s-edge-is-intelligence-per-dollar) — cryptographic non-spoofable agent identities, one directory, short-lived task-scoped permissions, full agent-to-agent handoff traceability — is close to a literal implementation of [the agent-security argument](https://www.citrix.com/blogs/2025/08/04/ai-agents-are-the-new-insider-threat-secure-them-like-human-workers/) Brian published a year ago: verified identity, role-based access, complete action logging. Worth watching whether it actually solves the provisioning bottleneck Brian's argued is the real constraint (enterprise IT can't operationalize restricted-rights non-human accounts at scale today), or whether it's a vendor product sitting on top of a problem that's still unsolved underneath.

## What doesn't fit yet

[Gary Marcus's read on GPT-6 Astra](https://garymarcus.substack.com/p/hot-take-on-gpt-6-astra) sharpens something [named in yesterday's brief](https://danielmiessler.com/blog/peak-human-readership) as a live tension rather than a new one: Astra reportedly uses "recurrent depth," looping computation internally instead of producing readable chain-of-thought, and is described as more capable but less monitorable. OpenAI says it limited the technique to preserve legibility this time — but Marcus's point is sharper than that reassurance: strong performance on ARC-AGI-style benchmarks says nothing about robustness on open-ended tasks, and the opacity of how the system actually works is itself a problem independent of whether this particular release dialed the tradeoff back. This is the same fork already tracked, one incident closer to a real decision point for labs generally.

[Meta's two-tier pricing for its new open model](https://tomtunguz.com) — full price for zero data retention, a 92-95% discount if you let Meta train on your prompts — makes explicit something that's been implicit in every "AI is getting cheap" story: the subsidy isn't free, it's a data purchase, and Meta is now pricing it per-token instead of burying it in a EULA. At enterprise volume (1B tokens/day) the gap is roughly $454K/year — a real number for what a company is choosing to pay to keep its prompts private. This connects directly to the bubble-pop checklist's warning that subsidized token prices aren't permanent, but it adds a wrinkle the checklist doesn't cover: the "cheap" price isn't just temporarily subsidized, it's contingent on giving up something specific. Every enterprise running on discounted inference should know which tier it's actually on.

[Dataiku's CEO argument](https://archive.thedeepview.com/p/gemini-3-8-s-edge-is-intelligence-per-dollar) that companies will buy ~90% of their agents and build only the differentiated 10%, with "agent management" becoming its own discipline, is a plausible-sounding forecast with no supporting evidence in today's batch — file as a claim to watch, not a trend to act on.
