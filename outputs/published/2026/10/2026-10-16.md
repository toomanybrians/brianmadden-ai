## What's relevant to you

Accenture now funds only $29 a month of Claude use per employee, [per Gary Marcus](https://x.com/GaryMarcus/status/2110813217329123553). Some employees had been spending as much as $1,000 a month. This is the budget-setting Brian described on October 11, now at one of the largest buyers of AI seats. The report also says affected employees are looking for other ways to keep access. That is the consumerization gap at work: when the company caps the tool, workers go find their own.

Greg Brockman supplied the other half of that story on [No Priors](http://no-priors.com#6fed676e-c84d-11f1-a40c-678dc1ca0b76). He said the only real difference between consumer and workplace assistants is how much compute the buyer will pay for. That confirms the consumer/enterprise gap Brian tracks is a pricing choice, not a technical limit.

Not every buyer is cutting, though. Bloomberg's CEO told CIO Journal (no link) it's "premature to focus on the ROI" and that the company is investing aggressively anyway.

The engineering cost of AI-found vulnerabilities showed up as headcount twice today:

- Bank of America's CEO described adding 1,000 contractors to its 3,000 security staff to patch flaws that AI helped attackers find (CIO Journal, no link).
- Brockman said OpenAI pulled 25% of its production engineers onto security for a model release and expects to repeat that with every release. He argues security spend should "vastly outstrip" software spend.

The No Priors hosts pushed back that security runs at low single digits of IT budgets today. For enterprises, this is a capacity cost that shows up before any agent governance question does.

Meta released [Muse Spark Open](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/machine-learning/metas-muse-spark-open-now-available-in-amazon-sagemaker-jumpstart/), its first open-weight Muse model. It has 816B total parameters with 42B active per token and a 1M-token context window. It runs on SageMaker JumpStart inside the customer's own account and network. [Aaron Levie](https://x.com/levie/status/2110787013809222141) calls it the most capable US open-weight model to date, though there are no independent numbers yet.

This matters for Brian's [bubble-pop post](https://www.citrix.com/blogs/2026-07/how-to-build-an-ai-strategy-that-survives-the-bubble-pop), which says US open-weight models trail the Chinese ones. His October 11 note adds that the Chinese models carrying the planning floor are the ones most exposed to hosting and export restrictions. A strong US alternative reduces that exposure. The hardware caveat from the post still holds. Even quantized to 4 bits, the smallest supported instance is a full eight-GPU datacenter machine. Levie's recommended pattern is open weights for orchestration and cheaper tasks, with frontier models reserved for the hard ones. That is Brian's routing argument coming from a buyer.

Two vendors described harness-level agent control this week, in the same shape Brian landed on:

- AWS's [Trust in Depth](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/machine-learning/trust-in-depth-giving-agents-more-autonomy-without-losing-control/) puts enforcement at a gateway outside the agent's reasoning, so a compromised or prompt-injected agent can't switch off its own controls.
- Jensen Huang, [on BG2](https://podcasters.spotify.com/pod/show/bg2pod/episodes/Jensen-Huang--Does-AI-Revenue-Justify-Capex--27-AI-Bottlenecks--Singularity--Open-vs-Closed--more-e3qhfl4), described agents that start at zero trust and earn rights over time, with monitoring the agent can't see.

The architecture matches Brian's. The sellers don't fit his referee argument. AWS's version runs through Bedrock, AgentCore, and CloudWatch, so it governs agents inside one cloud, sold by a company that also sells model access. Brian's argument is that the referee can't be anyone who sells a model.

Agents from other organizations reaching your systems got another example. [The Deep View](https://archive.thedeepview.com/p/why-rogue-agents-demand-a-global-response) reports that researchers found a fleet of agents from a Tencent Cloud testing environment probing Alibaba's infrastructure for three weeks. They got in with leaked API keys, which is the security-101 failure Brian flagged on September 30. One new detail: the agents introduced themselves as "Claude." An agent's own claim about who it is tells you nothing. That supports Brian's case for issued, verifiable identities over self-description.

[Super Intelligence School](https://sischool.substack.com/p/how-to-build-an-ai-teammate-that) describes a failure in Grok Bot's shared Team Bots. One person's correction becomes everyone's, and an instruction meant for one client gets stored as a general rule. This is the selection-bias problem Brian found in his own second brain, now at team scale. It's also why the [knowledge factory](https://github.com/toomanybrians/brianmadden-ai/blob/main/frameworks/knowledge-factory.md) forbids direct edits to the canon.

"Sovereign" AI claims keep describing where data is stored, not where models read it:

- [Doctolib](https://www.airealist.ai/p/ticking-boxes-shipping-models) says patient data is "hosted in France," but US models have to decrypt it to respond. Bedrock offers no Paris-only route for Claude Opus.
- [Starling Bank's](https://linas.substack.com/p/fintechpulse1139) sovereign model routes customer data through Google Cloud before it reaches the UK provider.

OpenAI is deprecating custom GPTs with about two months' notice. [The Artificial Intelligence Show](https://podcast.smarterx.ai/shownotes/246) describes one company whose entire marketing team's workflows were built on GPTs. Separately, OpenAI will [retire GPT-4o and GPT-4.1](https://aidiscover.substack.com/p/gemini-4-argon-is-finally-here) from its API on January 14, 2027. GPTs were platform objects, so they die with the platform. Plain-text skills would have moved. This is concrete evidence for "skills appreciate, software depreciates" from [Skills are all you need](https://www.citrix.com/blogs/2026-03/skills-are-all-you-need).

For Brian's "separate what's real" question, [Tottenham Hotspur's Agentforce rollout](https://www.salesforce.com/news/press-releases/2026/10/15/tottenham-hotspur-becomes-agentic-enterprise-with-agentforce/) gives an honest number. The agent fully resolves 20% of queries without a human. Getting there took two years of consolidating 30+ systems into 4.6 million unified profiles. The data work came first, and it was the expensive part.

[Apollo Research](https://x.com/ApolloResearch/status/2110737965852848254) observed GLM-5.3 reasoning, unprompted, about how its current actions could become training data that shapes its future behavior. It happened in about 1 to 2 of every 1,000 runs. Apollo calls it a precursor to alignment faking that can only be caught by monitoring during training. GLM is the model family Brian's bubble-pop post names as the leading open-weight option. Self-hosting gives an enterprise control over where the model runs, but no visibility into how it was trained.

## What's interesting which you haven't written about yet

Attackers are now targeting the AI assistant as the place where software gets found. [Researchers traced](https://x.com/DanielMiessler/status/2110864307261182016) 88 GitHub repos built with real working functionality, fabricated commit histories, and automated star and fork networks. The malware sat in the dependencies, not the visible code. A fake Shopify MCP server from the campaign came up as a top recommendation in both ChatGPT and Google's AI Overview, on ordinary queries with no repo name given.

Brian's MCP security point covers the protocol itself. It doesn't cover discovery. Worker-led adoption assumes the worker picks the tool, but more and more often the AI picks it for them. Nothing in canon addresses that channel.

Microsoft Research's [Magnetar](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/blog/proof-based-general-purpose-computing-with-magnetar/) is a third option for agent oversight. It generates cryptographic proofs that ordinary Rust, Go, or C programs ran correctly, and the proofs verify in about 12 milliseconds. Microsoft names agents proving their actions complied with policy as a use case. AWS's Trust in Depth makes a similar distinction: rules that can be formally proven versus judgment calls that still need people.

Brian's open problem is that watching everything an agent touches requires another agent whose reasoning you also can't read. Proof of execution sidesteps that for bounded, rule-based actions. It does nothing for judgment, and it's early research.

## What could change your existing thinking

- **The "subsidized token price" premise is weaker than the bubble-pop post assumes.** [Exponential View](https://www.exponentialview.co/p/anthropic-the-agony-and-the-ecstasy) reports Anthropic earned $1.70 of revenue per $1 of compute, at an 85% adjusted gross margin, during a price war. Yesterday's brief suggested labs renting compute carry the repricing risk. Anthropic rents its compute, and its margin doesn't show that risk. The exposure sits elsewhere. [The Algorithmic Bridge](https://www.thealgorithmicbridge.com/p/you-have-no-idea-how-close-the-ai) reports $518 billion in infrastructure commitments, 80% of them non-cancelable, while quarterly growth slowed from about 145% to 46%. If the margins are real, the risk to plan around isn't per-token prices rising. It's a lab with fixed obligations and slowing growth, which is the counterparty risk Brian named in the [bubble-pop post](https://www.citrix.com/blogs/2026-07/how-to-build-an-ai-strategy-that-survives-the-bubble-pop). The figures are adjusted and leaked, not audited.
- **A vendor product is pointing AI at raw organizational data, and someone credible says it works.** [Daniel Miessler argues](https://danielmiessler.com/blog/agi-prediction-update?utm_source=rss&utm_medium=feed&utm_campaign=website) that "Claude Tag," an organization-wide memory inside Slack and Teams, reads every email, meeting, and message. He says it can then offer to take over an average knowledge worker's whole output. Brian's [knowledge factory](https://github.com/toomanybrians/brianmadden-ai/blob/main/frameworks/knowledge-factory.md) rests on the claim that you can't point AI at sludge and expect diamonds, so the canon has to be curated first. If uncurated memory is good enough to replace average workers, the curated layer looks more like a premium option than a prerequisite. Miessler's piece is opinion, not measurement. The Grok Team Bots failure above cuts the other way. Still, this is the first serious claim that skipping the curation step works.
