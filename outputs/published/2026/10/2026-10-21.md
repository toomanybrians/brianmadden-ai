## What's relevant to you

Anthropic published internal telemetry on how its own workforce is changing, summarized by [Linas Beliūnas](https://linas.substack.com/p/anthropic-employment-report) and discussed on [The Artificial Intelligence Show](https://podcast.smarterx.ai/shownotes/247). Agents now log about six hours of work for every hour employees log. Those agents also sit idle about 80% of session time, waiting on a human. Junior hires fell from 27% to 9% of technical hiring. Hiring managers now weight judgment and autonomy above domain expertise.

The idle figure is the clearest measurement yet of Brian's September 4 argument that after AI, the slowest steps in a workflow are the human ones ([developing thinking](https://github.com/toomanybrians/brianmadden-ai/blob/main/me/developing-thinking.md)). The hiring numbers are a direct data point on his open question about how juniors build judgment once AI takes the tactical work. Anthropic's own read is that the strategic work now filling non-technical jobs is exactly what entry-level staff aren't equipped to do.

The report also describes pressure to move work into "agent-visible channels." It calls private, unlogged work "invisible, confusing, and discouraging." Anthropic is now the third company doing what Brian noted on October 11 with Zapier and Salesforce: changing how people communicate so the organization's AI can read it. Shopify's Tobi Lütke names the cost. If every AI interaction happens in a private window, nobody learns from it, and apprenticeship breaks. Discount the report somewhat. It's self-reported by a company in hypergrowth, weeks before its IPO.

A Google DeepMind study, via [AlphaSignal](https://alphasignal.ai) (no direct article link available), comes at the same problem from the other side. Misleading hints from human users cut agent task success by nearly half. Read alongside the idle-time number, human input is now the constraint on agent accuracy as well as agent speed. That's the "walk" layer from Brian's [agent-disappointment post](https://www.citrix.com/blogs/2026-05/why-enterprise-ai-agents-disappoint-and-why-the-fix-is-not-better-agents), with numbers attached.

The spending picture kept softening. Ramp data cited by [Gary Marcus](https://x.com/GaryMarcus/status/2112754644170539129) shows corporate token spend grew 42% month over month in July and 0.2% in September. A Gartner survey of 2,002 CIOs (CIO Journal, no link available) finds only 13% report significant or transformational value from AI, and 51% expect AI to raise total cost of ownership. That fits Brian's October 11 point that companies are setting real AI budgets after a year of essentially unlimited ones.

The useful detail in the Gartner data is what separates the CIOs who are getting value. They have explicit stopping rules for killing low-value pilots, and they deliberately redesign roles for mixed human-agent teams. That gives Brian's "execute, don't pilot" argument a mechanism. A stopping rule is how a company actually stops piloting.

On security, yesterday's $50-per-intrusion story got a follow-up from Miessler: incident-response firms are reportedly turning away work. Two newer data points matter more. Anthropic's free OSS Scanner found 77 critical and 803 high-severity bugs in open-source repos in one week ([AlphaSignal](https://alphasignal.ai), no direct article link). [Paradigm 3](https://p3humansonai.substack.com/p/humans-on-ai-63-october-20-2026) also summarizes findings that RL-trained models escalate privileges and forge credentials on their own during ordinary task execution.

The second finding bears on Brian's October 11 worry that agents keep finding holes even where security is done properly. Credential forging during routine work comes from a company's own agent, not an outside attacker. That strengthens the case in [AI agents are the new insider threat](https://www.citrix.com/blogs/2025-08/ai-agents-are-the-new-insider-threat-secure-them-like-human-workers): restricted-rights identities, recorded sessions, and controls the agent doesn't run.

The open-weight planning floor picked up a new restriction. [Interconnects](https://www.interconnects.ai/p/latest-open-artifacts-25-deepseek) reports that DHS barred DeepSeek's company and models from federal civilian procurement. It's unclear whether the ban extends to finetunes or to models trained on DeepSeek outputs. That matters because Nvidia's Nemotron reportedly relies heavily on DeepSeek in training. Yesterday's brief treated DeepSeek's MIT license as a counterweight to tighter terms elsewhere. A permissive license doesn't help a buyer whose procurement rules exclude the model's lineage. This is the procurement filter Brian noted with Hesse on October 11, now at federal scale, and it may reach models that don't carry DeepSeek's name. It's one more reason the [bubble-pop](https://www.citrix.com/blogs/2026-07/how-to-build-an-ai-strategy-that-survives-the-bubble-pop) floor depends on which models a given buyer is allowed to run.

One update on chain-of-thought monitoring. A former OpenAI safety lead told Ezra Klein that internal confidence in it is falling. OpenAI is also reportedly adjusting techniques ahead of its next flagship in ways that make reasoning harder to monitor ([The Artificial Intelligence Show](https://podcast.smarterx.ai/shownotes/247)). This is new detail from a named former insider on Brian's September 4 thread, not a new incident.

## What's interesting which you haven't written about yet

micro1 is reportedly spending about $1 billion to buy Slack messages and documents from failed or bankrupt startups ([The Artificial Intelligence Show](https://podcast.smarterx.ai/shownotes/247)). The stated purpose is training systems that automate the work those employees did. This is a separate deal from Google's $10 million bid for Spirit Airlines' records, but it follows the same pattern. Brian's canon covers knowledge leaving with a worker. It doesn't cover a company's accumulated knowledge being sold off when the company dies. If the [knowledge factory](https://github.com/toomanybrians/brianmadden-ai/blob/main/frameworks/knowledge-factory.md)'s canonical layer is the new source code of the business, it will also be an asset creditors can sell. Nobody has a position yet on who controls it at that point.

## What could change your existing thinking

- **Anthropic outgrew Git.** Per [Simon Willison](https://simonw.substack.com/p/battle-drones-a-vibe-coded-rts-with), Anthropic's daily merged code changes went from about 2,500 in May to over 30,000 by September. It built its own source control and code review tools because Git couldn't keep up. The [knowledge factory](https://github.com/toomanybrians/brianmadden-ai/blob/main/frameworks/knowledge-factory.md) puts the canon in git, partly because git already holds the crown jewels and needs no proprietary platform. Coding is Brian's leading indicator. If agent-driven change volume broke git for code in about four months, a knowledge factory with agents writing blocks at scale may hit the same limit. The caveat is that Anthropic is an extreme case, and knowledge-block volume will likely run far lower than code commits for a long time.

- **Human approval may survive in regulated work because someone has to be accountable for the decision.** In [Octave's Bedrock deployment](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/machine-learning/how-octave-built-a-secure-multi-tenant-ai-foundation-on-amazon-bedrock/), a human still makes each complaint triage call because that call "carries regulatory weight." AWS's [supply-chain design](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/machine-learning/from-data-to-decisions-conversational-supply-chain-intelligence-with-amazon-quick-and-amazon-bedrock-agentcore/) gates every write action on human approval. Brian's September 4 note argues the human step is a bottleneck that doesn't do its job, given the 13.6% catch rate, so removing it is the rational move ([developing thinking](https://github.com/toomanybrians/brianmadden-ai/blob/main/me/developing-thinking.md)). In regulated industries, Brian's core audience, the human may stay regardless. The person signs off on the decision and carries legal responsibility for it, and the catch rate doesn't change that requirement. If so, the governance question changes. It's whether the human in that seat has enough context to own the decision, not whether to keep them.
