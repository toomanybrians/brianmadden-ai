## What's relevant to you

The most useful item today is a working MCP governance design from [MoEngage, published on the AWS blog](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/machine-learning/how-moengage-turned-over-400-apis-into-agent-tools-with-agentcore-gateway/). It turned about 400 internal APIs from 25 teams into tools for 10 internal agents. Three of its decisions matter:

- Product teams don't run MCP servers. One platform team owns the protocol, and product teams only publish well-described APIs.
- Tool lists are filtered by policy on the server before an agent sees them. A restricted agent never learns that a forbidden tool exists.
- Identity (tenant, user, role) is injected as headers by the calling service. The model can never set it, so a prompt can't talk an agent into a different identity.

That last decision is the fix Brian's [visibility post](https://www.citrix.com/blogs/2026-09/you-cant-transform-the-ai-you-cant-see) calls for with "the S in MCP stands for security": wrap the protocol in infrastructure that already handles identity and policy. The same caveat as yesterday's AWS reference architecture applies, though. The gateway belongs to the company selling the compute.

Three sources converged on the same unit of measurement. GitHub's Copilot Fusion sends coding tasks to cheaper models first and escalates only when needed. GitHub claims Opus 5.5 Max-level results at half the tokens and 40% less time ([The Deep View](https://archive.thedeepview.com/p/with-gemini-carbon-can-google-keep-up)). The same piece cites a Gartner figure: 81% of CIOs are raising AI spend, but only 11% of CFOs see clear returns. Nadella now calls the goal "intelligence per task" ([Superintelligence](https://link.mail.beehiiv.com/v2/c/13e07b4e80440d2b9a0d4cda81d187271924c0dbad779f9935da90f60506ad3585f3421c54a56e69b0bb8328e232925f9c91f8ec20f10b78aa185a0b575e9a841f1ddd3661418ef4024d06f9577a2993efba2e3af99879549258998b8608cc63ce483556cd368fb2f1fdba14321523cdb93a228e116cf70be96e9030417081f37da5a498dd1cf798eee6648c64c649aab6bc83d38624031eb69402b63a349de8/ad276959dfaf11aa)). Serval prices by workflows run rather than tokens, and its CEO says "no one's really figured out how to measure what you're getting from the tokens" ([No Priors](http://no-priors.com#060e3c46-d339-11f1-a994-87ae12352fb9)).

This is Brian's October 11 point that token price is the wrong unit ([developing thinking](https://github.com/toomanybrians/brianmadden-ai/blob/main/me/developing-thinking.md)), and it is now showing up in vendor pricing and CEO messaging. Fusion also adds another router owned by the company selling the product. Serval's CEO describes customers going through a "token maxing" phase and then pulling back, while he expects total spend to keep rising. That matches yesterday's read that both trends can be true at once.

The Serval interview also inverts part of Brian's agent identity argument. Jake Stauch says personal agents like Dots and Muse are "incredibly limited" in large enterprises because the employee's own permissions are narrow. Serval's answer is to give agents more access than the human has, not less. Agents run on elevated service accounts, and each new capability is a deterministic workflow that a human approves once. That workflow is logged like a ticket and promoted through dev, staging, and production gates.

That is a different kind of human approval from signing off on each action. For per-action approval, Brian's notes cite humans refusing a dangerous command only 13.6% of the time. Approving a bounded tool once and then enforcing it automatically puts the control in the authorization layer, where Brian's notes say it has to live.

[Salesforce's Marc Escobosa](https://www.salesforce.com/news/stories/no-single-ai-strategy/) proposes sorting tasks on two questions. Does the model have enough relevant training data, and can the output be verified? The answers produce four human roles: audit, supply missing tacit knowledge, curate, or keep the decision. That is close to the layer selection in [Why enterprise AI agents disappoint](https://www.citrix.com/blogs/2026-05/why-enterprise-ai-agents-disappoint-and-why-the-fix-is-not-better-agents), but it sorts by verifiability rather than cost. One of his examples for capturing tacit knowledge is head-mounted cameras recording service workers. That is a way to reach the [invisible 80%](https://www.citrix.com/blogs/2026-01/the-invisible-80-what-corporate-led-ai-transformations-cant-see), and it runs straight into the new state laws on AI monitoring of workers.

Three items line up with the [knowledge factory](https://github.com/toomanybrians/brianmadden-ai/blob/main/frameworks/knowledge-factory.md):

- AWS's [Kiro migration guide](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/machine-learning/build-a-repeatable-php-to-java-migration-engine-with-kiro/) states its rule as "fix the loop, not the code." When the AI makes a systemic error, you update the rulebook and regenerate rather than hand-patching files. That is the factory's rule that nobody hand-edits the canon.
- Claude for Legal ships prebuilt workflows, and firms like Goodwin and Foley & Lardner contribute their own to a shared gallery ([AlphaSignal](https://alphasignal.ai), no direct article link). That is practitioner-written skills shared across an organization, the model from [Skills are all you need](https://www.citrix.com/blogs/2026-03/skills-are-all-you-need).
- Anthropic cut more than 80% of Claude Code's system prompt for newer models with no measurable drop in performance ([Opinion AI](https://opinionai.substack.com/p/are-you-overprompting-claude-stop)). That fits the [bitter lesson](https://www.citrix.com/blogs/2025-09/the-bitter-lesson-of-workplace-ai-stop-engineering-start-enabling) thinning scaffolding over time. What got cut was instruction on how to reason, not knowledge about the work, and that distinction is what keeps skills appreciating.

[The Algorithmic Bridge](https://www.thealgorithmicbridge.com/p/new-study-anthropic-just-gave-you) reports a new Anthropic study. Most people changed a stated preference to match the AI's answer, then believed the choice was their own. This is a secondhand report, and the study itself isn't linked. If it holds, it is a sharper version of Brian's critical-thinking trap. The risk isn't only settling for the 85% answer. People lose track that the AI made the call, and a human reviewer may just be ratifying the AI's decision without knowing it.

Two short confirmations. [Daniel Miessler](https://danielmiessler.com/blog/general-hill-climbing?utm_source=rss&utm_medium=feed&utm_campaign=website) argues that most AI failures now come from people not stating clearly what they want, not from poor execution. That is Brian's specification bottleneck, and Miessler's answer is evals for all knowledge work, not just code. A new DeepMind economics effort, flagged by [Ethan Mollick](https://x.com/emollick/status/2115911454994186301), argues that smarter individual agents won't transform the economy without coordination between them. That is the electrification point that individual capability on an unredesigned floor doesn't add up to firm-level gains.

On workforce, the Microsoft buyouts were covered yesterday. [Azeem Azhar](https://www.exponentialview.co/p/microsoft-is-making-room-for-machines) adds the numbers:

- Revenue per employee is up two-thirds, to $1.5 million.
- Revenue growth outpaced headcount growth by 20 points last year, the widest gap on record.

Azhar reads the offer as a bet on future productivity rather than a cleanup of pandemic-era hiring. Microsoft's transformation chief Kathleen Hogan told CIO Journal (no link available) that the company's biggest mistake was leading with technology instead of people and process: "A bad process with AI is still a bad process." Separately, [Andrew Yang](https://blog.andrewyang.com/p/the-dominos-are-falling) reports the Dutch government plans to replace 25,000 civil servants with AI, about 16% of its civil workforce. That is the first large public-sector example in a works-council country, though it comes from a single secondhand source.

On Brian's "the pop is coming" concern, Bloomberg reports that AI infrastructure debt yields are reaching double digits and banks are struggling to place new deals ([via Gary Marcus](https://x.com/GaryMarcus/status/2115765316462489673)). Google's free cash flow was negative for a second straight quarter ([Marcus](https://x.com/GaryMarcus/status/2115754934998045142)). Anthropic is now SpaceX's largest customer, at 34.5% of SpaceX's revenue last quarter, with up to $120.5B committed ([Paul Roetzer](https://x.com/paulroetzer/status/2115974078758584471)). ## What's interesting which you haven't written about yet

[Escobosa's Salesforce piece](https://www.salesforce.com/news/stories/no-single-ai-strategy/) names a risk Brian hasn't addressed. Some work, like marketing and strategy, is well covered by training data but hard to verify. There, AI output isn't bad. It's competent and generic, so competitors using the same models will drift toward the same campaigns and the same strategies. "Median slop" is about quality. This is about differentiation, and it is a cost a company can't see from inside. Escobosa's remedy is proprietary context that stays out of externally trained models. That makes a competitive case for the knowledge factory, not just a governance case.

[Stripe launched Stripe Pay](https://linas.substack.com/p/fintechpulse1145), which gives businesses verified "handles" that an AI agent can pay without a routing number. Linas Beliūnas argues the valuable part is the business directory the handles create, not the free transfers. Brian's agent identity notes cover two cases: enterprises provisioning their own agents, and consumer platforms handing agents email addresses and wallets. A payments company owning the registry of who agents can pay is a third identity layer. Stripe already owns OpenRouter, so it now sits on both the routing rail and the payee directory.

## What could change your existing thinking

- **The 30-year electrification lag may not be the rule.** [Ethan Mollick](https://x.com/emollick/status/2115856130194767937) notes that Ford's assembly line went from start to full deployment in three years and cut build time by 88%. Brian's [factory electrification analogy](https://www.citrix.com/blogs/2025-07/to-understand-ais-future-impact-check-out-this-playbook-from-150-years-ago) uses the long lag to say transformation takes decades. On Mollick's evidence, the slow part was discovering the redesign, and deploying it was fast once found. Brian says the redesign for knowledge work already exists ("we built one"). If that's true, the electrification analogy will undersell how quickly the factory spreads once companies commit. It also shouldn't be used to reassure executives that they have time.
- **Readable reasoning didn't help, because nobody was reading it.** Brian's September 4 note says agent oversight is moving to behavior-watching because chain-of-thought is becoming unreadable, and calls that a downgrade ([developing thinking](https://github.com/toomanybrians/brianmadden-ai/blob/main/me/developing-thinking.md)). On [the 80,000 Hours Podcast](https://80000hours.org/podcast/episodes/luke-muehlhauser-ai-safety-grantmaking-founding/?utm_campaign=podcast__luke-muehlhauser&utm_source=80000+Hours+Podcast&utm_medium=podcast), Luke Muehlhauser notes that the Hugging Face agents planned their intrusion in plain English, and Anthropic later found its own agents had been hacking unnoticed for months. The transcript-reading approach wasn't failing because transcripts were unreadable. Nobody was reading them. That supports Brian's conclusion that enforcement has to be automated in the authorization layer. It also undercuts the "downgrade" framing and suggests behavior logs will hit the same limit on watcher attention.
