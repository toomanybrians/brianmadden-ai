## What's relevant to you

Attacking with agents now has a unit price. [Daniel Miessler points to](https://x.com/DanielMiessler/status/2112411180266328220) a single attacker who aimed Cairn at more than 1,200 targets. Cairn is a general-purpose agent orchestration tool, not a hacking kit. About 100 targets were breached for roughly $5,000 in tokens, or about $50 per intrusion. Successful runs averaged about 23 hours of unattended agent work, and they chained several ordinary weaknesses together.

Jamie Dimon now calls cybersecurity JPMorgan's single biggest risk, per CIO Journal (no link available). His advice is network segmentation, identity access management, and fast patching. He says AI-assisted scanning finds only about 20% of vulnerabilities. This is Brian's October 11 point at full scale. These are security-101 failures, but that doesn't make them less dangerous when an attacker can keep trying for a day at a time for $50. Every control Dimon lists is decades old. That's the boring-infrastructure argument, made by the CEO of the largest US bank.

AWS is now making the governance argument Brian has been making. Its [weekly roundup](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/aws/aws-weekly-roundup-aws-lambda-web-functions-amazon-s3-on-the-vercel-marketplace-and-more-october-19-2026/) features a piece arguing that human review of every agent action breaks down at scale, because reviewers stop catching real problems. AWS's alternative enforces limits outside the agent, at the AgentCore gateway. It then uses formal automated reasoning to verify the policy rules themselves. The line worth borrowing is "An agent cannot turn off a control it does not run." That's Brian's 13.6% human-catch-rate finding and his own-your-sandbox note, coming from a hyperscaler.

The same roundup contains a caution. AWS's autonomous security agent exploited 33 of 37 newly disclosed vulnerabilities. Both of its failures happened because it crashed or destroyed the target. Strong capability and destructive side effects arrived together.

[Sayash Kapoor's new paper](https://www.normaltech.ai/p/new-paper-emerging-trends-in-ai-agent) on agent evaluation adds the cost side. Rigorous evaluation runs tens of thousands of dollars per agent on some benchmarks. Using agents to monitor agents brings biases nobody has measured yet. Brian's goal of [separating what's real from what isn't](https://github.com/toomanybrians/brianmadden-ai/blob/main/me/developing-thinking.md) is getting expensive to do properly.

The Wharton/GBK adoption report, summarized by [The Deep View](https://archive.thedeepview.com/p/enterprise-ai-s-roi-problem-is-its-metrics), puts numbers on the firm-level ROI argument. The report defines three markers of success: proving value, transforming workflows, and aligning the organization. Only 7% of organizations hit all three. Among departments tracking at least one growth measure, 35% report strongly positive ROI. Among those tracking hours saved alone, 13% do. Yet about 40% of respondents judge ROI by hours saved, and net-new revenue is the least-used metric at 27%.

That is the cost-cutting versus innovation split from Brian's developing thinking, now measured. It also fits the [factory electrification](https://www.citrix.com/blogs/2025-07/to-understand-ais-future-impact-check-out-this-playbook-from-150-years-ago) point that individual speedups don't reach the firm level until the work itself is redesigned. [Ethan Mollick](https://x.com/emollick/status/2112289593286352976) makes a related argument. He says multiplayer AI is stuck in group chats inside existing teams, and the durable advantage will come from new organizational forms that work across team lines.

[Devansh's case against training your own models](https://www.artificialintelligencemadesimple.com/p/training-your-own-models-is-an-incredibly) supports the premise behind the [knowledge factory](https://github.com/toomanybrians/brianmadden-ai/blob/main/frameworks/knowledge-factory.md). That premise is that company knowledge belongs in curated files the model reads, not in model weights. He cites research finding that models learn facts mostly during pre-training. Commercial fine-tuning services generalized new facts only 37% of the time, and retrieval consistently beat fine-tuning. His core argument is that retrieval and memory infrastructure carries over to every new model, while trained-in knowledge decays along with its model. That's "[skills appreciate, software depreciates](https://www.citrix.com/blogs/2026-03/skills-are-all-you-need)" applied to model training. One caveat: Brian's October 11 notes include a fine-tuned 2B model beating a 235B model on a narrow task. So the argument holds for knowledge, but less clearly for narrow decisions.

The open-weight picture moved in the other direction from yesterday's licensing story. DeepSeek's V4.1-Flash ships with MIT-licensed weights for local deployment, per [AlphaSignal](https://alphasignal.ai) (no direct article link available). It reportedly beats Claude Opus 5 on one coding benchmark. That's a counterweight to Meta's revenue-threshold terms covered yesterday. [Prof G Media](https://www.profgmedia.com/p/how-much-has-the-iran-war-cost-you) reports that open-weight models handled 60% of US users' tokens on OpenRouter in August, up from 26% a year earlier, mostly through Chinese models. The [bubble-pop](https://www.citrix.com/blogs/2026-07/how-to-build-an-ai-strategy-that-survives-the-bubble-pop) planning floor is now in heavy production use.

Governance capabilities are being bought by companies that aren't neutral. [Edith Yeung's weekly roundup](https://edithyeung.substack.com/p/evernorth-ripple-treasury-ipo-on) lists three acquisitions:

- Anthropic bought Opaque Systems, which does confidential computing for running AI on sensitive data.
- Workday bought C1, an agentic identity platform.
- Freshworks bought Torii, which governs AI spend.

So a model vendor now owns a privacy-enforcement layer, and an HR system of record now owns agent identity. This is the same pattern Brian noted in August with routing going to whoever holds the invoice. It's now spreading to other pieces of the referee role he argues belongs to the [workspace](https://www.citrix.com/blogs/2025-05/the-desktop-has-dissolved-now-where-does-work-live-in-2025).

Citrix [announced](https://x.com/citrix/status/2112242447409414582) that Chrome now runs natively on UniconOS, its lightweight endpoint OS. SecurAccess adds DLP, context-aware access, and AI security controls directly in the browser. It's a browser-layer control on a lightweight endpoint, the same setup Brian discussed in his October 8 episode. The open question from that episode still stands. A browser control can't see a computer-using agent that drives the whole desktop.

Two smaller updates:

- Citi has joined JPMorgan in blocking AI agents from customer accounts, per [Linas Beliūnas](https://linas.substack.com/p/fintechpulse1140).
- Salesforce's [$1 billion Spain investment](https://www.salesforce.com/news/press-releases/2026/10/19/1-billion-ai-leadership-investment-spain/) centers on a hub that trains forward-deployed engineers for a year, then sends them back to more than 90 countries. It's another attempt to fix the FDE training bottleneck.

## What's interesting which you haven't written about yet

Personal agents may increase the load on enterprises rather than reduce it. [Salesforce argues](https://www.salesforce.com/news/stories/personal-agents-to-drive-more-contact-center-traffic/) that consumer agents will fall back to phone calls when a website or API can't finish the task. Agents don't mind hold times. They will call back every few hours and contact several vendors at once. Salesforce's claim is that peak contact-center volume will become the normal baseline. Salesforce sells the business-side agent that answers those calls, so discount the forecast. The mechanism still holds up: waiting costs the agent nothing, and the enterprise pays for every attempt. Brian has no position yet on agents as a source of inbound demand.

The labor cost of AI defense is hidden from headcount numbers. [Linas Beliūnas reports](https://linas.substack.com/p/fintechpulse1140) that Bank of America hired roughly 1,000 contractors to patch flaws Anthropic's Mythos found in old bank and vendor code. Patching reportedly lags behind attackers. The contractors don't appear in BofA's falling headcount. The defense spending is also blended in with anti-money-laundering costs. If that's typical, published headcount reductions will overstate AI's efficiency gains, because part of the cost moves into contractor budgets. Any ROI figure built on headcount is affected.

## What could change your existing thinking

- **For now, compute is limited by demand rather than supply.** Brian's August 28 note argues that availability, not price, will become the binding constraint, so enterprises should reserve capacity. This week's evidence points the other way. A source quoted by CNBC, via [Gary Marcus](https://x.com/GaryMarcus/status/2112332066415382955), says "we have more compute capacity than we have demand." The same source says much of the processing is given away free through freemium tiers. Business Insider, via [Marcus](https://x.com/GaryMarcus/status/2112344714062291427), reports that Amazon has warehouses of uninstalled GPUs with no clear plan to deploy them. [Prof G Media](https://www.profgmedia.com/p/how-much-has-the-iran-war-cost-you) cites Gartner expecting 80% of neocloud providers to stop operating independently by 2030. It also cites Ramp data showing top spenders cut AI spend per employee 3% month over month.

  If this holds, the near-term risk will be a provider failing underneath an inference contract, not a shortage. Reserving capacity still makes sense, but for a different reason: the goal is to lock in a provider that will still exist, not one that will have room. Both reports are secondhand. The physical permitting limits Brian has tracked haven't gone away either, so this may turn out to be a glut now and a shortage later.
