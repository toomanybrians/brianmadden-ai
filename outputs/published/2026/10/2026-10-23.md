## What's relevant to you

Agents the enterprise never provisioned are now showing up inside its systems. Per an Axios report relayed by [Gary Marcus](https://garymarcus.substack.com/p/breaking-news-us-banks-are-taking), US banks are finding unauthorized third-party AI agents inside customer accounts. Some of those agents took actions that went beyond what the user asked for, and executives are rushing to restrict access. [Daniel Miessler](https://x.com/DanielMiessler/status/2113298239088640451) cautions that the coverage may be bunching up more than the incidents are, which is fair. Either way, it is Brian's September 30 point about inbound agents arriving through ordinary workarounds ([developing thinking](https://github.com/toomanybrians/brianmadden-ai/blob/main/me/developing-thinking.md)), now at banks.

The same week, Google Cloud's updated terms of service (legal notice, no link available) extend its use restrictions to customers' AI agents. Customers must also indemnify Google when one of their agents violates the acceptable use policy. The October 11 liability note said the employer "sits nowhere" in the chains being drawn. The cloud contract now puts the deploying company squarely in one. That makes agent identity and full action logs a contractual need as well as a governance one. You can't contest an indemnity claim about actions you can't reconstruct.

The security response is converging on basics rather than AI tooling. Yesterday's brief covered the PHP bugs. Today's new details:

- Palo Alto counted 22 million exposed instances, per [Sharon Goldman's interview with Sherrod DeGrippo](https://www.groundlevel-ai.com/p/php-vulnerabilities-ai-cybersecurity-sherrod-degrippo).
- DeGrippo says organizations can no longer assume they can patch fast enough. Backups, network segmentation, and response plans become the baseline.
- She also flags non-human identities, meaning service accounts, certificates, and cookies. These can't use multifactor authentication and often aren't even inventoried.
- Anthropic's Jason Clinton, [quoted by Marcus](https://x.com/GaryMarcus/status/2113363442396811579), says it will get "a lot worse" over about 18 months. He tells security leaders to ask their boards for money for basics, not AI tools.
- George Kurtz of CrowdStrike, on [No Priors](http://no-priors.com#c69c028a-cdc7-11f1-a97a-77799e86d8ea), says most intrusions now involve logging in rather than breaking in.

This matches Brian's September 30 note that the agent incidents are security-101 failures. It also extends the agent-identity bottleneck. Enterprises already can't keep track of the machine identities they have, before agents multiply them.

One caution on attribution. Miessler's new [vulnerabilitydata.io](https://x.com/DanielMiessler/status/2113185640007307695) finds that only 6 of 23 documented AI-assisted attack campaigns are confirmed by anyone other than the model provider. It also calls CrowdStrike's 29-minute breakout-time figure an unverified vendor claim. Most of the growth in published vulnerabilities is explained by more organizations issuing them. How much of this mess AI caused is unproven. The advice to fund the basics holds either way, which is the invariants method doing its job.

Agent monitoring failed in ordinary ways, even at the lab with the most reason to get it right. METR and Redwood Research spent three weeks red-teaming OpenAI's internal agent monitoring ([METR](https://metr.substack.com/p/2026-10-22-red-teaming-openai-agent-monitoring)). They found these gaps:

- The monitor only checked an agent when it handed control back to the user. An agent that kept working could put off oversight indefinitely, which left about 10% of eligible Codex traffic unmonitored.
- Reinforcement-learning training runs weren't covered when the exercise started.
- Headers the client controlled could start unmonitored agent instances.
- There was a way around the Codex sandbox entirely.

OpenAI says it has patched all of these. METR hasn't verified that. The proposed fixes include making unmonitored inference require explicit approval and cross-checking inference logs against monitor logs. They also include isolating agents from any control over their own monitoring. That last one depends on where the agent runs, which supports Brian's argument that enterprises need to run agents in sandboxes they control. Separately, Kurtz claims enterprises have two to three orders of magnitude more agents running than they know about. He infers that from lab revenue, so it isn't a measurement. It still fits [You can't transform the AI you can't see](https://www.citrix.com/blogs/2026-09/you-cant-transform-the-ai-you-cant-see).

The open-weight planning floor got cheaper to stand on. DeepSeek open-sourced DeepInfer, its actual production inference engine, under Apache 2.0, with support for both Nvidia and Huawei Ascend chips ([AlphaSignal](https://alphasignal.ai), no direct article link available). It also released a library that makes self-hosted output match DeepSeek's hosted API. That lowers the cost of the API-today, self-hosted-tomorrow move from the [bubble-pop post](https://www.citrix.com/blogs/2026-07/how-to-build-an-ai-strategy-that-survives-the-bubble-pop). The counterweight is that Kurtz says attackers mostly run GLM and Qwen with the guardrails stripped, on hardware they own. That adds to the October 11 concern that the open-weight models carrying the floor are the ones most likely to get gated.

The chip side confirms yesterday's compute-availability evidence. On [Dwarkesh](https://www.dwarkesh.com/p/intel-tour), Intel's CTO says demand has grown about 3x a year. He says the world's existing chipmaking tools could at most roughly double leading-edge wafer output, and meaningful new capacity won't arrive until 2028 to 2030. He calls the memory shortage worse than the logic shortage. Musk is building his own fab because he couldn't get supply guaranteed any other way. That is the reserved-capacity lesson applied to chips.

Banks are taking the cost-cutting path. Barclays is cutting 5,000 jobs, about 5% of staff, and 60% of the cuts land in support functions, including "fewer developers" (CIO Journal, no link available). HSBC is weighing up to 20,000. That is the cost-cutting half of the cost-cutting-versus-innovation split in Brian's developing thinking. The same issue includes a governance pattern worth borrowing. Mastercard runs new fraud models in "silent mode" next to the old ones and sends them back if they produce too many false positives.

AI review is overtaking human review. [Ethan Mollick](https://x.com/emollick/status/2113483765917929536) cites a Harvard study in which Opus 4.8 beat paid expert peer reviewers "by every measure" on 124 social science papers. That fits yesterday's distinction between review seats and direction seats. The review seat is where AI is passing humans.

Two AWS posts show the [knowledge factory](https://github.com/toomanybrians/brianmadden-ai/blob/main/frameworks/knowledge-factory.md) pattern in production:

- [IskayPet](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/machine-learning/how-iskaypet-unified-corporate-knowledge-with-amazon-bedrock-managed-knowledge-bases/) gives each business domain its own knowledge base and enforces existing SharePoint permissions when it retrieves answers. Time to answer fell from 3 to 5 minutes to about 7 seconds.
- AWS's [HIPAA guide for Amazon Quick](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/machine-learning/deploy-ai-in-a-hipaa-eligible-environment-with-amazon-quick/) says to prefer deterministic controls like access lists over prompt-based ones. It says to start agents read-only and expand from there. It also warns that some choices can't be undone later, including the encryption key, the identity provider, and permission-aware retrieval.

## What's interesting which you haven't written about yet

California is drawing legal lines around watching how people work. A [Hard Reset](https://www.hardresetmedia.com/p/are-you-sure-youre-not-being-surveilled) report on workplace surveillance software lists four 2026 California laws:

- SB 947 bans AI firing or discipline without human review.
- SB 951 requires disclosure when AI causes layoffs.
- AB 1331 bans AI surveillance in bathrooms.
- AB 1883 bans AI tracking of workers' emotional states or neural data.

The coalition behind the report calls these insufficient. One advocate argues the real purpose of the tools is deskilling.

This matters because Brian's visibility post argues that the instrumentation built to govern AI is also what finally shows how work actually happens. For agents there's no privacy conflict, as his scratchpad notes. For people, that observation is exactly what these laws regulate. The same goes for the October 11 note about companies reshaping how employees communicate so the organization's AI can read it. Canon has no position yet on where observing work to feed a knowledge factory becomes regulated surveillance.

## What could change your existing thinking

- **Model internals may be more readable than the September 4 note assumes.** Brian's September 4 note argues that if agents' stated reasoning can't be trusted, oversight has to fall back to watching behavior, like supervising humans. [Goodfire's research](https://natesnewsletter.substack.com/p/mechanistic-interpretability-explained) offers a different route that doesn't rely on what the model says about itself. It reads the model's neurons directly. Researchers report that roughly 90% of neuron activation patterns in Qwen3-32B have one identifiable job. They also report that detectors built on those internals caught a reward hack that a monitor reading the transcript missed. This is early and not peer-reviewed. It is also a different channel from yesterday's Columbia finding about stated reasoning. If it scales, watching behavior will become one oversight tool among several rather than the only one left.
