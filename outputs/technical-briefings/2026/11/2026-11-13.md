# Daily Brief—2026-11-13

I read 16 posts today—here's what's relevant to your work:

## What's relevant to you

**Nvidia is reportedly pulling back from financing its own customers.** Jensen Huang has resigned, and CFO Colette Kress is interim CEO. For enterprises, the money matters more than the personnel change.

Gary Marcus [cites a report](https://x.com/GaryMarcus/status/2120971651021942996) that Kress pushed back internally on a proposed $250 billion credit backstop for OpenAI, which Huang had championed. Nvidia has put more than $100 billion into its own customers and supply-chain partners. Marcus [also cites The Information](https://x.com/GaryMarcus/status/2121044787008614671): Nvidia is expected to slow that investment strategy, which puts billions in startup funding and credit guarantees at risk. A separate account has Huang himself worried by late summer that funding the buildout from Nvidia's balance sheet [was running into problems](https://x.com/GaryMarcus/status/2121067929911329237).

Marcus calls November "the beginning of the end" of the bubble. That's a forecast from a long-time skeptic, not evidence. The evidence is narrower. The biggest supplier in AI is reconsidering whether to keep lending customers the money to buy from it.

Brian's canon already says a pop's failure mode will be counterparty risk, not just insolvency. A pullback by Nvidia is that counterparty risk showing up. An enterprise's exposure sits in vendors (AI startups, neoclouds) whose capacity or runway depended on those guarantees. Brian's [bubble-pop checklist](https://www.citrix.com/blogs/2026-07/how-to-build-an-ai-strategy-that-survives-the-bubble-pop) was built for this case. Portable data, model routing, and the open-weight floor don't depend on which lender walks away.

**Lilly's chair and Lilly's own deployment point the same way.** Anthropic's [IPO prospectus](https://x.com/paulroetzer/status/2120864966983164321) lists Eli Lilly as a customer that gave more than 50,000 employees Claude access, with over 85% active engagement in key functions. The same day, Lilly's chair Dave Ricks [said](https://x.com/GaryMarcus/status/2120833723507568649) AI's effect on drug discovery will be "steady and incremental," because "randomized human proof is the long pole in the tent."

Those two facts don't conflict. Lilly is getting broad value from AI running everyday work while expecting slow change in the science. That's the split the bubble-pop post draws: enterprise ROI today comes from AI orchestrating work processes, not from frontier breakthroughs. One caution: the engagement figures are customer self-reports in a vendor filing, with no baseline. Salesforce's "151% increase in effective output per developer" in the same filing is the same kind of number.

**Cheap, fast models keep getting better.** Google released Gemini 4 Hydrogen at $1.50 per million input tokens and $7.50 per million output, flat across a 1-million-token context ([Demis Hassabis](https://x.com/demishassabis/status/2120942605466800374)). Google claims 2.5x faster task completion than Gemini 3.8 Flash and five times fewer fabricated claims. Box ran it through its own internal complex-work eval and measured a 5-point accuracy gain over 3.8 Flash ([Aaron Levie](https://x.com/levie/status/2120947988143198302)). Testing on your own eval is the right habit, given yesterday's story about benchmark results being edited after publication.

This supports Brian's position that work-process orchestration doesn't need frontier models. Hydrogen is hosted, not open-weight, so it doesn't raise the post-pop planning floor.

**Wharton published "a paper you can run."** Ethan Mollick's lab released [Primer](https://x.com/emollick/status/2121001056637431873). A teacher describes a lesson, and the system interviews them one question at a time. It then produces a design document and builds nothing until the teacher approves it. Learning-science rules are written into each agent's instructions rather than left to the model. The project is open source and model-agnostic.

The paper's final appendix is a prompt that tells an AI agent to read the paper and [build its own Primer](https://x.com/emollick/status/2121002669250675075). That is Brian's line from [Skills are all you need](https://www.citrix.com/blogs/2026-03/skills-are-all-you-need), "apps were always just specs wrapped in a user interface," published as a research paper. It also fits his [coaching-as-output note](https://github.com/toomanybrians/brianmadden-ai/blob/main/me/developing-thinking.md): the deliverable is a guided exercise tailored to the user, not a document.

**Shadow AI is still a capability-gap problem.** Paul Roetzer [says](https://x.com/paulroetzer/status/2120938115510468626) locked-down enterprise deployments push employees to personal ChatGPT and Claude accounts. That includes the staff who set the guardrails. Nothing here is new to Brian's canon. It is more support for the [visibility post](https://www.citrix.com/blogs/2026-09/you-cant-transform-the-ai-you-cant-see).

## What's interesting which you haven't written about yet

**Agent-built software can carry license problems nobody tracked.** Mollick's Portals project produced 204 3D worlds in about twenty hours ([Mollick](https://x.com/emollick/status/2120947095280795823)). One lead agent owned the engine and coordinated 28 parallel crews of agents. Claude's own estimate is about 25,000 agents over the life of the project. Judge agents scored every world, and automated checks rendered each one before release.

A later audit still found shader and noise code that was unlicensed or under-credited. None of it appeared in the project's own third-party notices file. The quality gates caught broken frames, but nothing checked where the code came from. Brian hasn't addressed license compliance for code produced at this scale. An enterprise that lets agents build internal tools has a legal exposure its review layer may not be checking for.

## What could change your existing thinking

- **Anthropic's filing weakens the "frontier serving may be unprofitable" scenario.** The [bubble-pop post](https://www.citrix.com/blogs/2026-07/how-to-build-an-ai-strategy-that-survives-the-bubble-pop) names unprofitable frontier serving as one way a pop could play out. Anthropic's [IPO prospectus](https://x.com/paulroetzer/status/2120864966983164321) reports a 15% adjusted operating margin and more than 4x compute efficiency since Q1 2025. Most of its $23.3 billion Q3 net loss is a $21.9 billion non-cash revaluation charge. "Adjusted" is the company's own measure. The earlier leaked filing reportedly showed about $518 billion in compute obligations. If these numbers hold, the risk of a pop sits in financing and commitments more than in whether serving a frontier model can make money. Watching who is still willing to lend would then be a better early warning than watching serving margins.

- **A model vendor is shipping routing that cuts its own token sales.** Brian has [argued](https://github.com/toomanybrians/brianmadden-ai/blob/main/me/developing-thinking.md) that routing can't be left to the model vendor, because the vendor sells tokens. Google's new Orchestrator in Antigravity switches between Gemini 4 Carbon for coordination and Hydrogen for execution within one session. Google says it [cut quota use about 24%](https://x.com/demishassabis/status/2120942605466800374). So vendors will route against their own revenue when competition demands it. A narrower version of Brian's argument survives, because Orchestrator only routes among Google's own models. Daniel Miessler's [LifeOS router](https://x.com/DanielMiessler/status/2120975883007889833) routes from the user's side instead. It defaults to Opus 5.5 and escalates to Fable for security decisions, which is closer to where Brian puts the router. If this holds, the case for a neutral router rests on routing across vendors, not on vendors refusing to economize.
