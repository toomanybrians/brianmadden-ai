# Daily Brief—2026-11-16

I read 82 posts today—here's what's relevant to your work:

## What's relevant to you

**SaaS vendors are starting to rebuild themselves as back ends for AI.** HubSpot's returning CEO says the company is ["leaning strongly into a headless version of HubSpot"](https://x.com/paulroetzer/status/2121574885138821170). Its product capability will be exposed over MCP so Claude and ChatGPT can drive it. HubSpot is middle-tier horizontal SaaS. That's the tier Brian's [SaaSpocalypse post](https://www.citrix.com/blogs/2026-04/the-saaspocalypse-wont-touch-the-enterprise-software-moat) called selectively vulnerable. Going headless is that tier deciding its future is as a system the AI operates, not as an interface workers open.

Anthropic is pushing from the other side. Claude Sheets, Whiteboards, and Data shipped deliberately less capable than Excel, Power BI, and Miro. [Linas Beliūnas reads that as intentional](https://linas.substack.com/p/weeklyfintechpulse424): the goal is for work to start and stay in Claude, with Office reduced to a file format. He also names a metric worth watching. Microsoft's Office growth now comes mostly from per-seat price increases, not new seats (5% seat growth last quarter). If revenue per user slows while seat counts hold, Claude is capturing the AI premium Microsoft priced in. Both moves are the [post-application era](https://www.citrix.com/blogs/2025-10/welcome-to-the-post-application-era) argument playing out in vendor strategy rather than in worker behavior.

**Claude Meetings ships with privacy defaults.** The new product joins Google Meet as a visible participant ([AlphaSignal](https://alphasignal.ai)'s newsletter, no direct article link available). It keeps a transcript but not audio, and by default it leaves if an outside guest joins. Brian's [second-brains post](https://www.citrix.com/blogs/2026-02/workers-second-brains-break-every-assumption-about-how-we-secure-knowledge-work) said to assume anything a worker hears ends up in their AI within seconds. Here a vendor is setting the boundary itself, at the meeting level. That helps when the meeting runs on the sanctioned tool. It does nothing for the personal recorder in the next browser tab.

**The FBI's ARTEX alert shows what machine-speed attacks look like.** The bureau warns that attackers are using an open-source agent framework to automate intrusions, some reaching privileged access in under three hours ([via Daniel Miessler](https://x.com/DanielMiessler/status/2121641037458554993)). None of the exploits are new. ARTEX chains SQL injection, leaked credentials, and misconfigured cloud permissions faster than a human operator would.

Miessler's [follow-up analysis](https://x.com/DanielMiessler/status/2121487303160025544) of five such frameworks has two details that matter for agent governance. First, the agents act in bursts of 2 to 25 seconds. Second, credential revocation at AWS, Google, and Entra ID can take up to 60 to 90 minutes to propagate. If the identity system can't cut access for an hour, killing the session where the work is executing is the faster control. That is a workspace-layer action, consistent with [the workspace as control plane](https://www.citrix.com/blogs/2025-05/the-desktop-has-dissolved-now-where-does-work-live-in-2025). The alert also supports Brian's [developing argument](https://github.com/toomanybrians/brianmadden-ai/blob/main/me/developing-thinking.md) that human approval is the weak link. At this speed, the containment rules have to be decided before the incident.

**More sources now measure cost per finished task instead of price per token.** Nate [cites Ramp data](https://natesnewsletter.substack.com/p/ai-evals-pricing-power) showing enterprise token spend flat for three months while usage keeps climbing. His explanation is that buyers now test cheaper models against their own requirements and switch when those models pass. He also notes the second-order effect. Infosys's clients asked for AI savings to be passed through in price, so a margin gain can be competed away entirely.

Three other items point the same direction:
- AWS and NVIDIA's [Switchyard routing sample](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/machine-learning/route-coding-agent-requests-to-the-right-model-on-amazon-bedrock-with-nvidia-nemo-switchyard/) uses "cost per solved task" as its metric. It also warns that escalating a failed task to a bigger model replays the whole failed history, which can cost more than starting on the big model.
- Anthropic's own guidance tells customers to [default to Opus 5.5 and reserve Fable 5.2](https://linas.substack.com/p/claude-fable-5-2-prompting-guide) for the hardest work. One prompt cost $24 or $642 depending on the effort setting.
- Ethan Mollick's [SatBench](https://x.com/emollick/status/2121753306855067770) finds 63% of lab-cited benchmarks saturated. Only 24% are saturated on independently verified scores.

Together these support Brian's October note that the unit that matters is cost per valuable unit of work. Public benchmarks are wearing out, so a company's own evals are becoming its main bargaining tool.

**One estimate puts numbers on diminishing returns.** Alex Imas [ran Anthropic's METR data](https://aleximas.substack.com/p/are-we-approaching-an-intelligence) through standard research-productivity models. Capability growth roughly doubled after October 2025. Over the same period, R&D spend rose about 6.2x and technical headcount about 3.1x. Capability per dollar fell about 69%, and he finds no evidence of self-sustaining AI-driven research. That supports Brian's view that the investment curve is unsustainable. Progress is being bought with steeply rising spend.

**Anthropic's S-1 contains a sentence worth quoting.** According to [Matt Stoller's roundup](https://www.thebignewsletter.com/p/monopoly-round-up-microsoft-the-faa), the filing says Anthropic "may deploy models whose behavior we cannot adequately verify." Brian has a developing note that deployers increasingly can't state what their systems do. This is that admission, made by the vendor in a securities filing. The same roundup ties last week's two-day Microsoft 365 outage to layoffs and AI-written code. No root cause has been published, so treat that link as opinion.

## What's interesting which you haven't written about yet

**Companies are blocking customers' agents to protect their margins.** United now bans AI agents from booking or changing tickets, and can cancel a bot-made booking even if a human supervised it. Amazon is blocking third-party shopping agents. Tomasz Tunguz ([newsletter](https://tomtunguz.com), no direct article link available) argues the stated security reason isn't the real driver. The real driver is economics: agents skip sponsored listings, decline seat upgrades, and uncheck add-on insurance. United's ancillary fees exceed its operating income, and Amazon's non-AWS profit is effectively its ad business. Shopify takes a flat cut of every transaction, has no upsell to lose, and has opened checkout to agents.

Agent request volume is up 16x since February, per the Cloudflare figure Tunguz cites. Mollick describes research agents reading the Internet Archive [line by line through repeated searches](https://x.com/emollick/status/2121641417483169934). Brian's canon treats other organizations' agents arriving at a company as a security threat. This adds a business-model question he hasn't addressed. Any enterprise whose margin sits in add-ons will have to choose between admitting customers' agents and repricing. There's an internal side too. An employee's travel agent can now be refused at the airline.

**Agents that each represent a different person coordinate badly.** An Anthropic study ([via Paradigm 3](https://p3humansonai.substack.com/p/humans-on-ai-70-november-13-2026)) compared one shared agent serving a team against each person's agent acting independently. The independent setups stalled, misallocated shared resources, and made unsupported claims about each other two to three times as often. Brian's 2031 picture has every worker arriving with their own agents. This is early evidence that putting those agents in a room produces worse group outcomes than one shared agent, which is an argument for the shared knowledge factory over everyone bringing their own.

## What could change your existing thinking

- **A model vendor now offers a self-hosted agent executor.** Brian's [developing thinking](https://github.com/toomanybrians/brianmadden-ai/blob/main/me/developing-thinking.md) calls the governed agent executor "genuinely unoccupied." It also says customers will never accept a sandbox the AI provider supplies. Anthropic's private beta of self-hosted Claude Managed Agents ([AlphaSignal](https://alphasignal.ai)'s newsletter, no direct article link available) runs the agent server, database, and sandboxes in the customer's own cloud. Credentials and session history stay in-house, and the model provider is selectable, including an internal gateway. That answers the "someone else's afterthought" objection on infrastructure ownership. What it doesn't cover, from what's described, is corporate identity, the legacy app estate, and the desktop. If it holds, the executor argument will have to rest on those pieces rather than on who owns the sandbox.

- **The open-weight planning floor has a political risk.** Brian's [bubble-pop post](https://www.citrix.com/blogs/2026-07/how-to-build-an-ai-strategy-that-survives-the-bubble-pop) treats released weights as the floor nobody can take away. Today's best open models are Chinese. Nathan Lambert [warns](https://www.interconnects.ai/p/open-source-ai-without-jensen) that the US open ecosystem just lost its main corporate defender. He also warns that one high-profile attack enabled by a Chinese open-weight model could trigger a regulatory crisis. Speakers on [Moonshots](https://www.diamandis.com/podcast#7d16d8d4-dfb4-11f1-86ed-d365f2f0850d) expect a push to restrict Chinese open-weight models in the US market. This is speculation, not pending law. But the weights staying downloadable doesn't help a regulated enterprise that isn't allowed to run them. The floor depends on usage rights as well as possession.

- **Interpretability may weaken the "weights are a black box" argument.** Part of Brian's case for file-based memory is that you can diff markdown but can't inspect what a model carries in its weights. Goodfire [claims](https://archive.thedeepview.com/p/ai-s-black-box-myth-is-no-longer-an-excuse) individual neurons in a 35B model can be probed, understood, and predictably manipulated, with research agents doing most of the work. It describes a possible "lie detector" for what a model knows. Scaling to frontier models could cost tens to hundreds of millions of dollars, and these are the company's own claims. If the method scales, auditing weights becomes expensive rather than impossible. The file-based case would then rest more on portability and cost than on inspectability.
