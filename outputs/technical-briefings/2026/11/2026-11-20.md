# Daily Brief—2026-11-20

I read 53 posts today—here's what's relevant to your work:

## What's relevant to you

**Apollo Research has turned "who watches the agents" into an audit checklist.** Apollo proposes that independent evaluators work inside AI labs and test six claims about how internal agents are monitored ([Apollo Research](https://x.com/ApolloResearch/status/2123446207217389963)):

1. Monitors cover every deployment.
2. Monitors would detect an agent working against the lab's intent.
3. The monitors themselves can be trusted.
4. Incidents reach the right people in time.
5. Nothing was missed in the past.
6. Monitoring will still hold for future, more capable models.

The premise is the part that matters for Brian. Apollo says AI monitors built from the same internal models are now the main oversight mechanism. That is the open question in Brian's September 4 note in his [developing thinking](https://github.com/toomanybrians/brianmadden-ai/blob/main/me/developing-thinking.md): watching everything an agent touches only works if another agent does the watching, and you can't read that agent's reasoning either.

Apollo doesn't solve that problem, and it says the science is nascent. It does name the specific failure modes:
- A monitor might collude with the agent it watches or under-report it. A monitor that knows it's being tested might behave well only during the test ([Apollo](https://x.com/ApolloResearch/status/2123446205304762769)).
- Apollo counts something as "detected" only when a human confirms it.
- Evaluators should check who actually has the authority and the access to stop a model, including off-hours ([Apollo](https://x.com/ApolloResearch/status/2123446205694754996)).

Apollo wrote this for labs, but it applies to enterprises. Brian's September 30 note says companies have to run agents in environments they control. These six claims are what that control would have to prove.

Anthropic's IPO prospectus makes the same point from the vendor's side. Per [Ground Level AI](https://www.groundlevel-ai.com/p/anthropic-ipo-enterprise-ai), Anthropic acknowledges its agents can act in ways no one requested, and says tracing their actions across long workflows is hard. The model provider can't see a customer's SaaS apps or data, so governance stays with the customer. That is [the workspace as control plane](https://www.citrix.com/blogs/2025-05/the-desktop-has-dissolved-now-where-does-work-live-in-2025) argument, coming from Anthropic's own risk disclosures.

**BMW's agent governance matches Brian's architecture closely.** BMW runs agents across 12,500+ developers ([AWS](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/machine-learning/how-the-bmw-group-governs-hundreds-of-agents-across-thousands-of-developers/)). Four of its choices line up with Brian's thinking:

- **Governance sits in the integration layer.** Rules live in an MCP gateway and a central registry, not in each agent's code, so they survive model and framework changes. That is "govern it where work happens."
- **Rules are enforced in code, not in prompts.** Trust is set per workflow step and enforced by CI gates and policy-as-code. That matches Brian's point that the control belongs in the harness's action-authorization layer, not in model judgment.
- **Capability is layered.** BMW composes tools into skills and skills into flows, which maps onto the lower layers of [the cognitive stack](https://www.citrix.com/blogs/2026-02/understanding-the-cognitive-stack-why-your-ai-strategy-is-focused-on-the-wrong-layer).
- **Teams build, a central team curates.** Teams build locally and contribute to a shared registry, and a central team enforces checks. That is the knowledge factory's sequence: enable the pioneers, then industrialize what they proved.

BMW also runs a "governance agent" that audits other agents, with a human required to sign off before any fix is applied. That is Apollo's monitor problem again, inside a real company.

The results BMW reports are self-reported in a vendor blog. Separately, Microsoft's new persistent agents get their own ID and an assigned human "manager" visible to IT ([The Deep View](https://archive.thedeepview.com/p/newsom-pitches-california-ai-rules-as-us-template)). That is the restricted-rights agent identity Brian argues for, shipping by default.

**The bubble now has hard numbers and a physical constraint.** Anthropic's IPO filing disclosed $900.9 billion in compute commitments, with $70.7 billion due within 12 months (CIO Journal). An FT estimate puts the revenue needed for a 20% return on roughly $1 trillion of compute at about $602 billion a year ([via Gary Marcus](https://x.com/GaryMarcus/status/2123378742408188279)). SoftBank, one of OpenAI's lead backers, fell over 14% in Tokyo ([Gary Marcus](https://x.com/GaryMarcus/status/2123640152106951134)).

The physical constraint showed up at the ballot box. All 39 ballot measures the [Forecasting Research Institute](https://forecastingresearch.substack.com/p/early-findings-from-our-efforts-to) tracked went in favor of limiting data centers, and forecasters had put under 10% odds on that outcome. That supports Brian's September 25 point that compute availability depends on real-world build timelines and local politics. None of this settles timing, which is why [the bubble-pop post](https://www.citrix.com/blogs/2026-07/how-to-build-an-ai-strategy-that-survives-the-bubble-pop) says to plan on invariants rather than a date.

**Runway gives rare before-and-after numbers for worker-built software.** Runway built an internal ad-production agent and went from 77 ads a week to about 900. Return on ad spend reportedly doubled, and cost per subscriber fell to 41% of its previous level ([The Artificial Intelligence Show](https://podcast.smarterx.ai/shownotes/253)). Its no-code App Studio has produced hundreds of internal apps, including one built to strip an unwanted piano sound from video generations. That is [the post-application era](https://www.citrix.com/blogs/2025-10/welcome-to-the-post-application-era) at company scale. The caveat is that ads are countable output, so this doesn't fix the measurement problem for knowledge work in general.

## What's interesting which you haven't written about yet

**The OS vendor is becoming the gatekeeper for computer-use agents.** In macOS 27.3, enabling ChatGPT's computer-use agent takes 14 steps, up from 4. Apple cites security and is building its own agent ([Ethan Mollick](https://x.com/emollick/status/2123541572490817605), via [Mark Gurman](https://x.com/paulroetzer/status/2123603307369480496)).

Brian has asked whose computer a computer-use agent is actually using. Here the answer is being set by the company that owns the operating system, and that company sells a competing agent. For an enterprise, this is another reason the governance layer for agents on the endpoint shouldn't belong to a party with its own agent to sell.

**Liability for agent purchase mistakes is being assigned, in opposite directions.** Yesterday's brief noted that a wrong agent purchase settles like a normal transaction. Klarna's new shopping agent has Klarna carry the risk of bad purchases and markets that as the reason to trust it. ChatGPT Wallet leaves that risk with merchants and card issuers ([Linas Beliūnas](https://linas.substack.com/p/fintechpulse1154)).

AWS's AgentCore payments add a useful control ([AWS](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/machine-learning/enable-ai-agents-to-pay-for-aws-marketplace-x402-products-with-amazon-bedrock-agentcore-payments/)). One IAM role can set a budget but can't pay. Another can pay but can't raise its own budget. That caps how much an agent spends. It doesn't check whether the agent bought the right thing.

Consumers aren't ready to hand over control yet. In Salesforce's survey, 73% won't give payment credentials to a personal agent, and only 8% have had one buy something for them ([Salesforce](https://www.salesforce.com/news/stories/cyber-week-ai-sales-2026/)).

## What could change your existing thinking

- **Two operators say forward-deployed engineering is a phase, not a durable spending category.** Brian's "follow the FDE money" argument treats FDEs standing up knowledge factories as the next big category of IT spend. Jack Dorsey calls harnesses, Claude Code, and FDE setups "a gap in capability from the model itself" that will shrink as models improve ([HBR IdeaCast](https://hbr.org/podcast/2026/11/innovation-summit-2026-blocks-jack-dorsey-on-building-a-company-around-intelligence)). Bob McGrew, who helped build Palantir's FDE model, adds a condition. FDEs only produce leverage if what they learn on site gets built back into the product. "If they have to keep filling the gap, you get no leverage" ([No Priors](http://no-priors.com#8fd14988-e40e-11f1-99ee-ebfb28833f04)). Together they argue that FDE spending is a bridge to productized software, and that firms that don't productize are running consulting businesses. That fits Brian's own "consulting firms in disguise" note. It cuts against reading the FDE money as a long-lived category.

- **The open-weight planning floor may lose supply, not just access.** Brian's October 11 note covers pressure from hosting limits, license terms, and procurement rules. Julian Gewirtz adds pressure on the supply side ([80,000 Hours](https://80000hours.org/podcast/episodes/julian-gewirtz-china-us-ai-deal/?utm_campaign=podcast__julian-gewirtz&utm_source=80000+Hours+Podcast&utm_medium=podcast)). China's TC260 3.0 safety framework now warns that open releases lower the barrier to cyberattacks. Gewirtz says Beijing could restrict open releases "quite rapidly," because it only needs to look more open than the US. The models carrying the floor today are Chinese, and enterprise use is already exposed. Airbnb's customer-service AI reportedly runs on Qwen, which Airbnb disputes, and that is now caught up in the proposed US disclosure rule covered yesterday ([Moonshots](https://www.diamandis.com/podcast#c1898206-e3aa-11f1-8d5a-87b2ae3e8891)). Meanwhile AWS is making Kimi's coding agent easier to run on Bedrock ([AWS](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/machine-learning/use-kimi-code-cli-with-models-on-amazon-bedrock/)). Weights already released stay downloadable, but the next generation of the floor may not arrive.
