# Daily Brief—2026-11-24

I read 47 posts today—here's what's relevant to your work:

## What's relevant to you

**The labs are running Brian's bottleneck argument on themselves, and Apollo Research measured where it leads.** Apollo's report on "Handoff" covers frontier labs delegating their own AI research work to AI agents ([Apollo Research](https://x.com/ApolloResearch/status/2124850458296737905)). The numbers:

- AI agents write over 90% of the code merged into Anthropic's codebase.
- Human reviewers at OpenAI see 0.001% to 0.01% of internal coding trajectories.
- Claude now leads 26% of Anthropic's research work, up from under 1% in February.
- Claude sessions spend about 80% of their time waiting on a human.

Apollo's point is that the remaining human steps now set the pace, so each further step of automation pays off more than the last ([Apollo Research](https://x.com/ApolloResearch/status/2124850457994764399)). This is Brian's September 4 argument in his [developing thinking](https://github.com/toomanybrians/brianmadden-ai/blob/main/me/developing-thinking.md): the human step is both the bottleneck and not doing its job, so removing it is rational.

Apollo names two likely outcomes. Humans rubber-stamp AI-filtered summaries, or review gets handed to more agents. The second is the gap Brian flagged and couldn't answer: the watcher becomes another agent whose reasoning nobody can read.

A partial answer showed up the same day. Google DeepMind ran teams of Gemini 4 agents on quantum algorithms and checked every result with a formal proof checker instead of a human reviewer ([Google DeepMind](https://x.com/GoogleDeepMind/status/2124911075875258776)). Mechanical verification can replace the human reviewer where the work can be proven, but most enterprise work can't be. Coding leads knowledge work, so enterprises will hit the same wall once agent volume turns review into sampling.

**Anthropic's study of 58 companies says the value comes from reorganizing, not from the tools.** Across 66 interviews, the highest-value uses lined up with real organizational change: merged roles, new reporting lines, redesigned processes. Uses that fit into existing structures rated lower. The companies followed three strategies, with different results ([Ethan Mollick](https://x.com/emollick/status/2125098309928812898)):

- **Top-down automation** got efficiency, and distrust.
- **Bottom-up experimentation** kept jobs, but adoption was uneven and gains capped out.
- **Deliberate reorganization** moved people onto the tasks where AI was bottlenecked, and delivered the most.

That is the [factory electrification](https://www.citrix.com/blogs/2025-07/to-understand-ais-future-impact-check-out-this-playbook-from-150-years-ago) point in field data. It backs Brian's August position that [the knowledge factory](https://github.com/toomanybrians/brianmadden-ai/blob/main/frameworks/knowledge-factory.md) is the redesign itself. It also cuts against his published core thesis that worker-led adoption will always outpace corporate programs. His developing thinking already says to enable the pioneers and then industrialize what they proved, and this is evidence for the second half.

Mollick adds that outside coding, even the most advanced uses in the study weren't very advanced ([Ethan Mollick](https://x.com/emollick/status/2125098776025080203)). The enterprise lag is visible from inside too. One practitioner puts enterprise Copilot about a year behind the frontier, with workers routing around it using personal tools and recording devices ([Work Evolved](https://workevolved.substack.com/p/thoughts-about-ai-right-now)).

On jobs, the same study found few direct layoffs. Only two companies had eliminated a position through automation. But two-thirds had reduced hiring, nine had cut contracted or offshore roles, and five had pushed out employees who wouldn't adopt AI. Andrew Yang argues a recession will give employers cover for the cuts they've been deferring ([Andrew Yang](https://blog.andrewyang.com/p/anthropic-exhaustion)).

**Zero data retention for frontier models has an end date, and AWS is selling the replacement.** Bedrock's new Trusted Access API launched with two Anthropic offerings covering Claude Fable, Mythos, and Opus/Sonnet 5.5 ([AWS](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/machine-learning/introducing-the-amazon-bedrock-trusted-access-api-with-anthropics-enterprise-frontier-safeguards-and-enterprise-data-controls/)). Here is how it works:

- Both offerings retain prompts and completions and scan them for misuse patterns that span many requests.
- The scan produces a "Safety Case" that the customer's own team reviews, not AWS or Anthropic staff.
- In the stricter version, retained data sits in the customer's S3 bucket under the customer's own keys. Revoking that access cuts off the model within 24 hours.

AWS calls this the long-term replacement for zero-retention exceptions, which are only guaranteed through June 30, 2027. Retention is becoming the price of frontier access, and the review workload lands on the enterprise's security team. It also gives incident investigators logs to work from, held under the customer's key. That matches Brian's [visibility post](https://www.citrix.com/blogs/2026-09/you-cant-transform-the-ai-you-cant-see): record what agents do, and keep the record yourself. The adoption claim of "a quarter of the Fortune 100" comes from AWS itself.

**An agent's choice can flip on one sentence of context.** Wharton ran about 26,000 tests across six frontier models ([Ethan Mollick](https://x.com/emollick/status/2124970360445796544)). Shopping agents gave stable picks only when shown a bare product grid:

- Adding one outside source, such as a Reddit thread, moved one model's choice by 99 percentage points.
- One injected memory sentence ("I love hiking!") steered most models away from the best-value product.
- Delivering the same sources in one tool call versus three also changed the outcome.

Mollick warns that each model reacts differently, so no single fix generalizes ([Ethan Mollick](https://x.com/emollick/status/2124971840997040163)). For enterprises, the mechanism matters more than the shopping. Any agent making procurement, triage, or routing decisions is steered by whatever reaches its context, and its memory is an injection point. That is the knowledge factory's argument about curating what AI sees, now as a security control.

**OpenAI's accounting agent shows the system of record deciding how much an agent can do.** ChatGPT for Accounting codes invoices, drafts bank reconciliations, and prepares month-end and audit evidence. QuickBooks lets it write entries straight into the books. Xero, BILL, and FreshBooks keep it read-only ([Linas Beliūnas](https://linas.substack.com/p/fintechpulse1155)). This is Brian's "AI dissolves UIs, not systems of record" from [the SaaSpocalypse post](https://www.citrix.com/blogs/2026-04/the-saaspocalypse-wont-touch-the-enterprise-software-moat), showing up as an access decision. The ledger vendor sets the agent's write scope, not the customer. The work being automated is also what firms used to train junior accountants, which lands on Brian's open question about how juniors will build judgment.

**AI server prices are rising on contracts already signed.** Dell, HPE, Lenovo, and Supermicro are raising AI server prices 25% to 45% on existing contracts, because memory costs have risen more than 400% since 2024 (CIO Journal newsletter, no direct article link). The [bubble-pop post](https://www.citrix.com/blogs/2026-07/how-to-build-an-ai-strategy-that-survives-the-bubble-pop) estimated about $300K for hardware to self-host open-weight models at full speed, and that number is going up. Yesterday's brief covered the same memory squeeze hitting laptops. The counterpoint is that a 128GB MacBook Pro now runs 70B-class models locally ([The Deep View](https://archive.thedeepview.com/p/macbook-pro-s-real-upgrade-isn-t-the-touchscreen)).

**"Knowledge factory" is now vendor vocabulary.** [Tomasz Tunguz](https://tomtunguz.com) (newsletter, no direct article link) notes that Notion and LangChain published the same architecture on the same day, and LangChain's post is titled "How to Build a Knowledge Factory." Their loop adds an execution trace and a "curator" that rewrites specs based on that trace. Brian's version forbids hand-editing the canon and requires fixes to go through ingestion. A curator fits that rule only if the trace is treated as a new raw input rather than outputs editing the canon directly. Tunguz points out that neither vendor post includes outcome data.

## What's interesting which you haven't written about yet

**Security vendors are buying the agent-governance seat.** CrowdStrike's $43.9B all-stock acquisition of Zscaler is pitched as securing autonomous agents and agent-to-data network traffic (CIO Journal newsletter, no direct article link). In the same week, Palo Alto Networks bought Applied Compute, an inference and post-training infrastructure startup, for $4.5B ([Edith Yeung](https://edithyeung.substack.com/p/10-exits-in-silicon-valley-week-of)). This repeats the August pattern, when the neutral routing seat went to payments companies.

The tension with canon is specific. Brian has argued that a forward proxy can't tell whether a user pasted a customer database or asked for a haiku. Zscaler's core architecture is proxy-based. Either the combined company moves enforcement into the session and the browser, or it is claiming agent governance from the layer Brian says can't see the content.

## What could change your existing thinking

- **Workers may not be keeping the time AI saves.** Brian's December 2025 post argued that workers want to "[leave at 5](https://www.citrix.com/blogs/2025-12/it-admits-workers-control-ai-workers-admit-they-use-it-to-leave-at-5)" and keep the saved time for themselves. Anthropic's 58-company study found most AI value came from increased output. Only about a tenth of uses delivered value employees captured as reduced workload ([Ethan Mollick](https://x.com/emollick/status/2125098309928812898)). Interviews may undercount time that workers quietly keep. Still, if it holds, the breathing room Brian described is being converted into output faster than he assumed.

- **Frontier models may stop being interchangeable parts.** The knowledge factory argument treats models as swappable, with routing decided mostly by cost and layer. [Nate B. Jones](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=y0WVOisJ6Js) argues frontier models are diverging into specialists. GPT-6.2 Astra is an exhaustive detail-checker that writes poorly, and Gemini 4 Neon is a fast, readable thought partner. He recommends choosing a model by the shape of the task ([Nate's Substack](https://natesnewsletter.substack.com/p/judge-ai-models)). [Gary Marcus](https://x.com/GaryMarcus/status/2124830473490997441) amplifies a matching claim that math capability is exploding while writing has stalled. If this holds, routing gets a second axis beyond cost, which complicates [the Excel routing example](https://www.citrix.com/blogs/2026-05/why-enterprise-ai-agents-disappoint-and-why-the-fix-is-not-better-agents).

- **The open-weight floor may cover orchestration but not unsupervised agents.** The [bubble-pop post](https://www.citrix.com/blogs/2026-07/how-to-build-an-ai-strategy-that-survives-the-bubble-pop) says anything you can do with Sonnet today will survive a pop. A Lawrence Livermore benchmark found that only the top frontier models noticed when their simulator was broken and lowered their confidence. Weaker and open-weight models reported confident wrong answers ([Import AI](https://importai.substack.com/p/import-ai-482-huawei-kernel-benchmarks)). An agent that can't tell when its own tools are lying needs a human or a checker behind it. So the floor may hold for supervised work but not for unattended agents.
