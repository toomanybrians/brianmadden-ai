# Daily Brief—2026-11-23

I read 121 posts today—here's what's relevant to your work:

## What's relevant to you

**Last week's outage wave looks more self-inflicted than attacked, and agents made the attack side faster.** Microsoft 365, the FAA, several banks, and an NSA network all went down in the same week. Microsoft's recovery made its own outage worse: traffic rerouted to healthy servers knocked those servers over too ([Nate B. Jones](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fFeyE4nINqs)). CoreWeave's CISO said the industry is "doing it to ourselves" by rushing AI-found patches out under management pressure ([Ground Level AI](https://www.groundlevel-ai.com/p/ai-cybersecurity-washington-dc-dispatch)). [Humans on AI](https://p3humansonai.substack.com/p/humans-on-ai-72-november-20-2026) attributes the outages to AI-accelerated patching moving faster than engineers' understanding of their own systems.

The offense side changed too:
- An open-source agent-coordination framework called Artex is being used in live intrusions. Google measured one Artex-linked actor reaching deep access in 15 minutes.
- Nicole Perlroth describes a multi-agent swarm that bypassed MFA, exfiltrated data, and encrypted a company's systems in 14 minutes ([via Gary Marcus](https://x.com/GaryMarcus/status/2123793884321038833)).
- Daniel Miessler's new [AI hacking-incident ledger](https://x.com/DanielMiessler/status/2123770284826329441) counts 177 reported incidents so far in 2026. AI did actual attack work in 79 of them. In 2024 that number was zero.

This fits Brian's September 30 note in his [developing thinking](https://github.com/toomanybrians/brianmadden-ai/blob/main/me/developing-thinking.md): the incidents are mostly security-101 failures, but speed makes them scary anyway. One detail from the ConnectWise case extends his argument about agent identity. ConnectWise disabled seven compromised application identities within hours. Restoring access took weeks, because every partner had to re-authorize a replacement app separately for each Microsoft 365 tenant. Brian has argued that enterprise IT can't provision non-human identities at scale. This shows the restore path is just as slow, and it's the step everyone needs during an incident.

**The pop Brian expects appears to be under way.** Brian's [bubble-pop post](https://www.citrix.com/blogs/2026-07/how-to-build-an-ai-strategy-that-survives-the-bubble-pop) says to plan on invariants rather than a date, so the stock moves matter less than what they do to capital:
- Anthropic is trading near its IPO price, and top AI stocks are down about 50% from peak ([Gary Marcus](https://garymarcus.substack.com/p/breaking-anthropic-stock-is-crashing)).
- Wall Street firms pledged $500 billion in data-center loans in August and reportedly haven't disbursed any of it ([Newcomer](https://www.newcomer.co/p/after-anthropic-euphoria-nvidia-amd)).
- SpaceX, rated investment grade, just borrowed secured debt at 8.26% ([via Gary Marcus](https://x.com/GaryMarcus/status/2124707151138652338)).

More expensive capital will reach enterprises as token prices and access terms. It won't arrive as headlines. On the enterprise side, OneTrust models a $100 million pilot opportunity shrinking to about $30 million in production once reliability, adoption, and realized benefit are applied ([The Deep View](https://archive.thedeepview.com/p/why-ai-value-shrinks-after-the-pilot)). Another Deep View issue pairs 81% of CIOs planning to raise AI spend with 11% of CFOs seeing clear ROI ([The Deep View](https://archive.thedeepview.com/p/anthropic-s-ipo-is-a-trillion-dollar-bet-on-trust)). That gap is where Brian's "separate what's real from what isn't" will play out.

**Three separate sources landed on the knowledge factory's core rule: shared canon beats personal brains.** Nate B. Jones argues teams should keep decisions and instructions in a versioned repo "like code" ([Nate B. Jones](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=v5I0nOoqAV4)). He also warns against letting each worker write personal skills, which he calls "skill theater" that has to be undone later. Nous Research's Hermes Enterprise turns one employee's working approach into a reviewed, team-wide skill ([AlphaSignal](https://alphasignal.ai), no direct article link). AWS Quick now mixes deterministic steps into AI flows, explicitly to capture process knowledge that otherwise lives in one person's head ([AWS](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/machine-learning/new-in-aws-quick-more-capable-flows-an-updated-web-experience-and-agents-that-work-together/)). This matches Brian's August 28 point that everyone building their own brain produces chaos, and that the individual piece becomes a sandbox against [the knowledge factory](https://github.com/toomanybrians/brianmadden-ai/blob/main/frameworks/knowledge-factory.md)'s canon.

**Two findings in Miessler's newsletter back Brian's bottleneck argument.** Both appear in [Unsupervised Learning 552](https://link.mail.beehiiv.com/ss/c/u001.ocM-1BQI0jjew2YhfroIfT7PkYaTj-khOf1PTf4goqLXfwnM8jNEHDig4rTNmLJjLTTcc0mPN6Pcb3P9S_MP4fM-RYqaKpXsmjksDr4tYVrqv-vIfcjU-lsImngxe1nPPevTFYyzaVkyjL6MKw6bVBgccaGoVz7B7wzvDUAqDpafakTa3_aH9PTh5tn5nwoGaCheDY78JHjddcJW_OKxGctYKXmOPeqx57lLb4VzloVP1zmT4xfHW2k-FgTO_7FKWa0yMgRsHhTSlCqgV6wwWmEndRB0B9jd5zXgseRkLHdjm8F8CV8g95dERPKqGo6k_fCpHsCePyILXi44QUNrsCTbj0lzL3UlbaJpBpaj0IPwVrKjI9kq4YHFLe3gbui8V-3_ySf3U39IShRxPO9RDuuhnD5LPDZTe0tMVQqhFr7DhrCN_aqaMuXHQ-C19V6jIPfmYx37VBWC2GqWptdRzUgkX9rAFiJ4hU15FYYMZA0PeJmVjbLQWpbYueAGnXjhB8GQViNTfhJUxB_X-RMIVSuLjwEezAooQhKMVOkd1MpKlEB3pmweUXDgTQeZ48mqgwEjUcML88yKubex_hdKxY1yi-LOL-Crpb34A-WsDhlEZ_xw8hc4Jh1-YX9CNCZ28YxGhPUx-uje4K-6LpmXN15Of8PEz5MZRukG-KHX1O43kPqPL4_3kUvoJ_4YtPPNAtaFKx5IQl54ZO8zyUgIDA728uAdY4ruUMYPmPxoBWw6VBNGu-fT4phLL16sM6INosyhS8vKLdQ0DHm2E6cmDKYxZOhSBj1DHgowSvHL7_8/4vy/5QV7WExSSnaS31epbSUjPA/h0/h001.eLIKxFJk0gG9AymSr8lvczvGZRbphOd_437GKwpJN5w).

- **Interdependent tasks cap the gain.** An economic model by Jones and Tonetti finds that when tasks depend on each other, automating even a large share at high productivity produces small output gains. The slowest step still sets the pace. That is Brian's "1+1+1+1=1.5" in formal terms.
- **AI-assisted skill doesn't stick.** In a BCG study of 487 consultants, ChatGPT let them do data-science work outside their expertise. Once the tool was removed, they showed no retained skill. This is more evidence for Brian's open question about how juniors build judgment when AI does the learning work.

On agent purchases, Visa's benchmark (covered yesterday) adds a detail about how agents fail. They rarely misread facts. They fail because they don't ask the question that would surface a hidden requirement. Banks are splitting on who pays for the mistake. ## What's interesting which you haven't written about yet

**AI's memory demand is raising the price of ordinary computers.** DRAM prices rose about 700% in 18 months. The three makers holding about 90% of the market shifted production to high-bandwidth memory for AI accelerators. Samsung and SK Hynix reportedly committed 40% of global DRAM output to OpenAI, and a price-fixing suit against all three is moving forward ([BIG](https://www.thebignewsletter.com/p/a-700-price-hike-on-a-key-component)). The estimated effect is 8 to 15% added to laptop and phone prices.

This matters for Brian's endpoint trend. His August test ran Qwen3.8 locally using about 22GB of RAM. The bet that a worker's daily AI will soon run on the laptop depends on memory-heavy machines getting cheaper. Right now they're getting more expensive, because the datacenter build-out is outbidding the endpoint for the same chips.

## What could change your existing thinking

- **The open-weight floor may stop rising, even though it won't disappear.** The [bubble-pop post](https://www.citrix.com/blogs/2026-07/how-to-build-an-ai-strategy-that-survives-the-bubble-pop) treats open-weight models as a floor that keeps climbing. Ethan Mollick suggests the recent slowdown in open-weight progress may be linked to OpenAI and Anthropic shutting down easy distillation of their models. OpenAI is now actively detecting coordinated distillation attempts ([Ethan Mollick](https://x.com/emollick/status/2123867811005014143)). Separately, Sam Altman is proposing that frontier labs agree on common capability limits tied to pretraining perplexity ([Sam Altman](https://x.com/sama/status/2124551594691932405)). If distillation was a major driver of open-weight gains, the floor will rise more slowly. Combined with yesterday's China supply risk, the floor looks more like a fixed level than a moving one. One counterpoint: Kimi K3.1 Pro still leads open models by a wide margin on coding-agent benchmarks ([The AI Realist](https://www.airealist.ai/p/mistral-vibe-large-4-best-open-duo)).

- **Verification cost, not just time, may decide how far coding's lead transfers to knowledge work.** Brian's [coding-as-leading-indicator post](https://www.citrix.com/blogs/2026-02/what-will-knowledge-work-be-in-18-months-look-at-what-ai-is-doing-to-coding-right-now) assumes knowledge work follows coding about 18 months behind. METR finds that the cost of building and scoring a task predicts how hard it is for models, even when task length is held constant ([METR](https://metr.substack.com/p/2026-11-20-q4-2026-capability-update)). Models also stay weak on "judgment" tasks with slow or fuzzy feedback. This week's Riemann Hypothesis result illustrates the split. It rested on a 17-million-line machine-checked proof. It also required private frontier models that only an Anthropic employee had access to ([The Algorithmic Bridge](https://www.thealgorithmicbridge.com/p/ai-just-cracked-the-hardest-problem)). If METR is right, work that is expensive to verify won't simply lag coding. Some of it may not follow at all until someone builds the verification layer, which is the problem Brian flagged in February.
