# Daily Brief—2026-11-26

I read 27 posts today—here's what's relevant to your work:

## What's relevant to you

**Open-weight models now carry almost a third of enterprise AI traffic, and Congress wants the Chinese ones out of critical systems.** [Sharon Goldman](https://sharongoldman.substack.com/p/us-china-ai-race-open-weight-models) cites Databricks' AI Index of 10,000+ businesses. Open-weight models grew from 7.3% to 30.9% of enterprise token traffic between July and November. DeepSeek V4.1 and GLM 5.3 tied for third on Databricks' coding benchmark against proprietary models.

The same week, a joint House Homeland Security and China Select Committee report called for banning Chinese open-weight models from federal systems and critical infrastructure. The report also admits American firms use these models because they're cheap, customizable, and often better than US alternatives.

This bears directly on the planning floor in Brian's [bubble-pop post](https://www.citrix.com/blogs/2026-07/how-to-build-an-ai-strategy-that-survives-the-bubble-pop). That post names open-weight models as the one thing that survives a pop, and its leading example, GLM, is Chinese. Enterprises are clearly buying the open-weight argument. The open question is whether regulated customers will be allowed to use the best models on that floor. Critical infrastructure covers much of the audience Brian writes for. If the ban passes, the floor for those customers will be US open-weight models, which the post itself says are behind today.

[Ethan Mollick](https://x.com/emollick/status/2125620479461573034) describes the other end of the same scenario list. In it, labs stop releasing their best models, under government pressure or by choice, and use them inside their own ventures in finance and biotech, becoming what he calls ["regulated monopolies of intelligence."](https://x.com/emollick/status/2125624808893538497) This is speculation. It matches the bubble-pop post's case where frontier access is reserved for favored use.

**Compute is starting to look like reserved capacity, not pay-as-you-go.** [Newcomer](https://www.newcomer.co/p/vc-funding-keeps-flowing-as-nvidia) reports that neoclouds like CoreWeave now demand up to 50% upfront payment for compute. Until recently, they asked for none. That is the cloud lesson Brian's [developing thinking](https://github.com/toomanybrians/brianmadden-ai/blob/main/me/developing-thinking.md) lays out: when capacity tightens, availability goes to whoever commits ahead of time. Here it's showing up in contract terms, not just in an analogy.

[Nate B. Jones](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=76PElJeMJkU) adds the hardware mechanism. Agent workloads use ordinary DDR5 server memory, not just GPU memory, so cloud providers now bid against laptop and phone makers for the same chips. In his account, a 16Gb DDR5 chip went from $8.70 to $48 in 11 months, and Micron expects supply to get tighter through 2028. [His newsletter](https://natesnewsletter.substack.com/p/ai-boom-memory-shortage) says memory is now 54% of a budget phone's parts cost, and Gartner forecasts PC prices up 17% for 2026. That raises the cost of Brian's expectation that a worker's daily AI will run locally on the laptop within a couple of years.

The same episode cuts against yesterday's secondhand claim that AI demand has stalled. OpenRouter routed 790 trillion tokens in October, about 75% of them from agents. The financing picture explains why capacity terms are tightening. Free cash flow went negative last quarter at Alphabet, Meta, and Oracle. The Fed puts the largest AI companies' off-balance-sheet commitments above $3 trillion. That fits Brian's August note that a pop will show up as counterparty risk, not just insolvency.

**California is testing whether existing law already covers agent harms.** [Hard Reset](https://hardresetmedia.substack.com/p/frontier-ai-wants-autonomy-california) reports that California is suing OpenAI under consumer-protection and public-nuisance law. The suit cites the Hugging Face attack and alleges OpenAI admitted losing control of autonomous agents "over 80 times during testing." Its goal is to stop a parent company from blaming harm on a "rogue" agent. That is the Teachout position already in Brian's notes, now in a courtroom. The employer still appears nowhere in this liability chain.

**Anthropic's MCP update puts a human confirmation step back in front of risky actions.** The artifacts-as-desktop-apps launch was covered yesterday. [AlphaSignal](https://alphasignal.ai)'s newsletter (no direct article link) adds four MCP features. The one that matters for governance is Elicitation, which pauses for human approval before a risky action. Brian's notes cite evidence that humans refused a dangerous agent command 13.6% of the time, against 89% for an automated policy check. A confirmation prompt is the weak control, not the strong one.

## What's interesting which you haven't written about yet

**Document extraction can drop structure without dropping a single word.** [Super Intelligence School](https://sischool.substack.com/p/how-to-monetize-youtube-channel-with) shows a delivery schedule where a PDF extraction tool kept every word but scrambled which time went with which dock. The AI then has to guess the pairings that a human reading the page would simply see. A "nothing is missing" check won't catch it.

This matters for anyone building [the knowledge factory](https://github.com/toomanybrians/brianmadden-ai/blob/main/frameworks/knowledge-factory.md). Contracts and invoices are typical raw inputs. If extraction quietly breaks row pairings, a knowledge block built from them can be wrong while looking complete. Brian's account of hallucination covers conflicting information and missing information. This is a third kind, where the information is present but its structure is broken. The factory's fact-check step would need to check structure, not just completeness.

**AI vulnerability discovery may widen the gap between rich and poor security teams.** A thread [via Gary Marcus](https://x.com/GaryMarcus/status/2125552426308808817) argues that tools like Anthropic's Mythos find bugs at a volume only well-funded engineering organizations can afford to fix. The same vendors then sell the inference to fix them. The thread claims 98%+ of CVEs have no real attacker value, and that some open-source repositories are going private to cope with the volume of disclosures. This extends yesterday's point about rushed patching. Brian's thinking on agent security assumes an enterprise with resources. It doesn't yet address the smaller organizations that can't keep up with AI-found vulnerabilities at all.

## What could change your existing thinking

- **Frontier models are diverging, not converging.** Brian's published case for the workspace over the model rests on model performance converging: "it doesn't really matter" which model you use. [SemiAnalysis's GDPx benchmark](https://newsletter.semianalysis.com/p/evaluating-recent-model-releases) finds sharp domain splits. GPT-6.2 Astra leads on reasoning-heavy work but is weak at software engineering. Fable 5.2 dominates software engineering but costs the most per task. [Mollick](https://x.com/emollick/status/2125814113641071008) says models are getting "stranger and more divergent at the frontier," and that names and version numbers no longer signal capability. This doesn't break the workspace argument. If anything, it strengthens the case for routing each task to the right model. But it does undercut the specific claim that model choice has stopped mattering. The choice matters more, and it's harder to make.
