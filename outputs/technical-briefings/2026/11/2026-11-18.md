# Daily Brief—2026-11-18

I read 73 posts today—here's what's relevant to your work:

## What's relevant to you

**The FBI now treats unexpected calls to LLM APIs as a sign of intrusion.** An FBI FLASH advisory on ARTEX, the open-source agentic pentest framework covered yesterday, adds specific LLM API endpoints to its table of indicators. Daniel Miessler [points out](https://x.com/DanielMiessler/status/2122808869844111522) what that means in practice. A security team should now be hunting for outbound traffic to AI APIs it didn't expect.

That gives a law-enforcement source to the access step in Brian's [You can't transform the AI you can't see](https://www.citrix.com/blogs/2026-09/you-cant-transform-the-ai-you-cant-see). The post said to route enterprise AI traffic through a gateway so you can see who is using what. A company that can't tell its own sanctioned AI traffic apart from an attacker's will struggle to act on this advisory.

Three other details from the same advisory matter for planning:
- Most attacker-run ARTEX instances [use DeepSeek Flash](https://x.com/DanielMiessler/status/2122808814361772154). It works well enough, costs little, and doesn't object when told the job is a pentest.
- The attacks do best [on systems nobody watches](https://x.com/DanielMiessler/status/2122808833395626413): partner and supplier portals, mobile backends, legacy services, and third-party integrations.
- A database of 286 AI-assisted attacks [shows a sharp change](https://x.com/DanielMiessler/status/2122808955483418891). In none of the 27 incidents from 2024 did AI do the attack work itself. In 2026 it did in 78 of 174.

One correction tempers the scariest number. The FBI [reissued the advisory](https://x.com/DanielMiessler/status/2122808786415132851) and changed "enabled access to more than 2,250 downstream tenants" to "resulted in attempted sign-ins."

**Microsoft's Ignite pitch is the knowledge factory sold as a platform.** Microsoft's [Ignite overview](https://azure.microsoft.com/en-us/blog/microsoft-ignite-2026-whats-new-in-azure-the-platform-purpose-built-for-ai/) starts from the premise that frontier models are commodities and enterprise context is what sets a company apart. Its answer is Microsoft IQ, which captures context once and reuses it across every agent. Domain experts write "business plugins" that encode a piece of business logic once, and every agent inherits it. Foundry's [new skill optimization](https://azure.microsoft.com/en-us/blog/continuous-improvement-comes-to-microsoft-foundry/) turns repeated agent mistakes into versioned skills.

That is close to the canonical context layer in Brian's [knowledge factory](https://github.com/toomanybrians/brianmadden-ai/blob/main/frameworks/knowledge-factory.md). Microsoft hasn't said whether the result is plain files a customer can read and take elsewhere, or a store the customer can only reach through its platform.

The Oracle tie-in uses the same line Brian used about systems of record. [Microsoft and Oracle's announcement](https://azure.microsoft.com/en-us/blog/microsoft-and-oracle-bring-agentic-ai-into-flow-of-enterprise-work/) says "AI comes to the data rather than the data being copied out to AI." That is the [SaaSpocalypse post](https://www.citrix.com/blogs/2026-04/the-saaspocalypse-wont-touch-the-enterprise-software-moat) argument in vendor form.

Salesforce also announced [Copilotforce](https://news.microsoft.com/source/2026/11/17/microsoft-and-salesforce-announce-copilotforce-bringing-trusted-customer-context-and-action-into-microsoft-ai-experiences/), which puts its CRM data and workflows inside Copilot. It already has a similar Claude integration. That fits the October read that Salesforce is hedging across AI providers rather than picking one.

[Informatica's announcement](https://www.salesforce.com/news/stories/informatica-trusted-enterprise-data-microsoft-collaboration/) cites a Deloitte figure that sizes the readiness gap. Of organizations planning agent deployments, 85% expect to customize agents and 21% have mature agent governance.

**Production AI work keeps moving to open-weight and smaller models, even though open models aren't closing the gap at the frontier.** Yesterday's brief noted that no open-weight model has caught up with the current frontier. Usage is moving down-market anyway:
- At coding-agent company Factory, open models went from almost nothing at the start of 2026 to more than 10% of tokens, and over half for some customers ([Newcomer's CVAI recap](https://www.newcomer.co/p/13-videos-from-cvai-igor-babuschkin)).
- Nubank replaced GPT-5.2 with an open-weight Qwen model on its busiest support agent. Self-service rose 8.8 points and latency fell 25% ([Linas Beliūnas](https://linas.substack.com/p/nubank-ai-agents-simulated-customers)).
- Alaska Airlines fine-tuned a small model that beat GPT-6 Astra on classifying maintenance reports ([Foundry post](https://azure.microsoft.com/en-us/blog/continuous-improvement-comes-to-microsoft-foundry/)).

That is the pattern Brian's [bubble-pop post](https://www.citrix.com/blogs/2026-07/how-to-build-an-ai-strategy-that-survives-the-bubble-pop) predicted. The workhorse tier is enough for most enterprise work, so frontier progress matters less to the planning floor.

Nubank's real constraint was testing speed. It screened 29 configurations through 16,000 simulated customer conversations before running any live A/B test. One caveat is worth noting: a simpler simulator built on a frontier model ranked the best-performing agent version last.

AWS's guidance to [use code for rules and models for judgment](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/machine-learning/cut-generative-ai-costs-code-for-rules-models-for-judgment/) repeats yesterday's context-management theme.

**The skill of managing people predicts the skill of managing agents.** David Deming's team measured how well individual managers led human teams and then how well the same people led AI-agent teams on the same task. The correlation was 0.81 ([HBR IdeaCast](https://hbr.org/podcast/2026/11/who-will-make-a-great-leader-theres-a-test-for-that)).

That is experimental evidence for Brian's December argument that [workers want to delegate rather than build automations](https://www.citrix.com/blogs/2025-12/workers-dont-want-to-build-automations-they-want-to-delegate). It also suggests that the human-plus-agents worker in the [2031 worker-shape forecast](https://github.com/toomanybrians/brianmadden-ai/blob/main/frameworks/2031-worker-shape.md) needs existing management skill, not a new one. Companies that already assess management skill could use the same tools to find good agent orchestrators.

SaaStr gives a cautionary data point ([via The Artificial Intelligence Show](https://podcast.smarterx.ai/shownotes/252)). Three people and about 20 agents run roughly $20 million in revenue. The founder still spent one 13-hour day just reviewing and fixing agent output.

Separately, Anthropic now argues that [one agent often beats a sprawl of specialized subagents](https://x.com/emollick/status/2122664990624641421). That adds a third source to yesterday's two findings against splitting work across many agents.

**Agent sandboxes are filling in at the infrastructure layer.** Azure's [Agent Sandbox and Agent Grid](https://azure.microsoft.com/en-us/blog/introducing-azure-catalyst-agentic-modernization-and-new-ai-infrastructure-innovations/) give agent-generated code VM-level isolation and default-deny networking. They run on AKS, which Microsoft says customers can also run on their own servers. Simon Willison describes a [separate "external sandbox" pattern](https://simonw.substack.com/p/running-the-full-server-side-datasette). Code runs outside the agent, behind a proxy that only allows approved destinations and swaps in credentials.

Both support Brian's September 30 point in his [developing thinking](https://github.com/toomanybrians/brianmadden-ai/blob/main/me/developing-thinking.md) that enterprises need agents running in environments they control. The open question is whether "runs on your own AKS" counts as an environment the customer controls.

## What's interesting which you haven't written about yet

**The rush to patch AI-found vulnerabilities may itself be causing outages.** Ethan Mollick [relays reports](https://x.com/emollick/status/2122869773683831129) that Wall Street executives and the NSA told Congress the same thing about this month's run of outages. They blamed a rush to patch vulnerabilities that AI models had found, not AI attacks. Mollick calls this both the official reason and the most plausible one. CIOs described exhaustion from the volume of patches.

Attackers are now using patches against the companies that ship them. Google's threat-intelligence team found a hacker-run framework that [reads the code changes in security patches](https://x.com/DanielMiessler/status/2122706949389398022) to work out the underlying flaw. It then builds exploits before organizations finish patching.

Together these describe a new operational risk. AI shortens the time a company has to patch, and rushing the change process breaks production. Brian's October note says these systems keep finding holes even when security is done properly. This is the operational side of that point, and he has no position on it yet.

One small, odd detail: the FBI asks responders to report ["multiple sub-agents overwriting each other"](https://x.com/DanielMiessler/status/2122808888051556656). A routine bug in multi-agent tools has become a forensic signature of an attack.

## What could change your existing thinking

- **A company that sells models is going after the neutral governance seat.** Brian argues that the referee role between the estate and AI models can't be played by anyone who also sells a model. [Microsoft IQ's business plugins](https://azure.microsoft.com/en-us/blog/microsoft-ignite-2026-whats-new-in-azure-the-platform-purpose-built-for-ai/) are designed to work inside Claude and ChatGPT over MCP and to carry Microsoft's permissions and governance along with them. Entra Agent ID and Agent 365 add agent discovery and auditing. Microsoft claims about 60,000 customers already use Microsoft IQ. If enterprises accept that, the cross-model context and governance layer goes to a model vendor, and "structurally can't" becomes a question of whether customers care.

- **VC money is betting the personal context layer is a standalone, portable product.** Brian's August 28 view is that inside a knowledge factory, the individual's second brain shrinks to a personal sandbox working against corporate canon. a16z is backing First Person Labs to build ["Second,"](https://www.a16z.news/p/investing-in-first-person-labs) a personal model of the user. It runs on the device by default, the user can inspect it and take it to other tools, and the user decides which agents can read it. Their framing is that "whoever controls that model will have considerable influence over what your agents do." If personal context becomes a product workers carry from job to job, the bring-your-own-agent portability question comes back from the worker's side, just as employers like Microsoft build the company-owned version.
