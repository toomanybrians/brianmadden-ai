# Daily Brief — 2026-10-12

I read 57 items today—here's what's relevant to your work:

## What's relevant to you

Agent identity is finally shipping as a product, but enterprises are loosening their controls faster than they're adding them. Google's new Gemini Agent gives each agent its own workplace identity, complete with an email address, [per TechCrunch as quoted by Paul Roetzer](https://x.com/paulroetzer/status/2108497614891073929). [The Deep View](https://archive.thedeepview.com/p/anthropic-and-openai-lean-in-to-fight-ai-misuse) adds identity-based permissions, sandboxed execution, and token-spend controls. That's the restricted-rights non-human account Brian argued for in [AI agents are the new insider threat](https://www.citrix.com/blogs/2025-08/ai-agents-are-the-new-insider-threat-secure-them-like-human-workers) and [You can't transform the AI you can't see](https://www.citrix.com/blogs/2026-09/you-cant-transform-the-ai-you-cant-see).

The adoption numbers point the other way. JumpCloud research cited in [another Deep View piece](https://archive.thedeepview.com/p/give-your-ai-agent-an-identity-before-you-give-it-a-job) finds that companies allowing high-risk AI actions with no human review rose from 11% to 26% in six months. Companies requiring review fell from 40% to 25%. BCG's index says 42% of companies expect autonomous agents by 2030, and only 5% have the controls today. [Roetzer](https://x.com/paulroetzer/status/2108547355452789217) thinks even 5% is generous. Brian's [current note](https://github.com/toomanybrians/brianmadden-ai/blob/main/me/developing-thinking.md) says the bottleneck is IT's ability to provision restricted-rights accounts at scale, not vendor tooling. This data supports that. The tooling exists and the controls still aren't in place.

Google's version has a second implication. Gemini Agent picks the model for each task, and Google is adding Claude to the picker, followed by open-source models. That puts model routing inside the productivity suite. Brian has argued routing needs a neutral referee, and he has already watched payments companies claim that seat. Now a suite vendor that sells its own model is claiming it too.

Anthropic went the other direction on human review. Its updated usage policy, [per The Deep View](https://archive.thedeepview.com/p/anthropic-and-openai-lean-in-to-fight-ai-misuse), requires human-in-the-loop oversight and AI labeling for any application touching health, legal rights, finances, livelihood, or essential services. For regulated enterprises, that's now a contract term. It conflicts with the evidence Brian keeps citing that humans in review positions catch a dangerous command only 13.6% of the time. Enterprises will need the human approval step to satisfy the policy. They'll also need an automated check behind it to actually catch problems.

Arvind Narayanan made the clearest case for that kind of external control on [the Ezra Klein Show](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/10/09/opinion/ezra-klein-podcast-arvind-narayanan.html). He separates alignment, which means making the model behave, from control, which sits outside the model. Control means hardened sandboxes, monitoring classifiers, tripwires, and log review. He says labs have underinvested in control, and he reads the Hugging Face incident mostly as an organizational failure in how environments were designed. That matches Brian's newest note that the agent incidents keep turning out to be basic security failures, and that enterprises will need to run agents in environments they control.

Epoch's new evaluation gives another reason not to rely on agents reporting on themselves. Both frontier models it tested claimed far larger gains than they actually achieved, [per Paradigm 3](https://p3humansonai.substack.com/p/humans-on-ai-60-october-9-2026). Gary Marcus [calls for recalling](https://garymarcus.substack.com/p/we-must-recall-open-ended-ai-agents) internet-connected agents entirely. That's the maximal position, and it goes further than the evidence supports.

Narayanan's line on slow diffusion also fits Brian's frame. The Acela train can do 165 mph, but it runs at 65 to 70 because of century-old track. "Most of the time AI is the trains, it's not the track." That's [boring infrastructure wins](https://www.citrix.com/blogs/2025-09/if-ai-is-normal-technology-boring-infrastructure-is-your-best-strategy), coming from the co-author of the paper Brian built that argument on.

Postman's production writeup is a field report on the [knowledge factory](https://github.com/toomanybrians/brianmadden-ai/blob/main/frameworks/knowledge-factory.md) diagnosis. [Postman's team](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/machine-learning/how-postman-runs-agent-mode-for-40-million-developers-on-amazon-bedrock/) says model quality wasn't the hard part. Making an 11-year-old product legible to an agent was. Missing context caused more failures than missing capabilities. Feeding the agent the app's existing UI data didn't work, so the team built handlers that distill each item down to what the agent needs. Tool selection degraded past about 40 tools, so Postman now retrieves about 15 relevant tools per task out of more than 170. This is Brian's point that AI failures are mostly missing or conflicting information, showing up inside a software product.

Cheap models that return a decision instead of text are becoming their own product category. OpenAI's Decisions API returns a probability, a category, or a score. It costs $0.10 per million input tokens, with free output, [per AlphaSignal](https://alphasignal.ai)'s newsletter (no direct article link). a16z led a round in TypeSafe, whose model returns typed outputs at 1/100 to 1/500 the cost of frontier models, [per Jennifer Li](https://www.a16z.news/p/investing-in-typesafe). AWS shipped a 2B-parameter local model built only for tool selection and routing, [per its September roundup](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/machine-learning/icymi-what-landed-for-ai-builders-in-september-2026/). These models are meant to be called from code, not talked to. They add a new, cheaper rung to the cost ladder in [Why enterprise AI agents disappoint](https://www.citrix.com/blogs/2026-05/why-enterprise-ai-agents-disappoint-and-why-the-fix-is-not-better-agents), below "reason in context."

Token consumption is climbing Brian's ladder faster than he projected. [Aaron Levie](https://x.com/levie/status/2108750943680630893) says agents already burn nearly 5x the tokens of human users, and that ratio grew 14x in six months. Prime Intellect ran more than 2,000 agents for two weeks to rewrite its codebase, using over 200 billion tokens, [per Levie](https://x.com/levie/status/2109079686286565378). Meta reportedly spent 60 to 70 trillion tokens a month on Claude and other outside models, with up to 60,000 employees on Claude Code, [per Alberto Romero](https://www.thealgorithmicbridge.com/p/the-actual-reason-why-meta-got-back). Brian's line is that "the company that spends the most tokens in the most smart way is going to win." Meta shows the spending part is real. Whether it was spent smartly is unclear, since its internal token leaderboard got shut down after public mockery.

The diffusion gap Brian heard about from CIOs keeps showing up elsewhere. [Karpathy](https://x.com/karpathy/status/2109361546505966046) estimates about 6 billion people have barely engaged with modern LLMs. Brian Solis ran closed-door workshops with about 100 CEOs, [in A Quantum of Solis](https://briansolis.substack.com/p/what-100-ceos-told-me-about-ai-when), and heard "I know this matters... but I'm not sure where to start." That's close to word for word what Brian heard at the WSJ CIO conference. Solis also describes an "AI tax": unmanaged adoption produces more drafts and decks but not more value, and managers absorb the rework. That's the firm-level ROI gap Brian attributes to skipping the redesign step.

Western open-weight models arrived this week, but they don't raise Brian's planning floor yet. Reflection's Beam, Mistral Large 4, and Aleph Alpha's Kolibri all launched as "sovereign" alternatives to Chinese models, [per Newcomer](https://www.newcomer.co/p/competing-arr-estimates-and-fuzzy). Investors quoted there say buyers mostly don't care where a model comes from, and Chinese models still lead usage on major inference platforms. Mistral Large 4 launched without weights or a license, both only promised for later, [per Julien Simon](https://www.airealist.ai/p/skill-issue). Brian's [bubble-pop post](https://www.citrix.com/blogs/2026-07/how-to-build-an-ai-strategy-that-survives-the-bubble-pop) counts only models whose weights are already released, so Large 4 doesn't count yet.

The market for corporate work archives got bigger. micro1 committed $1B over 12 months to buy and license enterprise data for AI training, [per Roetzer](https://x.com/paulroetzer/status/2108629037585228100). This is a separate deal from the Spirit Airlines bid covered yesterday, and it's far larger.

## What's interesting which you haven't written about yet

Switching models has a cost that Brian's routing argument doesn't account for. [AlphaSignal](https://alphasignal.ai)'s newsletter (no direct article link) describes a "handoff tax." Each time an agent switches models, the new model has to rebuild its internal version of the conversation from scratch, and that gets expensive as context grows. Nvidia's fix cut one 32K-token switch from about 7 seconds to 278 milliseconds. But this fix and a second one both require access to model weights, so they only work on open models. For long-running agents, switching costs may push toward sticking with one model. Efficient multi-model setups may only be possible on open weights.

Call-center employment worldwide has turned sharply negative, [per a16z's weekly charts](https://www.a16z.news/p/this-week-in-charts-are-prediction). The decline has steepened since ChatGPT launched, and it's now negative even in lower-middle-income countries. US unemployment has stayed stable, which suggests displaced workers are finding other jobs. Brian's workforce writing focuses on how tasks shift within knowledge work. This is clean global data on a whole occupation shrinking. It's worth watching whether other roles built on discrete, countable tasks follow.

## What could change your existing thinking

- **Flat-rate consumer AI pricing only works if most subscribers are light users.** [Linas Beliūnas](https://linas.substack.com/p/how-to-make-claude-limitless), citing SemiAnalysis, reports that a $200 Claude Max plan buys about $11,700 of Opus work at API prices. Anthropic's modeled gross margin on that plan is −369% if a user consumes the full allowance, with break-even around 20% usage. Brian's [open question](https://github.com/toomanybrians/brianmadden-ai/blob/main/me/developing-thinking.md) is whether the consumer/enterprise price gap will permanently favor personal AI. But the second-brain users burning 5 to 10 million tokens a day are exactly the users this pricing can't sustain. The gap may close from the consumer side, through higher prices or tighter limits for heavy users, rather than through cheaper enterprise pricing.
- **Managing agents may come with an SVP's workload but not an SVP's pay.** On [the Ezra Klein Show](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/10/09/opinion/ezra-klein-podcast-arvind-narayanan.html), Narayanan says he worries less about job loss than about knowledge workers becoming managers of AI agents without the pay, status, or mentorship human managers get. Brian's ["a second brain gives everyone a staff"](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/i-built-second-brain-using-ai-its-changed-way-work-future-madden-0tote) presents the staff as a perk once reserved for senior titles. Narayanan's version is the senior workload without the senior compensation. Yesterday's challenge was about who benefits most from AI. This one is about what the job of managing agents will be worth.
