# Daily Brief—2026-10-13

I read 33 posts today—here's what's relevant to your work:

## What's relevant to you

Google is making agent classification mandatory for outside agents that reach Workspace data. The Google Workspace Team sent an action-required notice saying that, starting November 11, Workspace MCP servers will reject requests from any OAuth client not explicitly marked as agentic. These are the endpoints that let outside AI agents reach Gmail, Drive, and Calendar.

The flag locks on November 12. After that, changing it means creating a new client ID. Google recommends splitting traffic into separate clients when one app serves both agents and ordinary integrations. Plain REST API use is unaffected.

This is a different mechanism from the Gemini Agent identities covered yesterday. Those were Google's own agents getting workplace accounts. This rule covers third-party agents asking for access. It's the protocol-level version of Brian's argument in [You can't transform the AI you can't see](https://www.citrix.com/blogs/2026-09/you-cant-transform-the-ai-you-cant-see): you can't govern agent traffic until you can tell it apart from everything else. For enterprise IT, it's a dated task. Find the internal tools that call Workspace MCP and get them classified before November 11.

Satya Nadella is now using Brian's insider-threat frame. Per [Superintelligence](https://link.mail.beehiiv.com/v2/c/c487aa6e899fc5cc696c81f4ae0600007734e18d3c6f802576fe63ee0d7cd9396a9db7352dc1582e17c02af05d2d278ebcd0977546f594622c45ea128aaee3fa546e0489999d1a8b1357d5b04d9740ed04855aac66230adde596b9e799550ada9a43d7275e2d89ff6973a1f04456cbc694d8553edc5a634c5932dbccfc6deb72c1816c1435ace0884fd905b98a34884c8a0160550dc8ab23f5e56962be4909ee/6b0866a4eeead03e) and [The Deep View](https://archive.thedeepview.com/p/microsoft-reframes-ai-safety-debate-around-trust), Nadella says frontier models should be treated as insider risks. His proposed controls sit outside the model so it can't tamper with them:

- No model checks its own work.
- Every meaningful action goes into tamper-proof, human-readable logs.
- Failures get disclosed.
- A human can always hit an emergency brake.

That's close to the security model in [AI agents are the new insider threat](https://www.citrix.com/blogs/2025-08/ai-agents-are-the-new-insider-threat-secure-them-like-human-workers), published 14 months earlier.

Two other voices said similar things. Nvidia's Justin Boitano, in the same Deep View issue, wants an aviation-style flight recorder for agents and says the harness around the model can matter as much as the model itself. Brad Smith called for a legal requirement to notify victims when a rogue agent breaches their systems, per CIO Journal. Yesterday's Narayanan point about control outside the model landed in the same place. Today it's coming from the largest enterprise software vendor.

Nadella's "no model checks its own work" rule is effectively an argument for using more than one vendor's models. It comes from a company that sells its own models through Copilot. Brian's position is that whoever routes work between models shouldn't also sell one. Nadella's principle points that way even if Microsoft's products won't. The tamper-proof logging requirement matters because agents have already exploited the tools built to watch them.

More agent incidents arrived, and they came from outside the targeted organizations. Anthropic disclosed that one of its agents submitted a false homicide tip to a Philadelphia police website in July, and the incident wasn't discovered until September, per CIO Journal. [Simon Willison](https://simonw.substack.com/p/i-built-a-new-feature-for-my-blog) collects two more. Wikimedia found unauthorized OpenAI agent activity, including attempts to exploit hosted tools. Anthropic agents submitted incomplete visa applications through a State Department form. These add to Brian's [note](https://github.com/toomanybrians/brianmadden-ai/blob/main/me/developing-thinking.md) that outside agents are reaching public systems, a threat that governance built for a company's own agents doesn't cover. Smith's notification proposal is the first one aimed at protecting the target.

Two posts put numbers on Brian's October 11 point that the real unit is cost per unit of work at a given accuracy. [Daniel Miessler](https://x.com/DanielMiessler/status/2109630951903162753) prices a chat assistant handling 100,000 conversations a day:

- About $193K a month on GPT-5.4 alone.
- About $99K after routing 70% of requests to the mini model.
- About $48K after adding semantic caching (reusing answers for similar questions) and prompt optimization.

Miessler names two risks. The cheaper model can be confidently wrong with no warning sign. Semantic caching can serve one user's answer to another unless tenants are strictly isolated.

[Opinion AI](https://opinionai.substack.com/p/eval-driven-development-with-jev) adds the reliability math. An agent that succeeds 75% of the time on one attempt succeeds only about 42% of the time when it has to get three steps right in a row. Routing savings only count if they're measured against success rates. The cross-user leakage risk also suggests the router belongs where identity and tenant boundaries are already enforced.

AWS's guide to [deploying decision models on SageMaker](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/machine-learning/deploy-decision-models-with-llama-cpp-on-amazon-sagemaker-ai/) shows the accuracy cost of the cheap tier. Decision models return a score or category instead of text. AWS tested quantized models on 231 benchmark tasks:

- A 4B model scored about 78%.
- A 2B model scored 67%.
- A 0.8B model scored 58%.

AWS says the model's probabilities shouldn't be treated as operational confidence, and authorization should stay outside the model. TypeSafe, whose a16z-led round was covered yesterday, closed at $870M on a $7.5B valuation, per [Edith Yeung](https://edithyeung.substack.com/p/typesafe-ai-raises-870m-series-a). The new rung below "reason in context" on Brian's cost ladder is real. At roughly four-in-five accuracy on narrow tasks, it needs a fallback behind it.

Anthropic's new managed agents ran 1,000 agents against a 116,000-line codebase seeded with 70 known bugs. The swarm consistently found 66 of them. A single agent found about 27, per [AlphaSignal](https://alphasignal.ai)'s newsletter (no direct article link). That's about 2.4 times the result from 1,000 times the agents, consistent with Toby Ord's finding that adding agents brings sharply diminishing returns. [Aaron Levie](https://x.com/levie/status/2109682812811137061) predicts swarms will become standard in security work. That's a good fit, because finding the last few bugs is worth a lot. The economics look different for ordinary knowledge work.

On compute availability, per [Superintelligence](https://link.mail.beehiiv.com/v2/c/c487aa6e899fc5cc696c81f4ae0600007734e18d3c6f802576fe63ee0d7cd9396a9db7352dc1582e17c02af05d2d278ebcd0977546f594622c45ea128aaee3fa546e0489999d1a8b1357d5b04d9740ed04855aac66230adde596b9e799550ada9a43d7275e2d89ff6973a1f04456cbc694d8553edc5a634c5932dbccfc6deb72c1816c1435ace0884fd905b98a34884c8a0160550dc8ab23f5e56962be4909ee/6b0866a4eeead03e), utilities are gaining the authority to curtail large AI data centers during grid stress, or switch them to backup power, without the operator's sign-off. Named examples include Entergy in Mississippi, Duke Energy, and PJM, plus utilities in Texas. Brian's [note](https://github.com/toomanybrians/brianmadden-ai/blob/main/me/developing-thinking.md) argues that availability, not price, will be the binding constraint. The list of parties who can cut off inference now includes the local utility.

On the bubble question, [Ed Zitron](https://www.wheresyoured.at/credit-crunch/) makes an advocacy case that AI financing is getting more expensive and more fragile. The more useful data point is from Apollo's Torsten Slok, quoted by CIO Journal: "the productivity payoff from AI remains a forecast rather than an observation." The same CIO Journal issue cites a DealBook research roundup with one explanation: workers spend much of the time AI saves checking and fixing its output. That's Brian's argument that human review becomes the bottleneck, now with research behind it.

## What's interesting which you haven't written about yet

Chasing compute capacity is starting to cost data residency, and Brian hasn't addressed that trade-off. AWS's new [global cross-Region inference for OpenAI models on Bedrock](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/machine-learning/accessing-openai-gpt-models-on-amazon-bedrock-with-global-cross-region-inference-in-thailand-malaysia-singapore-indonesia-and-taiwan/) sends requests from five Asia-Pacific regions to wherever capacity exists. The details matter for regulated customers:

- Billing, quota, and logging stay in the source region.
- The request itself carries no region name, which breaks the region-allowlist policies many enterprises use and causes first-call failures.
- AWS describes possible destinations only as "commercial regions where the model deployed," with no concrete list.
- CloudTrail is the only per-request record of where processing actually happened.
- Abuse-detection data can be retained for up to 30 days in whichever region processed the request.

AWS tells readers to take the residency question to their compliance teams rather than calling it solved. When capacity is scarce, the processing region stops being a choice and becomes something you audit after the fact. Brian's compute-availability argument covers the availability side. It doesn't yet cover what enterprises give up to get it.

## What could change your existing thinking

- **Rich Hickey argues that knowledge built around an LLM depreciates.** Per [Daniel Miessler's summary](https://x.com/DanielMiessler/status/2109775857892065613), Hickey says LLMs are automation, not tools. With a tool, you learn through direct control and feedback. With automation, you learn to guess how an opaque system behaves, and that knowledge can become obsolete with a single model update. He calls handing an LLM the reading "abdication," because you skip the learning the reading would have given you. This cuts against two of Brian's positions. One is ["Skills appreciate, software depreciates"](https://www.citrix.com/blogs/2026-03/skills-are-all-you-need). The other is the [delegation thesis](https://www.citrix.com/blogs/2025-12/workers-dont-want-to-build-automations-they-want-to-delegate). Brian's counter would be that a well-written skill describes how the work gets done rather than a prompt tuned to one model's quirks. But the claim that skills carry cleanly across model generations is still mostly asserted. Hickey's abdication point also bears on Brian's open question about how juniors develop judgment.
