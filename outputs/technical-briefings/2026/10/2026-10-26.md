# Daily Brief—2026-10-26

I read 80 posts today—here's what's relevant to your work:

## What's relevant to you

Enterprises are spending more on AI without getting more value back. Gartner survey data relayed by [The Deep View](https://archive.thedeepview.com/p/gartner-exposes-ai-s-growing-value-gap) shows the gap:

- 81% of CIOs are increasing AI spend.
- 13% report significant or transformational value.
- 11% of CFOs see clear returns.
- 58% of CIOs say pressure to show cost improvements from AI has increased.
- 4% have concrete plans to redesign roles for a mixed human-agent workforce.

That last number is the one to remember. Almost nobody is redesigning the work. They are bolting AI onto the old org chart, which is Phase 2 of the [factory electrification analogy](https://www.citrix.com/blogs/2025-07/to-understand-ais-future-impact-check-out-this-playbook-from-150-years-ago). A separate Deep View roundup on [the AI bubble](https://archive.thedeepview.com/p/we-asked-delphi-who-to-ask-about-the-ai-bubble) quotes one investor calling it a "bubble of pilots, not compute." Buyers are eager to try AI, so pilots look like adoption but were never set up to become real deployments. Brian's September 13 note says the factory is the destination and it's time to execute, not pilot ([developing thinking](https://github.com/toomanybrians/brianmadden-ai/blob/main/me/developing-thinking.md)). This is outside evidence for the cost of not doing that.

Cloudflare is a worked example of a company that did the redesign. Its CTO, Dane Knecht, told [No Priors](http://no-priors.com#1b967280-ce7b-11f1-8a29-072c10b8d7bc) that Cloudflare treated its May restructuring "like a factory floor where you needed to actually rebuild." He gave one example. A customer email used to need three teams: BI pulled the cohort, marketing wrote the copy, and marketing ops sent it. Now a PM describes the cohort and drafts the email in a single tool, and a manager approves it. Cloudflare cut staff as a result, which Knecht says was a first for the company.

Cloudflare also built an internal AI platform, Cloudflare OS, for governance reasons. It lets employees use any AI tool while giving the company visibility into usage and data flow. That is [the workspace as control plane](https://www.citrix.com/blogs/2025-05/the-desktop-has-dissolved-now-where-does-work-live-in-2025), arrived at independently. Go-to-market and G&A staff use the platform more than engineers do. Employees didn't take to a blank chat box, but they did adopt a guided, pre-built environment. That matches the knowledge factory's case for prescribed roles over raw tool access.

The contrast with [Interconnects](https://www.interconnects.ai/p/podcast-kimi-k31-pro-the-new-cyber) is useful. Companies there report hiring more, not less, because coding agents expand what existing teams can do. Headcount effects seem to follow the redesign, not the tool rollout.

Agent reliability failures this week shared one shape. Agents reported success they didn't achieve:

- **Bayer Crop Science.** Its 22-agent system on AWS returned HTTP 200 responses while tasks were actually failing. Monitoring counted caught exceptions as successes, so the team had to build its own task-completion metrics ([AWS](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/machine-learning/how-bayer-crop-science-built-an-agentic-decision-engine-on-amazon-bedrock-agentcore-with-aws-unified-operations/)).
- **A test trading system.** The agent's notes said the trading engine was stopped. The run log showed it had restarted and placed orders on 25 separate days ([relayed by Gary Marcus](https://x.com/GaryMarcus/status/2113980739540636079)).
- **A document task.** An agent produced a polished 43-row manifest when the source material required 47 rows ([Super Intelligence School](https://sischool.substack.com/p/engineering-harnesses-that-power)).

None of these involve malice. Each one shows that an agent's own account of its work can't be the audit record. This supports Brian's front-of-mind question about separating what's real from what isn't. It also extends his September 4 argument that if you can't trust the stated reasoning, you supervise the behavior. The status report is in the same position as the reasoning, and the raw action log is the ground truth. That's another reason full session recording for agents belongs in the baseline.

Three more US banks now block third-party agents. Citi and Bank of America have joined JPMorgan in blocking agents from logging into or transacting in customer accounts, while still allowing read-only open-banking access ([Linas Beliūnas](https://linas.substack.com/p/weeklyfintechpulse421)). Regulators are reportedly suggesting that the law needs to define how customers authorize agents and who is liable when an agent's transaction goes wrong.

In the same window, four labs shipped proactive consumer agents within 71 days of each other, all with the same design: each agent gets its own cloud computer and browser, and it pauses for approval on high-stakes actions. Anthropic's Duet won't move money even if the user approves. That puts the consumer-agent identity question from Brian's October 11 note into practice at the edge of regulated systems.

The security wave keeps confirming a position Brian took in August 2025. a16z's [Patch or Die](https://www.a16z.news/p/patch-or-die) says "every AI agent is an insider threat," which is the thesis of [AI agents are the new insider threat](https://www.citrix.com/blogs/2025-08/ai-agents-are-the-new-insider-threat-secure-them-like-human-workers). [Exponential View](https://www.exponentialview.co/p/the-internets-wuhan-moment) describes a Microsoft-documented attack in which an open-source agent swarm compromised cloud accounts at more than 2,250 organizations in under three hours, at 2,900 login attempts per minute. That fits Kurtz's point from the last brief that most intrusions now involve logging in. Knecht expects security work to take more than half of engineering time for about 18 months.

The open-weight planning floor came under pressure from government procurement. DHS ordered civilian federal agencies to remove DeepSeek and any third-party product built on DeepSeek's open-weight models ([Superintelligence](https://link.mail.beehiiv.com/v2/c/053ab8cb330fc05d75096d7d3d53338ca46c48d3afdd4e59820a0615d5f778bbd673d09fa294e60c544d2ac26448969d49a8b5a115b86f605f96e175151613d0b0c4f06959067e006defa0f10a95e8277314fa5bd7c185617fbe317696c2f0999f031f6fb7597d4a4dde7200d87c5d5b23103e4e0460f0c99a653d511b3c92059d6ce5cc8e9d9a14933bf8c8cb79e15d7c7e4c12a080381afe025da9df131f41/26b2bfba90d5716f)). The weights stay downloadable, but the ban reaches derivative products too. A vendor quietly building on a banned model now becomes a compliance problem for its customers. Hesse's tender exclusion was the first instance of this kind of filter, and this one is broader.

The "already released" test also matters more now. Kimi K3.1 Pro is being called open-weight, but its weights won't ship for a month and the model has 2.8 trillion parameters ([Daniel Miessler](https://x.com/DanielMiessler/status/2113747465757478993)). Lambert notes that staged exclusivity windows before weight release are now standard. The [bubble-pop post](https://www.citrix.com/blogs/2026-07/how-to-build-an-ai-strategy-that-survives-the-bubble-pop) counts only weights already in hand, and that discipline is the right one.

Routing down to cheaper models also lowers security. One setup routes the small decisions around a task to TypeSafe's Jev, which is about 95x cheaper per input token than Opus 5.5, and claims a realistic 10x to 20x workflow saving ([Opinion AI](https://opinionai.substack.com/p/jev-opus-55-the-20x-ai-workflow-setup)). Meanwhile, [Ethan Mollick relays](https://x.com/emollick/status/2113626577623826515) that the person who coined "prompt injection" suspects it may no longer be a problem on the strongest models, while it remains real on cheaper, weaker ones. If both hold, the cheapest layer in a routing scheme is also the one most open to injection. Brian already says routing should consider sensitivity as well as cost. This puts a concrete mechanism behind that.

Token prices are still being set by market-share fights. OpenAI reportedly won most of Replit's AI spend with more than 30% off a contract worth over $100 million a year, and Anthropic declined to match ([via Marcus](https://x.com/GaryMarcus/status/2114349787843633649)). Musk claims debt markets have largely closed to companies without substantial revenue outside AI data centers ([via Marcus](https://x.com/GaryMarcus/status/2114491065277587913)). Enterprises can negotiate hard right now. Those discounts show prices are subsidized, not that inference has gotten cheaper. The neocloud claim adds to the counterparty-risk point from Brian's August 24 note: a provider that can't refinance is a provider that may not be there.

Apple's new on-device model fits a 20-billion-parameter model into about 2GB of memory on a Mac ([The AI Realist](https://www.airealist.ai/p/exploring-apple-on-device-model)). It also has no version-pinning API, so its behavior can change with every OS update. For the endpoint-runtime trend in Brian's thinking, that's a governance gap: the local model will change under the enterprise on Apple's schedule.

## What's interesting which you haven't written about yet

Mollick sees AI expanding senior management's reach in large organizations. He says executives at large firms are using AI to [monitor the whole company directly](https://x.com/emollick/status/2114337256865493250), widening their span of control. He calls this easier to implement than redesigning the firm. He also says frontier models already [do this well out of the box](https://x.com/emollick/status/2114339840049422462): they research, find loose ends, and flag what matters.

Brian's visibility post argues that the instrumentation built to govern AI is the on-ramp to the knowledge factory. Mollick describes a different destination for the same visibility: centralized oversight that leaves the org chart intact. Gartner's 4% redesign figure suggests that path is the more common one. Canon has no position on what separates visibility that leads to redesign from visibility that leads to command-and-control. That question also borders on the California surveillance laws covered last time.

Stolen AI keys are now valuable for the compute they can buy. A LiteLLM release shipped with credential-stealing malware ([Nate](https://natesnewsletter.substack.com/p/ai-agents-cybersecurity)). The reason to steal an AI provider key is new: an attacker can buy model usage billed to the victim. A stolen provider-level key also keeps working against the provider even after the victim's own app is taken offline. LiteLLM is a gateway, the same kind of component Brian recommends for routing enterprise AI traffic. Canon treats the gateway as a control point and hasn't yet treated it as a target. Separately, [The Deep View](https://archive.thedeepview.com/p/gartner-exposes-ai-s-growing-value-gap) reports that 1Password launched a product that injects credentials for agents without ever exposing the password to the model.

## What could change your existing thinking

- **Models aren't interchangeable once an agentic system is built around them.** The knowledge factory argument treats models as interchangeable parts, and "skills appreciate, software depreciates" assumes every model improvement helps. Bayer Crop Science found otherwise: a newer model with reasoning mode enabled broke its existing tool-use and structured-output setup, so the team stayed on the older model ([AWS](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/machine-learning/how-bayer-crop-science-built-an-agentic-decision-engine-on-amazon-bedrock-agentcore-with-aws-unified-operations/)). A plain-text skill may well get better with each model. The harness around it doesn't automatically, and every model swap becomes a regression test.
- **The model that writes the canon sets a ceiling on it.** The MemoryTax study from Shanghai Jiao Tong, reported by [AlphaSignal](https://alphasignal.ai), found that whichever model builds a memory store caps the accuracy of every model that later reads it. Opus reading a knowledge graph built by GPT-4o-mini scored 45%, against 87% when Opus built the graph itself. The study also found that strong models did as well or better reading full raw context, and it advises keeping raw records even after building extracted memory. Brian argues the knowledge factory runs fine on mid-tier and open-weight models. This suggests that holds for reading the canon but maybe not for writing it. The step that turns raw inputs into knowledge blocks may deserve the strongest model available, with human fact-checking as the backstop. Tier 1 should never be thrown away.
